Symphonies of Smiles

"In came Mrs. Fezziwig, one vast substantial smile"
—Charles Dickens
"A Christmas Carol"

THANKSGIVING is the season for smiles. Someone called the late President William Howard Taft a "symphony of smiles." Perhaps that is why those who knew him loved him. Once, at a great singing festival, he said to your editor, "If we didn't have music and laughter, life just wouldn't amount to much." If all the rulers of the world, bar none, could echo President Taft right now, peace would be very near. Let's all make a genuine effort!

Music and smiles bear a curious analogy. Both are spontaneous, both are pervasive, both are irresistible, both are radiant, both are restorative, both are essentials to happiness and contentment.

During the past year many of the significant prophecies of hopeful dreamers, who have endeavored to find ways to help man alleviate his woes through developing a healthy, happy mental attitude, have been fulfilled as demonstrable, scientific truths. The results are astonishing. For instance, stomach ulcers, according to the findings of a group of American physicians of the highest standing, have been found to originate, not in the stomach, but in the brain. They are the end results of hate, worry, anger, anxiety, grief, and other negative mental attitudes. "But," says the reader, "these conditions with many people are uncontrollable."

That is often true, but you must realize that if your thoughts are toxic, your whole body and your whole future life may be perilously poisoned.

Mankind has thought of smiles and music as passing superficialities. Smiles affect the face and music affects the ears. Both, however, literally saturate the body and soul, producing psychological and physiological results of limitless importance. Music, which affects the emotions so definitely and directly, is one of the inexplicable benedictions of the Creator in helping all to regain a personal equilibrium, particularly in these days of dreadful strain. How music works to accomplish this result is still a great enigma. Some day it may be scientifically explained. A relatively short time ago, few had any idea what electricity is. Although the force had been used in a gigantic manner, its operation was little understood. It was the discovery of the electronic theory which solved the age-old puzzle. Sir Joseph John Thomson (Professor of Experimental Physics at Cambridge University, England from 1884 to 1908) discovered electrons, which are nothing more than fabulously minute particles of electric dust which seem to exist between atoms and between molecules, which are groups of atoms. You are doubtless familiar with the old experiment in the physics laboratory in which a glass is filled to the brim with water and then a teaspoonful of alcohol is poured into the glass. It does not overflow because the alcohol runs in between the molecules of the water. Well, change the picture to a vessel filled with molecules, and you can pour in an almost equal quantity of electrons without making it run over.

At this moment you are surrounded by millions of electrons, which are even passing through your body in incalculable numbers. Infinitely smaller than any atom of any other element, they can be put into motion to produce light, power, heat, and even music itself, as in the case of the Hammond Novachord.

Because electronic innovations and amplifications have now become so closely identified with music, and because they help in making clear our point in this editorial, we may be pardoned for dwelling a moment upon some characteristics of their nature which some of our readers may not be familiar. We all are literally saturated with electrons, but we know little about them. Formerly an atom was considered the smallest conceivable particle of matter. No one as yet has seen an atom or a molecule. Water is composed of atoms and molecules (groups of atoms). As water flows over a mill wheel, power is created. Music and Culture

The Musician
and the Common Cold

How Famous Artists Have Fought the Most Common Malady

by Waldemar Schweissheimer, M.D.

TO THE ARTIST MUSICIAN of renown a cold which compels the cancellation of an important public appearance may result in very real loss. One artist claimed that a cold which lasted, with its distressing symptoms, for six weeks, cost her over fifty thousand dollars. The teacher, to the chorus singer, and to the orchestral performer whose weekly income may be quite moderate, a cold may be relatively no less of a calamity. It is generally recognized that colds and the complications which may accompany them result in an economic loss to the nation which is staggering. There is a general consensus of opinion among medical men that colds are directly the result of a communicable virus (or collection of several viruses) which is so minute that it passes through the finest filters, no matter how fine they may be, and although it has not yet been discovered, all thought of the many remedies which act as palliatives and help to prevent the development of the grave conditions which may result from colds. The virus is spread by contact and through the air. The most active distributor is the coughing or sneeze of a victim which, if uncovered, may spray a large room with cold germs. During the cold season "germs are everywhere." If you are tired, run-down, or if your digestive system is "out of order," due to excesses in eating and drinking and resulting in a state of auto-intoxication, you become at once a host to millions of cold germs. Sore throat, inflamed eyes, "come all over," and all the distressing symptoms are only too familiar. The best remedy for a cold is a visit to a good doctor as soon as you can get there. He will probably tell you to go to bed and not to sing for several days if you have a fever. There are, however, certain sensible remedies, which I shall describe later, which every musician should know in case a physician is not at hand. Today our conditions have reduced the number of physicians for civilian treatment, and everyone should be familiar with approved emergency measures for the treatment of colds in their first stages.

Various Treatments

Association with musicians has enabled me to secure a number of incidents revealing how many have sought to combat colds.

The famous singer, Luisa Tetrazzini, was suffering from a cold and was worried about the condition of her voice for the evening's concert. Enrico Caruso told her, "I have a good spray! It will make your throat well in no time." He sprayed her throat carefully with a mixture containing, besides other ingredients, ether and iodoform. As he sprayed she began to pant quickly and she dried no sop for a moment or she would have gone down her lungs and as anesthetized as this spray acted something like a varnish or a coat of shellac over the vocal cords. The result was that she was benefited, for according to the morning papers Madame Tetrazzini sang with her usual pure and beautiful voice. Such a method, however, should not be attempted except in the hands of a medical expert.

All musicians, instrumentalists as well as singers, are permanently afraid of catching colds. There is nearly always a draught at some part of the orchestra or on the concert stage. Colds are frequently caught in over-heated, unventilated halls or cars. Many singers have taken to living in an overheated room and then suffer from sudden chilling. A singer who has been exposed to dampness or the cold of an evening should seek warmth and rest immediately.

Body Susceptibility

An external struggle exists between window-opener musicians and window-shutter musicians — that is to say, those who do not like draughts and others who do not care, the genuine "draught hater." At least this group should be obliged to have some control. A few of the older musicians take this matter seriously and do not expose themselves to chills which result from careless habits in exposure. A cold may, in some cases, be prevented by the occasional use of simple home methods such as the following: Avoid eating and drinking to excess and overindulgence; keep your bowels regular; have a proper night’s rest; protect the throat and chest from sudden changes of temperature; avoid continuous use of alcohol on the eve of a performance; do not use tobacco freely; and above all, if you feel the onset of a chill, get to bed, keep warm and call a competent physician. A few simple preventative measures will often avert a protracted illness.

Music and Culture

Color in Singing

An Interview with
John Charles Thomas

Renowned American Baritone

SECURED EXPRESSLY FOR THE ETUDE BY ROSE HEYLBUT

JOHN CHARLES THOMAS

ACCORDING to John Charles Thomas the essence of good singing is color. Just as the artist secures his effects by blending the paints on his palette, so the singer projects personality in music by varying the color of his tone. Further, both artist and singer use similar working methods: before they are ready to work in color they must know the theory and the effect of the various pigments; and they must create one color only after the foundations of their craft are solidly laid. To the artist, technique means line and form; to the singer it means sound, tone and production.

"While the singer is still a student it is difficult for him to realize that the business of vocal production which occupies his attention so completely, is not really singing at all. It is merely the material from which singing is made; the foundation upon which it is reared. Neither does singing mean the mere sounding of notes or arias one learns. It means but one thing: the validity of what one is able to project—the significance of music. This, naturally, involves a number of things, ranging from simple rules of health to philosophies of interpretation.

"First of all, the singer should be in robust health and should take active and constant steps to keep that way. If some singers—especially opera singers—peter out early, it is because they get into a set routine of coaching, singing, eating and sleeping, without giving the body proper care. Perhaps they think that 'health' means the absence of symptoms. It doesn't, of course; the basis of good health is body tone. And it is this, precisely, that is so necessary to public work. The singer who stirs his audience conveys an atmosphere of vitality, and this vitality demands as much care as any wholly technical department of singing. The singer's diet is important. I cannot tell young singers that to eat and what to avoid, those are matters that each person must decide for himself according to the needs of his body metabolism. But I can tell you that the object of diet should be vigorous health, vim and naturalness of response. I prefer outdoor exercise such as can be used as a hobby as well as a health precaution. The positive mental reaction that results from the hearty enjoyment of a good game like golf (my own hobby) and

from the consciousness of being close to God's great earth, build up reserves of physical and spiritual tone that are drawn upon in future work. Just now, my chief vocal problem is improving my golf swing. I say this in all seriousness. I work at my golf technique every day, partly because I like it and partly because I know it will serve as a stimulus to my singing.

Good Teacher Plus Good Student

"The basic vocal technique upon which all artistic projection rests is generally thought to depend upon a good teacher. Actually, this is only half true. The other half of the truth is that it depends on the student. The one who is being taught must be alert to the correct factors which the teacher will point out. The teacher can do the job alone. The student is responsible for more than carrying out day-to-day instructions; he must make sure that the teacher's methods help him. For a number of singers—some in high places—have had many teachers and still don't feel secure. It is possible, of course, that a person might have the idea that the student alone should have the vocal talent and that the teacher merely guides. But the reverse is the case; the faults of many students arise because they do not studiously observe the teacher's directions. A teacher may show what to do but the student must do it. There is no substitute for steady practice in the right way.

"The second vital point in basic production is resonance. The proper concept of resonance must be established in the student. He must know how to use the cavities of the head and chest, how to place tones, how to support them by a steady breath stream and how to avoid forcing the sound. Too frequently young singers try to 'project' by strain. The result is a hard, unpleasant tone and a tired voice. Resonance, correctly used, gives a natural, free, resonant tone.

"The secret of breathing is another important thing. I do not believe in teaching a singer to 'breathe low' as a metaphysical formula. Breathing must be correct and it must be natural. I would say to every student, learn to breathe in a relaxed manner, using the diaphragm properly, yet not rigidly. I have heard a lot about 'learning how to breathe,' of course, and as my lessons progressed and nothing was said about this prodigious problem, I grew worried. Mrs. Blackman told me that I breathed naturally and that she was therefore unwilling to interfere with my habits. I have since learned to be doubly thankful for such wise guidance. Breathing should not be made for a fetish, never be doiable obstacles show themselves, the singer should be unhampered. He may use exercises, of course, to strengthen and develop the breath capacity, but the breath should be freed from 'methods' and from unnatural attitudes.

"The student, likewise, in the taking, of the breath should be natural. In ordinary life we are most conscious of our breathing in singing and in effort. The thing to avoid is the breathing that affects the tone. The student should think more of the tone than of the breath; the former will take care of the latter if the basic principles are properly observed.

"The problem in resonance is that production is often sacrificed to mere loudness. The voice must be full, even, and under control. The place of resonance is not a question of 'face' or 'mask' alone but of the whole tone system working together. When a singer learns to make full use of the cavities above the larynx he obtains a tone that carries and at the same time is free from strain. The laryngeal muscles must be relaxed and the glottis must vibrate freely. The student who works only on the laryngeal part of the mechanism and ignores the facial and nasal cavities will always be handicapped.

"Good diction and fine musical instincts are also a part of what I call 'color' in singing. To secure color the singer must care for nuances, shading, subtleties of volume and tone. He must study the text and the musical phrase until he understands exactly what is required. One cannot expect to create fine interpretations from a mere imitation of another singer. Individuality is essential. Study the styles and devices of the great singers, but do not copy them. Find your own method of expression consistent with your voice.

"I have been fortunate in attracting good students during the last years. They come to me not so much because of fame but because they want practical results. The voice must be trained so that it may be used for a lifetime of expression. A teacher who gives the student reliable methods rather than 'tricks' is the one who turns out good artists."

The Importance of
Piano Posture
by George MacNabb

CORRECT POSTURE is as important as any requisite of a good pianist. It is impossible to render fine playing without it. Posture has a definite psychological effect in establishing confidence, comfort, and ease. It should be an asset and not a handicap.

Natural posture is the best posture. It gives freedom of motion and coordination of mental and physical faculties. The posture which enables one to accomplish the particular pianistic problems of the moment with the greatest simplicity and economy is the one to be encouraged. One must be comfortable to be in command of every detail of performance. Numerous playing problems deny one set posture. It is a variable and it changes for every pupil. Encourage the position which will bring out a maximum of results with a minimum of effort. Matthay says, “Good posture is the resultant but not the assurance of correct balance in the forces we use.”

Muscular energy used in piano playing is not produced by the fingers alone, but flows from all parts of the body. It is important, therefore, that all parts of the body be placed so as to cause no obstruction to this flow of energy. Hands that are too close, too low or too high, cramped position. Since piano playing involves movement, and movement always means change of position, basic posture rules are general and for the purpose of orientation only. Correct or natural posture means balance, coordinated, better circulation, more careful and concentrated listening, and finer playing results.

A Variety of Ideas

Some of the early masters had very dogmatic ideas on posture. Busoni they considered the prime mover since he claimed from his point of view, that the hand is the sole source of power and tone. While he insisted upon a quiet, inactive hand, Moscheles demanded passages played with a glass of water balanced on the wrist; Clementi used coins on the wrist and the back of the hand; Dussek urged inclining the body a little to the left because of the difficulty of giving power and action to the right hand; Kalkbrenner said a little to the right of the middle of the keyboard to accomplish the same effect on the right side. Apparently these men played well in spite of their theories, if not because of them, and they may have unconsciously used modern approaches. Even Leschetizky insisted upon arched knuckles for great muscular development of hands and fingers. This muscular strength can be attained without greatly arched knuckles, and both are often exaggerated. What we really want is not sheer muscular strength but an equalization of strength, energy, and flow of power. Leschetizky developed some of the famous positions of his day, although he disclaimed any particular method. He wisely studied the particular needs of each pupil and supplied the solution accordingly. Regarding posture he said, “Sit at the piano and consider and direct like a good horseman, and yield to the movements of arms and as far as necessary, as the rider yields to the movements of the horse.” He was urging pliability and adaptability.

Basic body posture directions are few and simple. Sit intuitively at the same spot in front of the middle of the keyboard with the elbows on a general level with the keybed. This elbow level varies with the arm size, shape, and muscular condition of the student. Lean slightly forward in the chair. The length of the arms decides the distance of the chair and the player from the keyboard, but the keyboard must be reached comfortably. Avoid rounding and stooping of the shoulders. Any leaning forward should come from the waist. It is important that the feet rest on the floor at all times. Dangling of the feet is certainly not conducive to any kind of concentration. Harold Bauer says, “Test this by studying on a mantle with the feet dangling.” Even small children can usually reach the floor with the feet if they sit near the edge of the chair, and if this is not possible a footstool should be used. The chair height is decided by what is natural for balance and coordination. Chopin said, “If your playing looks well it probably also will sound well; or if you do it well, it will also probably look well.”

The best pianistic hand position is the one which is most natural and comfortable to the particular hand. The actual shape of the hand and fingers varies with each individual and with each passage, but the very general rule is the most facilitative. For variations of hand positions and finger actions look for the great virtuosi and you will find many different approaches with beautiful results in every case. Mme. Taussig [or Mme. Tauber—word unclear] contrary to all teaching she had read, played with flat hands and fingers, claiming it was the only way she could play, and she excelled in the performance of Mozart, Scarlatti, and the early composers. A great singer sings the best he has ever; the imitation of some method may not be the best for you; however imposition by teachers to impose their method often produces poor tonal results and makes the pupil more mechanical and less creative.

Hand Posture

Hand posture, like body posture, is for the purpose of relaxation, because position changes as soon as the hand moves beyond the range of natural and movable play, although it should be varied more than is necessary. It takes long training to attain control, strength, and flexibility. No matter how natural, every hand needs training, amount of training. As Paderewski said, “Before one becomes a genius he must first be a drudge.” The entire system—arms, shoulders, neck, and back—must be trained so the hand is in a useful position.

Correct vowel sounds are as vital to basic tone production as they are to the separate study of enunciation. A good way to correct them is this: Sing a tone on a vowel (your best vowel first; all of them in time), and when the tone comes freely, follow it up with M or N as a sort of tiny grace note at the end. Your exercise will sound Ah—ah—m, or ah—m—ah, and so on. This exercise prepares the way for good enunciation and ensures to keep the tone well in the mask by fixing it either with one of the up-arching or nasal consonants. Why put the consonant at the end? Because tone is more likely to slip after the initial attack.

Enunciation and Tone Production

The problem of enunciation is closely bound up with that of basic tone production. Here the American singer is at a certain disadvantage since he must learn to sing in all languages, while the Italian, French, or German singer makes use of his own tongue. The use of many languages involves more than the mere business of learning them. It involves a thorough knowledge of the color of each language and of the effect that color may have on tone production. English, for instance, is a nasal language; French, a chest language; German is guttural; Italian, in the head. Once the singer has practiced his in good order, he should have no difficulty in adjusting to the individualities of the different languages; but the inexperienced student must watch these adjustments with careful alertness so that the guttural nature of German text, let us say, does not force his tones into his throat.

It is my belief that good enunciation is the first step to artistic interpretation. I always base my own approach on the pattern of the song—indeed, I never do anything industrially. The word 'industrial' does not seem moving and (or) beautiful to me. The words set the mood for a song and give it its color. The music emphasizes and enhances the mood and color. That is why it is important to sing songs in their original keys; that way the transposition of operatic arias does not permit the range to do a singer’s voice. The real effect is lost to the singer when his recitative is pitched to his own convenience.

Music and Culture

The Original Don Cossacks and the Music of the Don

An Interview with

Serge Jaroff

Founder and Conductor of The Original Don Cossacks

SECURED EXPRESSLY FOR THE ETUDE BY STEPHEN WEST
ASSISTED BY WASSILY PLUSHKOFF, TENOR

FOR THE PAST twelve years, Serge Jaroff's Original Don Cossack group has ranked as an organization that, for all its vigor, color, originality, and excellence, was in no sense more than a strictly musical organization. Last winter, however, events gave the group new significance. The Donets Basin, scene of the most gallant fighting in the history of war, is the home of these thirty-two Don Cossack giants. The spirit animating Russia's heroic resistance of aggression is the spirit of their music. Their songs and their singing convey more than mere entertainment; they reflect the pulsing essence of everything that comes to mind with "Russia's winter of 1942-43." Oddly enough, this same winter marked an event of which the Don Cossacks are as proud as they are of their heritage, since the group received their American citizenship!

A Happy Accident

The Original Don Cossack group had its beginnings in a happy accident. About twenty-four years ago, a crack Cossack regiment was stationed in a lonely camp in Turkey. As was customary in the old Russian army, the men with the finest voices were chosen to take part in the religious services. Among the group in the Turkish camp was young Lieutenant Serge Jaroff, who served as choir-master. Lieutenant Jaroff was a gifted musician. Fresh from the famous Semenoff School (for conductors), where he had distinguished himself, Jaroff heard the men singing the regimental mass and decided these voices were material to be welded into a vocal instrument. Within two years, the Cossack choir had grown. In 1921 the group was chosen as the official choir of the Orthodox Cathedral of St. Sofia in Bulgaria's capital, and before long music-lovers from all over the world made their way to the great cathedral to hear this choir. An impresario, who came with the idea that he perhaps the most original a cappella choir in the world, urged Mr. Jaroff to enter the concert field. Since 1923 the Original Don Cossacks have given over five thousand concerts in four continents, and in more cities than they can keep track of without reference to tour-books.

In the following conference Mr. Jaroff traces the significance of the Cossack music, and outlines some of the technical points that make the singing of his group unique.

"Cossack songs are the songs of the people. When you hear them, you hear more than melody and rhythm; you hear the very soul of the Cossacks, voicing the joys and sorrows of a thousand years. The Cossacks, as a people, have been on the watch for centuries and have learned to sing of all things — of the ride of the patrol, of the loneliness of a picket, of the parting of a mother and son, of the return of a hunter, of a harvest song, of a wedding song. Our songs are not written; they are born. They live and grow, just as man does. But the songs do not die. They rest for a period of years, and come back in new forms. Today a 'new' melody may be easily recognized by a 'new' variation as one of the old melodies he knew as a child. The people of the Don make their songs from the stuff of their lives. Today the people are mostly farmers, working the land or from the steppe country. When the harvest is gathered, the men load it on great farm wagons and drive it home, often twenty miles distant. The movement of the heavy carts is slow and the trip lasts for hours. And the man who drives the horses thinks along all the way of home. He thinks in song. Perhaps he sings a traditional air that suits his mood; perhaps he expresses his thoughts in simple words which he repeats over and over until a tune that fits them comes to his mind. Then he has a song of his own. He may weave a melody about a single word. He watches his horse and notices a small thing, a stone. He may set the stone to a word and that is enough for a song. The world for him is grayish (gvos-dik). He begins to sing the simple gvos-dik, gvos-dik... elaborating it with turns of melody until he sees his house ahead of him; then he brings his song to a close and adds the final -shtik. The entire process may last for hours, and only one word has been sung.

"Such spontaneous songs come into being in most of the little communities where people have few belongings except their songs. Many of our songs are characteristic of occupations — Cossack cavalry songs, Cossack shepherd songs, etc. Songs also arise out of such circumstances as the Cossack wedding, the cassock service, the supper hour, and the spring holiday. The Don man sings at table; he sings when he works; he sings when he worships. There is often a religious basis to the singing, and many of our sacred songs are based upon the old Orthodox chant.

"Our songs are modal, and the chief characteristic of their quality is the use of parallel intervals. We do not have a polyphonic system in the Western sense; we are not harmonizing to the chordal system. Instead we follow a tradition of parallel voice-leading which very often produces intervals of the fourth and the fifth between the voices. The bass voice in our chorus often sings a kind of pedal, a very low drone which supports the upper melody and provides a resonance, an organ-like foundation for the rest of the vocal fabric."

It is often supposed, Mr. Jaroff continued, that the Cossack chorus is a purely male institution; that, in short, the repertoire is masculine and the choir is made up of men alone. "In our home practice we had both men and women singers," he said, "but as the group developed into a concert organization we found that the best public effect was obtained with the male voices. The nature of many of our songs needs the masculine timbre and the harsh ringing quality which the male voices give. In Russia both men and women have a rich singing tradition, but what we have chosen to present to the world is the voice of the Don as expressed by the men."

The technical qualities of the group which impress the lay listener most are the clarity and precision of ensemble, the depth of the basses, and the ringing top notes which, at the climaxes of the songs, seem to pierce the air. Mr. Jaroff explained that these technical results are produced by a number of devices. "First," he said, "we do much preliminary work in the building of tone. Each member of the chorus must produce the proper quality of sound, and for this reason we drill carefully in tone-emission and in vowel formation. We insist on a closed throat and a rounded vowel, which permits the voice to ring. Secondly, we have strict attention to the balance of voices. The basses are not merely supporters; they are full solo voices which must be carefully placed in relation to the tenors and the altos. Thirdly, we use certain devices of articulation and accent which are characteristic of our singing. The cut-off of the syllable, the particular stress on a vowel, these are things we practice day after day.

"Our repertoire is made up of folk-songs, ritual songs, and songs which have been arranged from popular themes. In arranging a folk-song for chorus we try never to change the basic character of the melody. We arrange by supporting the original tune; we do not recompose it. There is no big harmony added for its own sake. Harmony is a servant of the melody and must always be subordinate to it."

The Cossack singing style, Mr. Jaroff said, has been influenced by many historical factors. "In the old days, the nomadic life, the constant warfare, the long rides, had a direct bearing on the kinds of songs that were made. These songs were often short, direct, and full of vigor; they were made to be sung aloud and to carry far. The long open steppe, with horses and wide horizon, gives a different quality to the music than does the forest or the city. In the last hundred years, the contact with other peoples, and particularly with Ukrainians and Russians, has modified the style. But the essence remains — the modal scale, the strong bass, the resonant top notes, and the sense of rhythm that was made for riding."

A question about notation brought from Mr. Jaroff the reply that most of the songs have not been written down. "I myself have made arrangements and collected a good number of the old tunes," he said, "and many of our current arrangements are based on oral tradition. When we prepare a concert program, the music must be fixed; it is not a purely improvisational art at that point. But the roots are in folk tradition."

Asked whether he taught the singers any particular kind of breathing, Mr. Jaroff replied that their breathing method is in the main the natural breathing of men who are used to long singing and to shouting. "We do work on breath control," he said, "but we do not use the operatic training which some choral groups employ. Our production is more direct; it is a chest voice production which gives the ringing quality we need. We teach the men to place the tone forward in the mouth and to use the resonance of the head as well. We do not seek a mellow operatic tone; rather we want clarity, projection, and an edge which will carry in a large hall."

The question of language and diction brought out Mr. Jaroff's insistence upon the importance of the text. "A song without clear words is of little use for us," he said. "The words are the vehicle of the story and the feeling. We sharpen consonants, we articulate, and we make every syllable speak. In many of our songs the text is as important as the melody."

Mr. Jaroff was asked to comment upon the use of movement in his concerts — the sometimes spectacular stepping and squatting which are associated with Cossack performances. "The dances and the acrobatics are not merely gimmicks," he said. "They are expressions of the life of the people. The squat, the high kick, the rapid change of position, these are derived from the riding and the fighting; they are part of the physical vocabulary of the Cossack. But we use movement sparingly and with taste. We do not want the concert to become a circus. The movement must serve the music, not the other way around."

On the subject of appearance and costume Mr. Jaroff said that he aimed at authenticity. "We use the Cossack uniform and typical costume," he said. "The visual effect is important, for the costume represents the people whose songs we sing. But the costume is not a pretence; it is the real garb of a people. The fur hats, the beshmet, the sash — these all mean something to those who know the life of the Don."

Mr. Jaroff was asked about the future of his group and of Cossack music in general. He said he hoped that he might continue to expand the repertoire and to do more to preserve the old songs by making careful transcriptions. "I would like," he said, "to have a collection of Don songs that would be accessible to the public and to students. We must not let these tunes die. They embody a portion of Russian life that is of value for history and for art."

There are, of course, modern composers who have borrowed freely from the Cossack tradition, Mr. Jaroff observed, and some of them have successfully integrated Cossack elements into symphonic or choral works. YOUTH IS HOURLY clamoring for a new Bill of Rights. It calls for a fascinating interest in all its undertakings which seems to throw prismatic lights upon the gray hours of childhood and the happy "teen" years.

Miss Carlton handed Jane a new piece. "For the next lesson," she directed, "you may start practicing on the first page." Reluctantly Jane glanced at the title: Adagio from "String Quartet in G minor," by Haydn. Stuffing the music indifferently into her brief case, she walked listlessly out of the room.

Can you blame her? No, of course not. An assignment given in a tone so matter-of-fact and devoid of inspirational value will not arouse ambition. How could Miss Carlton have stimulated interest in this delightful classic? By helping Jane to get into the spirit of the composition before asking her to start the drudgery of technical mastery. The words Adagio, String Quartet, and Haydn, did not create any mental image in Jane's mind. But they could easily be made to do so.

Most of Haydn's quartets were written while he was Musician to the Court of Esterhazy. An imaginative teacher would describe to Jane the candle-lighted music room in the rococo palace where musicians under Haydn's direction played to aristocratic audiences dressed in periwigs and satin finery. Doesn't that add color to the dull words Adagio, and quartet?

Let us consider some of the ways a teacher can add glamour to his assignments.

Biographical and Interpretative Approach

It is helpful to tell the pupil something about the life of the composer. Not a long, biographical lecture consisting of unimportant dates and statistics, but interesting human information which bears upon the composition to be studied. If historical or interpretative notes of this kind are printed on the edition of the composition being used, try to end your remarks with a question, the answer of which can be learned by reading the editorial comments. Unless you awaken the curiosity of the pupil in this manner, he may not bother to read the printed matter. If you do not know the circumstances under which a piece was created, tell outstanding facts about the composer's life, and discuss briefly the characteristics of his style found in the music under examination.

Less important composers present more of a problem, because it is difficult and often impossible to find anything about them. In such cases, you will have to dig deeply into the piece to discover for yourself the tonal message. You will find much of this color background in "Music Masters Old and New," as well as in carefully outlined composer programs. Audiences of all sizes are always devoted to the works of some distinctive master and appreciate biographical notes.

Hearing an entire composition before practice is begun on sections stimulates the pupil to a whole-hearted interest. The many ways of setting a tempo which makes us wish to eat the food. If you will play a piece for a pupil or let him listen to a recorded, or call his attention to a forthcoming radio broadcast, his musical interest will be awakened. The lovely rhythms and harmonies that delight at the hearing will be remembered and the child will give him a goal toward which he can work with zest.

Other Interesting Methods of Approach

Rhythmic introductions are effective. If you ask a pupil to play the entire number, or to learn to sing it, the piece will be easier and more enjoyable for him to learn to play the selection.

Discussion about a piece will frequently excite curiosity. Take the number, The Bee's Lullaby, by Frances Terry, in the July, 1938, Etude. What a novel title! Start conversation with questions like these: "Have you ever seen a bee baby?" Some pupils will answer and tell you what they know about the life of bees. They will then be ready to practice this charming little composition.

Correlating an assignment with something familiar, such as a current happening, increases its attractiveness. For example, if a nearby town is preparing for a celebration, talk about the anticipated event. Then assign A Village Festival, by Frederick Williams. When the circus is coming to town, give The Clown, by Carl Wilhough [sic]. Some music teachers find it profitable to ask pupils to dramatize a composition, or to plan a small program based upon a simple theme. A group of pupils might be asked to present "An Evening in Old Vienna" with appropriate music, costumes and readings. The child's imagination will do the rest. He will purchase interest and enthusiasm.

Make the assignment concrete by requiring some material product. Ask the pupil to prepare a program note, or to mount some pictures, or to report in writing on the composer. Let him bring to the lesson a picture or an object which will help him to understand better the mood of the composition. Give him a definite task to perform, and he will work for that achievement.

Theatrical Devices

If you have the slightest dramatic ability, you can use it effectively. A teacher who is a good story-teller can create a charming introductory tale which will set the pupil's imagination into motion. An eccentric costume, a few stage properties, or even a simple tableau created by the pupils themselves will arouse enthusiasm. The dramatization need not be elaborate. A cardboard castle, a homemade trumpet, a pair of wings for an angel, will often serve the purpose. Theatrical presentation is particularly effective for little children.

A simple pantomime or a short skit connected with the theme of the composition will add color to the lesson and will make technical practice less tedious. The pupils will be eager to prepare for the presentation and will practice more willingly.

Visual Aids

Pictures, maps, charts and prints are effective aids in music teaching. A visual background helps the child to see a mental picture of the composition. Make a study corner in the studio where you keep material to illustrate the life of composers, the costumes of different periods, and the types of instruments used at various times. A child's sense of wonder will be increased if he can see what you are discussing.

Phonograph records and radio programs are splendid means of making music familiar. To have a child hear a composition in its entirety will often do more to develop understanding and appreciation than many lessons. The teacher who knows how to use the sound media will find them invaluable.

The Library and the Museum

Take advantage of public library resources. Many branches maintain fine collections of records and books which are at the disposal of students. Illustrate lessons with prints from the library, borrow lantern slides, collect programs and biographies. Museums can be visited to show the pupils examples of decorative art of various periods.

Make use of school concerts and community music events. Encourage pupils to attend concerts and to report upon them. Ask older pupils to write short reviews of concerts which can be read in class.

Club and Class Programs

If you are responsible for a class or club program, plan it carefully with your pupils. Select a theme, and arrange the music so that it is presented with variety. Provide a balance between old and new, serious and light. An attractive program stimulates listeners as well as performers.

Pupils should be taught how to prepare a program note. Give them models of short, interesting program notes and ask them to write their own. This task will develop their power to think about music in expressive words.

Teaching the Art of Listening

Much of music study today centers upon the correct mechanical production of tone and the development of technique. But the training of the ear and the cultivation of discriminating taste are equally important. The teacher who helps pupils to listen intelligently, who directs attention to form, color, texture, and balance, finds the work more rewarding.

Play examples, point out themes, harmonic changes, and distinctive rhythms. Teach the pupils to follow the score in yet simple compositions. Show them how a melody is passed from one instrument to another, and how different timbres create a variety of effects.

Summary

The teacher who will give time to the imaginative introduction of each new piece, who will use biography, pictures, story, dramatic presentation, and the sound media, will stimulate interest and develop musical taste. Pupils who are excited by their assignments will practice with greater enthusiasm and will achieve more lasting results.

Glamour and Color in Music Study
How to organize Class and Club Programs that Stimulate Interest
by Helen Oliphant Bates

[Right page, top right header: Music and Study]

"A MERICAN MUSIC LOVERS need to cultivate 'a concept of style,'" said one of our foremost orchestra conductors recently, in addressing a convention.

All that matters is music and the styles of interpretation appropriate for various kinds of music. Style has come to be associated, wrongly, with individual performers, conductors, instruments, or groups, rather than with the composers whose music is at stake. Connoisseurs drool over military music; conductors distort and romanticize Bach; dance-band "maestros" flatten out the classics into vulgar "hits," and all these enemies against taste are condemned by saying, "That's his (the performer's) style!" Now this tendency has reached the world of organs and organists.

This article, therefore, raises three questions regarding style in organ building and organ playing: First, what is this distinction now made between "classical" and "romantic" organs? Second, what is the historical relationship between organ and orchestra? And finally, can we not apply the same criteria to the organ that are applied to other musical instruments? These questions embrace the power to interpret all styles of music and the adaptability for mingling with other instruments and voices in ensembles, for a purpose.

I. "Classical" and "Romantic" Organs

Much ink has been used to explain the differences between "classical" and "romantic" music. Yet music is not worthy of the name unless it is both "classical" and "romantic"; that is, unless it has both "formal" structure together with "romantic" expressiveness. Even an unimaginative composer or performer gives us music according to "classical rules" made by pedants, the result may be as correct as a skeleton, but it will be just as dead. When a very emotional person makes thrilling crescendos and diminuendos with breast-heaving vibratos his music may be effective, but unless it is closely harmonized, the effects may be purely "expressive"; but unless it is well balanced, together with line and rhythmic balance, the effect will be chaotic. Every work of art must be as well constructed in form and as beautifully colored in expression as the poet who says much in few words.

Fundamental Differences

Today this problem is manifest in all organ building and organ playing, and it has been called by some "the romantic organ." On one hand the new "classical" organs are designed after the old historic instruments, notably the Baroque organs of Germany, Holland, England, and France. These instruments are characterized by color contrasts and by brilliant, decisive pipe speech. They tend to provide clarity and animation, yet they are lighter in construction and emphasize the clean, detached attack. These organs are effective for contrapuntal music and for the interpretation of music written for the organ in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

On the other hand the modern "romantic" organ is designed to imitate the sounds of the orchestra and to supply the lush, continuous background so useful to theatrical and symphonic music. To do this the organ builder has developed many stops and couplers, flue and reed combinations, balanced swell boxes, and a large number of stops providing rich foundation and heavy basses. The romantic organ is at the other extreme. Not content to be an organ, it attempts to imitate the intimate orchestra, with shimmering strings, swelling oohs and aahs, bubbling French horns, flutes of all kinds (hooty, boomy, and cutely), trumpets, trombones, tubas, celestas, harps and all the utensils of the orchestra battery and the endless variety of gadgets in the sound-effect room.

Power Biggs or Carl Weinrich plays old music on the classical organ, in the hope that it will give the clarity of singing voices; the modern organ adaptation is designed, as the organist exaggerates, to give an orchestral background so that music not well adapted to the organ will sound otherwise acceptable and useful. The modern organ will have all the sounds of an orchestra. This adaptation is useful in many churches, but the classical organ can perform a wide range of music with distinction when the organist respects the style.

Artistic Restraint Needed

On the other hand, the romantic organ usually can be played with artistic restraint. By not using certain features of the orchestral organ and by selecting stops judiciously a skillful organist can play in a manner which effectively supports congregational singing or accompanies soloists with sympathetic sonority. But the romantic organ needs an organist of taste who will exercise restraint. If the organist is overactive there is danger of creating an orchestra in the sanctuary which is out of place and out of proportion.

There are other considerations in the question of organ types. The classical organ is usually more effective in small churches where the architecture and the acoustics favor clear, crisp tone. The romantic organ is often preferred in large churches or in theaters and auditoriums where a grand, orchestral effect is desired. Both types have their uses and their limitations. The important thing is to choose the organ that will best serve the needs and the musical purposes of the place in which it is to be used.

Music or Show

by Maynard Klein

THE PHENOMENAL GROWTH of choral singing in our schools and colleges is common knowledge. The few choral organizations of recent decades have multiplied to the extent that every school now has its glee club or chorus. The pioneers of music education and those who are active in this development are worthy of the highest tribute, for without their foresight and unwearing interest in the attainment of an ideal, this growth would not be possible. It is not at all uncommon that choral singing should flourish in a country like ours, a nation where unbounded youth and enthusiasm make it possible to accomplish the apparently impossible. In music education, as in all other phases of American life, success has been secure because there is no concession made to failure or defeat.

Now that we have accomplished this apparent success in the organization of choral groups in our schools and colleges, let us analyze the aims and objectives that have been the motivating force in most cases. The following statement may seem a bit strong, but it is our opinion that in many instances the lofty values of choral singing have been diminished to student or conductor exploitation, thus banishing all hope of maintaining the subtle cues to richer life that would be apparent through the religious study of the great music that is our cultural heritage. The choral director must project his thinking beyond a mere concert program if any of these values are to be realized. Too many of our schools have placed the choral groups in the same category with its athletic teams; in other words, making their purpose that of excelling in their locality, to the educational detriment of the students. There is no doubt that competition for excellence will do much to motivate the development of any music group (the contests and festivals have proved this point), but competition should be considered as incidental to the study and appreciation of the masterpieces of choral literature, past and present.

The Point of Departure

What then should be the point of departure for the choral director in school and college? The answer is MUSIC! This answer can be meaningfully put into those teachers who have a genuine and intelligent interest in good music and an appreciation of the cultural heritage. To realize a better choral group who would direct choral music should have an insatiable desire to seek out the best literature that has been produced by the great masters, and should have, as well, a sincere and honest approach to the music of our own modern composers. He should be sensitive in his musical production to the needs of the public. The public is not, in a true sense, the critic at the time of presentation.

It would be dangerous for anyone to feel that the mere reading and study of the great masterpieces would be sufficient. The choral director should be competent in many ways. It is absolutely necessary that he should be a thorough, practical musician, and that his working knowledge of harmony, counterpoint, analysis, and sight-singing is beyond reproach. He should possess all the attributes that make possible practical musical production. His knowledge of the many problems of voice production and choral training should be unquestioned. The various successful methods of organizing choral groups are dealt with in detail in any number of books that are easily accessible to the choral aspirant. If he is sure that he possesses this practical musicianship, and if he has also a pleasing approach and a love of people, he is ready to go to the basics of the whole problem—musical background.

It is right here that the trouble begins, for the apparently talented person described above will find it very simple to approach an easy success without doing the things that we hold important in the great aim of our art, namely the presentation of the best choral literature. He will present good “shows” that are flashy and sensational at the moment — he will get an immediate response from the audience and his name may become “catchy” and fall in line with the imitative programs of the moment. Arriving so easily at this type of success, he is sure to deem it unnecessary to go deeper into the study of literature. But it is only through careful research in the field of choral literature that the “talented” musician will find his true salvation and, in turn, the musical salvation of his students; for, however well the choir may sing, the important question to be raised should always be, “What did they sing?” And then the question, “How did they sing?” When the choral director possesses this something called musical background, the way he relates both questions will receive favorable answers. There is no substitute for a truly musical program presented solely for music interest.

How should he go about getting this intangible background? It is not to be had from a publisher’s catalog! The music catalog is a most important device for the choral director only when he has the musical background sufficient to use it in the proper way. The choral teacher should begin by admitting the fact that he knows but an inkling of the real amount of literature that is available and then he should begin a systematic program of historical research and study of materials that are so easily recorded at this time. Study of social as well as musical history, study of the allied arts, and reading of the literary masterpieces should be the point of departure for a larger appreciation of the values of great choral music. It may seem discouraging at first; for the teacher who suddenly becomes aware that he knows so little of the great music from which he is to choose in building the repertoire for his students. This, however, should not be the time for despair; it is the redeeming factor and it makes growth possible. He will then gain a proper perspective for the kind of music that he believes his group can best handle. He will be able to worship great art as a religion, and only then that his love of beauty will begin to be felt by the students. This is no shallow thing, such as the presentation of a program before a civic group; it is, rather, the unleashing of an inner drive to express something greater than self, an expression made possible through the minds of great masters.

How will the choral director know when he has found music that will call for the best that is in him and his students? Only through sincere study and an uncompromising love of ideal. He may say, “I will be content to present a program as it is, but the music should be studied and music well produced will bring to the students a realization of the beauty.” This is indeed true. But in the long run he will develop to a higher plane if he keeps this ideal always before him.

With the relative sentiment of musical background and an urge to sing and to reach the goals that have become the artistic property of the choral director, the whole problem of repertoire selection must be treated in a manner different than now. He will no longer say, “I have a fine choir, what music shall I get for them?” Instead, he will say, “There is such a great fund of music that must be given to our students and the teacher will get together and sing.” He will then gather singers and schools. He will not choose merely the agreeable music of the moment; rather he will construct a program of music based on the great choral literature of the ages — old and new — so that the students will begin to have a real sense of perspective regarding musical values and the religious content of this music.

The Band as a Medium for Symphonic Accompaniment

by Arthur H. Christmann

Technician, Fifth Grade

Arthur Christmann was born in New York City of a long line of musicians, his father having been a member of the New York Symphony and New York Philharmonic Orchestras. His musical education began with the piano at the age of five, and later he studied at the Institute of Musical Art and the Juilliard Graduate School of Music, taking his B.S. and M.S. degrees at Columbia University. At the Juilliard Graduate School he was the possessor of a fellowship in the Conducting Class under the late Albert Stoessel, and for three years the Institute of Musical Art he received the Artist’s Diploma as well as the annual Morris Loeb prize. From 1924 to 1934 he served as instructor in the Brooklyn schools and as assistant conductor of the Brooklyn Symphony Orchestra. Since 1934 he has been on the faculty of the Institute of Musical Art of the Juilliard School of Music. He has conducted numerous orchestral and choral works, and his practical experience in arranging for bands and orchestras has given him opportunity to work on many occasions as a conductor and arranger in New York City. At present Mr. Christmann is in charge of classes in symphonic band arranging at the Institute of Musical Art and is also serving as a technician in the U.S.M.A. Band at West Point. — Editor’s Note.

SERIOUS band musicians are endeavoring, in every way possible, to increase the dignity of their medium, to widen the scope and literature of the symphonic band movement, so that the programs of this type of ensemble may compare in musical value with those of any other type of symphonic organization. It was with this purpose that the following experimental work was carried on in the symphonic band and in the classes in brass and woodwind ensembles at the Institute of Musical Art of the Juilliard School of Music.

The basic hypothesis worked upon was the idea that a combination of instruments could be found, within the limits of the symphonic band, which could directly represent the string choir of the orchestra. Obviously the clarinet section suggested itself here, as did the bassoon section, since the bassoon in the modern symphonic orchestra gives such great care to the body of the orchestra.

In support of the conviction that the clarinet section in itself could serve as a complete wind orchestra, corresponding to the string orchestra, was the conviction which had been achieved by the studies that were being done at the Institute of Musical Art. In those classes a clarinet ensemble had been developed which played music with full string parts, and in most cases played it directly from the original string parts. In a few instances treated from the original string parts, band instruments were used as written, thereby transposing the concert key of the piece down one whole tone, or transposed the piece in its correct concert key.

The Clarinet Ensemble

The clarinet ensemble, as it was set up in these classes, consisted of B-flat clarinets, bass clarinets, and a contrabass clarinet. The first violin, second violin, and viola parts were taken by the B-flat soprano clarinets; the bass parts by bass clarinets and contrabass clarinet, respectively. The alto clarinet and cornet were not used, although there is no reason why an organization with sufficient numbers might not add the alto to the viola line. The clarinet is, of course, an instrument with a particular tonal quality and it is not always possible to obtain the same sonority as the string choir. Therefore the pieces selected for the ensemble had to be carefully chosen.

In order to create an effective tonal balance, parts were assigned so that the more lyric and melodic material would fall to the soprano clarinets, while the inner voices and the continuo lines were reinforced by the bass clarinets. The greater rhythmic and harmonic support could be given by supplementary wind and brass as found necessary in certain passages.

The technical requirements for the clarinet players were strict: fine tone, steady legato, and accurate intonation. The players had to be capable of expressing the phrasing that is characteristic of string playing — long, singing lines, and light, articulate figures. In practice it was frequently necessary to have players practice in small groups so that the ensemble could secure the necessary phrasing and balance.

To insure sufficient practice in it, the viola part, on the other hand, is quite another story and requires special treatment. If the piece was to be rendered in the original key, the B-flat players transpose their own parts up one tone; then this would be most practical to use for the bass clef for the B-flat clarinet, with the proper correction for accidentals, of course. Actually the players read bass clefs most accurately in their hands; that is, the middle C of the viola was fingered like [symbol] on the bass clarinet, with the thumb and first two fingers of the left hand. It was found advisable, therefore, to place on the viola part those students who were already the most fluent in their reading of the bass clef. In cases where the B-flat soprano clarinets were reading their own parts exactly at the pitch written, thus transposing the piece one tone lower in concert key, it was found most advisable to write out the viola part; otherwise the student who could not read viola clef would be forced to read it as treble clef, transposing one tone higher and an octave lower and make the proper correction for accidentals, a process which would be just a little too complicated to be comfortable for anybody concerned!

A Slight Drawback

Using B-flat soprano clarinets for the viola part has but one slight drawback. It will be noted that the clarinet lacks the low sonorities of the viola, the C and C-sharp, concert. It is true, however, that these notes occur very infrequently, and when they did it was always possible to make a slight alteration or adaptation which never destroyed the integrity of the composition. This problem should be overcome with very little difficulty, for instance, in a solo for the viola which would be inspired by any change of register or notes, it would indeed be a time when the use of some B-flat alto clarinets in this section would prove to be a great boon. A few B-flat clarinets with the low E-flat and a few A clarinets in the section, especially if one or all happened to have the low E-flat and the low C-sharp, would completely cover the range of the viola. In this case the players having the E-flat might double the first or second viola parts as required by the score and thus fulfill their obligation.

Out of all this clarinet ensemble work grew the idea of experimenting with symphonic arrangements for practical public performance. The first arrangement attempted was a concerto by Mozart, the Concerto in E-flat, K. 417, by Mozart. Mozart has scored for the traditional two horns and two oboes. These could well have been rendered in their original instrumentation, but it was decided, as a rule, for the first performance was to take place in the large Juilliard concert hall, and the resources of the band were considered adequate.

For the Larger Concert Hall

The significant adjustment which performance in a larger concert hall made necessary was addition to the bass line, which was found to be insufficient when carried by the two bass clarinets and contrabass clarinets alone. A tuba was added to the 16' bass and a baritone to the regular 8' bass. Later, however, the baritone was replaced by a baritone saxophone. Strangely enough, although it would have been difficult to foresee this, the baritone did not blend with the reeds, although the tuba served its purpose admirably. Its broad but unobtrusive tone gave the ensemble just that breadth and symphonic richness which it lacked.

In later performances and experiments it was found that almost any bass instrument with a blending and unobtrusive tone could be used to reinforce the bass and contrabass lines. At various times bassoons and contrabass viols were added, and all served the purpose splendidly. The chief requirement is that the bass line be built up in volume so that it has parity with the upper lines, and that it have sufficient weight and breadth to enrich the entire structure. The mere presence in the ensemble of an instrument of the 16' pitch is some guarantee that the latter requirement will not be entirely overlooked, but it was found in our case that the presence of one tuba, and probably of not more than one, was a sine qua non.

Carrying out the same principle, theoretically it would be possible to add other instruments to the other lines if desired, provided always that no one line become over-prominent and that no instrument with a strident or over-reedy tone be added. Such instruments will stand out individually and will never blend. Saxophones, E-flat alto and B-flat tenor, could, for instance, be used to reinforce the second violin and viola lines; but there is grave danger here, since every symphonic band leader knows how few saxophones are played with sufficient blending quality to fit into a symphonic ensemble at all. Flutes can form a very good addition to the first violin line, especially if there are any passages which lie especially high for the clarinets. However, if there are too many of these high passages, that particular concerto had best be avoided for purposes of this treatment. In our work at the Institute of Musical Art of the Juilliard School of Music we always kept the three upper lines pure clarinet tone. Experiments were often tried with the reeds, however. One of these was the addition of flutes to the upper line, as noted above. The result was favorable, but the device was never used at a public performance.

In concert accompaniment, a prime requisite is that the instruments doing the accompaniment do not cover the soloist. This should be especially remembered when dealing with the less transparent timbres of wind instruments, and in this type of adaptation the conductor would never be forgiven if, in rendering the accompaniment on wind instruments, he "showed under" the soloist. This requirement would favor a small but competent ensemble. At a subsequent performance of the Bach "D-minor Piano Concerto," only the very best clarinetists in the school were used, and only two players were placed on each of the upper three parts. The bass was kept in proportion. These selected players had such highly developed embouchure control that, when occasion demanded, they could render a tone so soft that the most delicate nuances of the piano soloist could be distinctly heard. There is no reason why, if the conductor is sufficiently demanding, an ensemble consisting largely of competent clarinets cannot render one of the softest sound textures conceivable, for the instrument is noted for its ability to do this, and many instrumentalists treat it as an eloquent testimony to this characteristic. At no point in the Bach "D-minor Piano Concerto" did the soloist have to force his tone in order to come through, and at no time did the ensemble cover him.

Some Limitations

There are, of course, many limitations in this type of adaptation. In the first place, only a limited number of concertos are adaptable for this type of work. They must be in certain keys. On the whole, only works in simple keys, and perhaps the very simplest sharp keys, ought to be considered. In this restriction the small ensemble is not much worse off than the entire band, which is always more comfortable in flat keys. However, in the small ensemble there is so much more of the transparency of chamber music that any passagework muddled because of the presence in the signature of too many sharps (or flats) will stand out in ugly nakedness.

Closely related to the consideration of key is that of the style of writing for the original strings. The conductor will do well to stay away from any concerto in which there is a predominance of writing which is strictly idiomatic for strings and which cannot possibly be made to "come off well" on reduced instruments. This category also includes passages which, in range, lie well outside the effective upper limits of the clarinet. An occasional passage may indeed be changed in some minor way to suit the word—higher octave, but this privilege certainly should not be abused.

Closely related to this question is that of the whole general style of the piece selected. The method of adaptation which is the subject of this article is not at all suited to the more brilliant, modern concertos. If these are to be played, it would be far better to make arrangements for the full band. Its brilliancy and resources are needed here. As a matter of fact, full band accompaniment for solos and concertos are nothing new. Those who have heard the great wind of the United States Military Academy Band at West Point will recall with pleasure the many excellent symphonic band transcriptions of concertos which have been made for the distinguished artists who have appeared with this organization by its able director, Captain Francis E. Resta. This technique of direct adaptation of the accompaniment is far better suited to the older concerti, in which the strings form the main, if not the only, body of accompaniment, in which the woodwinds are used conservatively, if at all, and in which there is no heavy brass. Such composers as Bach, Handel, Mozart, Haydn, and their contemporaries are best for this treatment.

Finally, many will object to the monotony of color which is inevitable with such a small selection of wind instruments. The validity of this criticism cannot be denied, and the only answer possible is that this combination has practically the same relationship to the symphonic band as the string orchestra has to the full orchestra. Lacking all of the contrast of the full orchestra, the string choir still possesses a milder beauty and a charm of its own, and achieves some variety within itself. The same may be said for our wind-accompanying ensemble. Lacking the variety of the full band and even the flexibility and transparency of the string orchestra, the playing of an ensemble such as this, if clean, intelligent, sensitive, and eloquent, will still have much to recommend it, and will be always of such value to the small ensemble lovers of music who will see in it one device for extending the scope of symphonic band literature. In addition, one must not overlook the fact that this simple and direct technique of adaptation would actually make available many more concertos for all types of instruments on symphonic band programs, a circumstance which, in itself, would add immeasurably to the variety of the programs.

With all these limitations just discussed, our feeling is that there is still much to be said on the positive side. The First Year
How It Can be Made Interesting for the Young Student
by Harold Berkley

Today it is realized, as never before, that the first five years of a child's life are of extreme importance to his later development, that the influences and environment surrounding him during this period tend to form habits, reactions, and thought-processes which remain, with more or less modification, throughout mature life. The first year of music study bears relationship to a child's later musical development — a fact which some teachers and far too many parents take little account. This lack of perception is one of the main reasons why so many children — estimated as high as fifty per cent — give up studying before they have passed the elementary stages.

A witty Frenchman once said that a child's first exercises are its parents. The idea may at first seem fantastic, but nevertheless it calls for some thought; in the field of music, and especially of violin study, there is more than a grain of truth in it — though the parents are certainly not wholly to blame for failures. No doctor, in our experience, is ever free from the habit of giving his patient something to do, and so it is with the parents. Young children, if left to themselves and not given any definite plan to follow, will generally from their own initiative fall back into the easiest, most natural way of doing things, and this will not always be desirable. A teacher, therefore, who wishes to be successful with young children must keep constantly in mind that the home life will almost inevitably affect the child's interest in his lessons. If the home is not cooperative, he will do well to instruct the parents in the necessity of their co-operation, and let them know how they can help, and why they cannot do the work of the teacher.

It is my conviction that almost anything can be taught to a child of five if he be trained in such a way that the training does not make it tiring or unpleasant. In other words, the aim should be to coax the child along, to help him to do the right thing by making it seem natural and interesting rather than by forcing him by sheer discipline. This is one of the most difficult arts in teaching.

My article is based primarily on violin teaching, but most of the principles apply equally well to other instruments.

Responsibilities of the Teacher

So much for the responsibilities of the parents. Let us look at those of the teacher, and examine the means by which he may best carry them out.

Certainly, the main responsibility of the teacher is to develop the best of the child's artistic and musical sense, and gradually engender a love and understanding of music. How this may best be done must vary with each child, for each child's fundamental make-up, temperament and the amount of training, of which every individual will profit in some degree or other, will differ widely. With beginners it is, of course, necessary to direct the child's attention to the essentials and to teach him rhythmic combinations — such as 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4.

The means by which these essentials can be taught are many and various; each teacher will have his favorite approach. What is important, however, is that the teacher avoid committing himself to the use of any one method; every child has a mental make-up of his own and the teacher must be prepared to individualize each lesson, not in the details, but in major points.

The aim should be to give the pupil the sense of accomplishment and of progress; to encourage him by praise and to avoid over-critical comment. The teacher should be tactful, avoiding unnecessary emphasis on the mistakes. It is better to let the pupil learn at his own pace, and to shape lessons so that they are neither too easy nor too hard.

With very young children, the teaching of elementary solfège should be made into some sort of a game, for this is the easiest way to hold the child's attention; however, as soon as interest has been awakened, the game element should be gradually eliminated and the musical demands increased. As soon as possible the pupil should be encouraged to think of musical sentences as a whole and to understand melodic lines as such, not merely as finger patterns.

Tone-quality should be emphasized from the very beginning. The pupil should be taught the natural way of producing a full tone; he should be shown how to use the weight of the arm and swing of the wrist in bowing, and to avoid squeezing or pressing with the fingers. The pupil should be made conscious of the difference between legato, staccato and portato, and should be trained to play with purity and clarity. The preliminary tone exercises should include open-string bowing, long tones on single notes and simple scales.

When the pupil has mastered these elements, harmony and simple ensemble playing should be introduced. Duets are particularly useful at this stage, for they give the pupil an opportunity to play with another musician and to learn ensemble skills. Sight-reading should be part of every lesson, and the teacher should select easy pieces that will give the pupil pleasure as well as musical training.

It is vital to develop the pupil's sense of rhythm and pulse. The teacher should use clapping, rhythm games and actual rhythmic dictation to cultivate these skills. The child's memory can be trained by the regular practice of short, simple pieces that are repeated until they can be played correctly and musically.

Careful attention should be given to posture and instrument set-up. The violin should be adjusted to the size and comfort of the child; chin-rests and shoulder rests should be used as needed to allow the child to hold the instrument without strain. The teacher should check the child's posture frequently and correct slouching or strained positions.

If the child is to progress, regular practice must be insisted upon. The teacher should set reasonable practice goals and communicate these clearly to the parents. Short, daily practice periods are much more effective than long, infrequent sessions. The teacher should give the pupil the tools to practice intelligently, by assigning specific technical tasks and musical goals for each practice session.

The long-term aim of the first year is to lay a solid foundation upon which the later years may build. If the teacher concentrates on tone, rhythm, simple technique, basic reading and the cultivation of musical taste, the pupil will have a head start in all succeeding work.

It must be remembered that the first year need not be a dreary grind of exercises. On the contrary, it should be a time of discovery, excitement and reward. The child should be led to feel that music is something alive and worth pursuing. If this is done, the chances are that the child will continue to study and make music a permanent part of his life.

From Kreisler or Heifetzes, the teacher should insist that from two to four months — depending on the quickness and natural ability of the child — be spent on preliminary training in the rudiments of music, and on elementary ear-training. This will make the task of the violin teacher much easier, and the early violinistic difficulties of the pupil will be lessened. Teacher will be able to give his mind to playing the violin without at the same time having to learn notes and intervals.

The preliminary work should be done at the piano. The child may be taught to recognize the tones on the keyboard and to play and sing them before being shown the position of the notes on the staff. The early training on the piano will often make the subsequent violin instruction much easier, and at the same time will help develop the child's ear and sense of rhythm.

Tone, the many ways in which these essentials can be taught are many and various; each teacher will have his favorite approach. What is important, however, is that the teacher avoid committing himself to the use of any one method; every child has a mental make-up of his own and the teacher must be prepared to individualize each lesson, not in the details, but in major points.

The Game Element

With very young children, the teaching of elementary solfège should be made into some sort of a game, for this is the easiest way to hold a child's attention; however, as soon as interest has been awakened, the game element should be gradually eliminated and more serious musical demands should be made. The teacher should not force new technical matter until the child is physically ready, and even then the pace should be adjusted to the individual. It is not necessary that the child be rushed.

When the time comes to begin lessons on the violin, the teacher must decide for himself a question which may seem somewhat revolutionary: Should this pupil be started in the first position—or the third?

All teachers know the difficulty most children find in attaining a correct shaping of the hand in the first position, a difficulty greatly increased if the child has short arms and fingers. It is, indeed, a highly unnatural shape for the hand and arm to take, and both have to be gradually trained to it. Discussing the problem some years ago with a group of my students who were teaching beginners, I suggested that they start all short-armed pupils in the third position. This they did, and favorable results were so immediate that the question arose whether it would not be advisable to start all beginners in the third position. Experience has proved that many difficulties and lost hours can be avoided if this is done.

So far as I know, there is not in print a book of third-position exercises suitable for the pupil who is just beginning to play. However, with the aid of manuscript paper and a little thought, no teacher should have difficulty in producing exercises sufficient for the needs of his pupils. The preliminary open-string exercises can be studied from the violin method the teacher intends to use, and while studying these the pupil should be encouraged to rest his left hand at the shoulder of the violin. This angle of the arm and hand brings about fairly naturally the shaping necessary for the third position.

First Position After Third

Practice in the first position may begin as soon as the student is able to play (in the third position) quarter-notes on all four strings with a nicely rounded hand; also—this is important—when he can play, slowly, with his first, second, and third fingers while holding the fourth finger on the next lower string. The value of this exercise lies in the fact that it trains the hand to take the extra turning necessary in the first position. To start with, the transition from the third position to the first should be made with the fourth finger, so that the note arrived at may be tested at the unison; or with the third finger, when it may be tested at the octave.

One of the best means of encouraging a child's interest in violin playing is to make him aware of his tone. Practically all violinists—even young ones—find tone the most personally interesting element of their playing, and the teacher who can develop this interest in his pupils is not likely to find them dropping out for lack of incentive. The manner of finding out good intonation must always be kept before the student, of course, but the pupil should be trained to realize that the note which is played in turn with a poor tone is just as much a failure as a note played out of tune with a good tone. For this reason, the teacher should strive to develop and improve the tone as soon as a steady bow can be drawn. For the same reason, he should make it his business to use the best tone at his command whenever he demonstrates for the pupil: a child's ear is sensitive, and the influence of the teacher's tone can have a profound effect. The development of a good tone carries with it the necessity for a sensitive and relaxed bow technic, for clumsy bowing will rarely if ever produce a pleasing tone quality. Unfortunately, the teaching of bowing is often neglected, after the first few months, in the interests of left-hand technic. This is a pity, for if more care were taken with it, there would be more satisfied and interested violin students.

The material a teacher uses is an important factor in determining the attitude of a student. He should, therefore, have a wide acquaintance with the teaching material that is available. It is by no means a good plan to use the same set of books for all young pupils. A child who is sensitive and self-conscious—the two qualities of life go together—may at first make quite slow progress, and if he finds he is not going through his book as rapidly as another (possibly less sensitive) pupil is progressing in the same book, he is likely, in subconscious self-defense, to lose interest in the violin. In such a case, the teacher would be wise to avoid using the same book for both pupils. Competition between pupils is often a great asset to the teacher, but there are times when it becomes a liability.

Suggested Material

For general purposes, the best violin method is probably that of Nicholas Lauterbach (?). It presents each new problem in its simplest manner, the several instructions are clearly expressed; it is well graded, and the exercises and studies are strictly adapted to the child's capacity. The technical portion of the first volume indicates a rather old-fashioned method of holding the violin and bow, and may well be removed before the pupil begins to use the book. For the little children, the "Very First Violin Book" by Rob Roy Peery, Maud Byng's "Violin Method," and "Violin Method" by Ann Hutson (?) may be strongly recommended. The graded Violin Lessons by Louis Bostelmann give the pupil a charming series of exercises from the beginning—which is interesting for the child and a spur to his musicanship.

As soon as the pupil has progressed sufficiently, he should be given a book of specialized studies. The "50 Studies" by Wohlfahrt are, of course, well known and invaluable, but many pupils find them dry and uninteresting. Should this be the case, the teacher may well substitute the "26 Melodious Studies" by Josephine Trott, or the "Studies" by Sitt, which are equally musical and are excellent for tone production and bowing practice.

Books of elementary pieces are legion, and a particular favorite with many teachers is the "European School" series, which offers a graded sequence of easy pieces from various authors. Mention should be made, however, of the "Folk and Master Melodies" by Wesley Sargent; they are excellent reading pieces, and every piece is full of genuine musical worth. The latter point is of great importance in developing a pupil's love of music, for a child often has instinctively good taste and quickly detects the cheap or meretricious.

Ensemble playing is another ready means of awakening interest, and should be introduced as early as possible. An excellent ensemble book for beginners—the duets start with the open strings—is Louis Bostelmann's "Graded Ensemble." Teaching beginners can be, and often is, a drudgery and a chore; on the other hand, it can be a series of interesting explorations. The occasional lull pupil who cannot learn is extremely trying; there are, however, few youngsters who are really unable to study who are so unmusical that nothing can be made of them. And finding the right approach to each budding individuality that comes for lessons is, or should be, fascinating work. If the teacher has within him something of the enthusiasm of the gardener and the explorer he will always enjoy teaching, and his pupils will enjoy studying with him. Furthermore, he will have the satisfaction of knowing that through him the love and understanding of music is being brought to many who will later pass it on to others. To accomplish this is surely the mark of a successful life in music.

The Trail of the Tempered Scale

by Arthur S. Garbett

THE PATH of the tempered scale is a long, long trail. It winds out of the mists of antiquity and enters Europe from the Middle East. It passes through the glory that was Greece and the grandeur that was Rome. It is lost in the twilight zone of the Dark Ages, emerges again in the Middle Ages, widens out in the Renaissance, and finally becomes a broad highway after Bach demonstrated its worth with his "Forty-eight Preludes and Fugues for the Well-Tempered Clavichord."

A tempered scale is a tiny ladder of notes which modifies the "scale of nature," the pure tones. Our diatonic scale has two small steps or semitones in among the whole tones, but if turned true to nature the whole tones are vary in width. The chromatic scale has twelve half-steps, but these, too, are uneven unless equalized or "tempered." There are other temperings than ours. There are Arabian, Persian, and scales more remote which have smaller steps and more of them; scales with wider and fewer steps, carefully graded by mathematical calculation; and some, such as the five-toned pentatonic, which are but roughhewn. Hubert Parry says that, while the pentatonic is universally found, its steps differ somewhat in width in every region where it prevails.

The trail of the tempered scale reveals an age-old conflict between voices and instruments; between the human tendency to sing from a high note down and the overtones of nature which rise up from a root or "fundamental."

A Long Misunderstanding

The voice is a glider. It soars or dives between high tones and low, with no fixed ceiling and no permanent landing place. It has no "keynote" of its own from which the "steps" of the common chord arise, as from a bugle, requiring smaller steps between. A scale is a graded howl, and the story of the tempered scale tells how it became graded.

The voice also wobbles and quavers in modulation and inflections of undetermined pitch, only mechanically approximated by our semitones and quarter tones. Intuitively, the voice drops about a fourth, as from C to G, at the end of a sentence. In his "Evolution of the Art of Music," Parry shows how the voice wavered and quavered about this lower tone in fluctuations, as wide as a whole tone about it, or in narrow wavers more faintly suggested by A-flat and F-sharp. Its fluctuations rose above the C, also, perhaps up to E. Out of that grew the pentatonic scale, running down: E, D, C, A, G.

Most primitive scales run downward. So do cultivated scales, and we know, after long centuries of misunderstanding, that the ancient Greek modes were song descending. When Bishop Ambrose of Milan tried to revive them in the fourth Christian century, he had them ascending. The resultant misunderstanding lasted until within the last half century, or less.

The discovery and tempering of scale steps has been the province of instruments, not voices. The trumpet may have revealed the common chord; but the measurement of the steps by mathematician or artist is derived from the harps: the behavior of strings, short or long, thick or thin, at various tensions. Thick strings are heavier and vibrate more slowly than thin ones.

Thousands of scholars have contributed to the forming and tempering of the scale, but four names give us the turning points of its history: Pythagoras, the Greek (582 B.C.); Claudius Ptolemy, the Alexandrian (second century); Giuseppe Zarlino (1517-1590); and Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750).

Pythagoras made himself a monochord, a one-stringed harp with a movable bridge. He found that half the string-length, pitched, say at E, produced the octave of the whole; two-thirds produced a perfect fourth, E-A, and three-quarters gave a perfect fifth, E-B. Beyond that he did not go, because of a comma or "gap," a little quirk in the scale of ratios that may be better explained after we have met Claudius Ptolemy. The Comma of Pythagoras is what causes the need for tempering of the scale.

This Greek, however, bequeathed a pretty problem to his disciples. If two-thirds of a string produce a perfect fourth, and three-fourths a perfect fifth, what will the difference between two-thirds and three-fourths, or one-twelfth, produce? Call it a "tone." Can you make an even scale of such whole tones between the octave? You cannot. Our own whole-tone scale is possible only if the scale is tempered. Even then, the scale has no perfect fourth or perfect fifth. The vital E-B-F# is omitted. The Greeks eventually figured out of these notes two descending tetrachords and four notes each, with E-Eb and A-E as the notes of each. For the middle strings they worked out three ways of tuning called genera: the chromatic, enharmonic, and diatonic. The first two matter little. The chromatic crowded two half-steps immediately above the lower notes of the tetrachords, the notes B and A, making E-C-B-A; E-B-F#-E. The enharmonic dropped the sharps, and split the C and F each into two quarter tones. This was evidently in deference to the afore-mentioned natural drop of the voice and its quavering about the lower tone. Incidentally, they named these strings not according to pitch, but according to the way the lyre was held — with the tone lowest in pitch farthest away. Thus the lowest note became the highest, and vice versa, to the confusion of good Bishop Ambrose.

The diatonic form was like our white notes on the piano; the descending Dorian diatonic Mode running E-D-C-B: A-G-F-E, with two half-steps at the end of each tetrachord. Our ascending major mode reverses this: C-D-E-F, G-A-B-C. From the Dorian Mode, the Greeks finally devised a two-octave scale as of A-Minor. This they called the "perfect System" and from it all their modes were derived, as the church modes were later. It had no half-steps, but a later Dorian Mode descending from D instead of A, demanded a B-flat: D-C-B-flat-A; G-F-E-D. This, ascending, became the first of the four Ambrosians modes, the others beginning on E, F, G.

If Pythagoras taught us to derive tetrachords by measuring string-lengths, Claudius Ptolemy first described in full the bugle-tones or "scale of nature" revealed in the harmonics of a single string. The whole string is tuned to C, the harmonics series is as follows:

We are not here concerned with mathematics, however, but with the musical facts revealed by Claudius Ptolemy. Namely, that if a scale is formed in steps derived from the "scale of nature," its intervals are not in accord with ours. We measure intervals in scale-steps, as unisons, seconds, thirds, fourths. We modify these into major, minor, augmented, or diminished, by the addition or subtraction of half-steps. A comma is simply a separation, a "gap."

About the C Clef

Q. Would you please tell me about the different clefs? I was taught only the C clef and I would like to know about the others. — M. R.

A. You are probably thinking in terms of playing the piano or organ, so I will tell you at once that in playing keyboard instruments you need to know only the F and G clefs. However, there is also the C clef, which is used in the case of certain orchestral instruments and which is still to be found in much old church music. The clef marks Middle C, just as the G clef marks the G above, and the F clef marks the F below. The difference is that the staff on which the C clef is placed does not have to be on the same line at different times — at least it appears so. Actually the C clef is always on the same line (Middle C) and it is the number of lines above or below this line which varies. The Child Who "Hates" Music
by Kathryn Sanders Rieder

CONFRONTED by a child who dislikes music, the average music lover is perplexed, baffled, and a little exasperated at so unnatural a condition. Too many times such children are shrugged aside with the feeling that nothing can be done for them. We have, too, the child who likes music but who has no inclination to apply himself in learning it. He hates to practice. What can be done for such children, and what is the condition which occurs rather frequently? It comes as a surprise, perhaps, that many of the great masters disliked music at first. We know that Beethoven hated music until he was about eleven years old. His father, quick to see that the child had talent, drove him to his practice in order that he might earn money from playing. The one thing that would have made life tolerable—playing tunes of his own—was also forbidden. When his father heard him improvising on his violin, he told him roughly to "scrape the notes" or take his punishment.

Yet we recall how miraculously Beethoven changed when he went to Neefe for lessons. This good musician taught him to love music, to love working at it, and he encouraged Beethoven to compose. No matter where we find excellence in a musician we find in that life a friend who held him to his best efforts. We know that Liszt as a boy did not care for music but that he learned to love it through his study, making remarkable progress. Even Chopin at first showed an aversion to the piano, until lessons with an excellent teacher cured him of this.

To be unable to appreciate a great art shows serious lack. The art is not on trial, but we are. We should be earnestly concerned about such deficiency and eager to correct the unfortunate situation.

Music and the Battle of Life

Why Music Gives Us Courage

by Doron K. Antrim

So many people now are employing music to fortify themselves against the most terrible of tribulations brought about by world conditions that a survey of its application in special cases is of real present interest.—Editor’s Note.

A WOMAN who read one of my articles called "Wake Up Singing," in the American Magazine, wrote me that a song saved her from suicide. Her husband had been killed suddenly, leaving her with four small children to support. "There were days," she wrote, "when the ache in my heart seemed more than I could bear. I longed to go to sleep and never wake up. On one of these days this feeling was so strong that I feared I might give in to it. In desperation I turned on the radio. Someone was singing My Creed. It brought back a flood of memories. I had sung it in school when fourteen years old and always liked it. The song did something to me. I sang it frequently after that, sometimes having to choke back sobs to do it. But it helped me, probably more than anything else I did, and gave me strength and courage to go on."

Indescribable incidents of the power of music could be given. We recall that in the last war, Nurse Edith Cavell faced a firing squad without flinching. She had prepared herself to meet this ordeal by prayer and song. Her favorite hymn, Abide With Me, was on her lips till the end. In the Battle of the Marne the lines of a brigade of the Ninth Army were breaking in on a retreating line. A Union soldier started singing "The Battle Hymn of the Republic." Soon the entire regiment joined in. The lines stiffened and held. More incidents could be recited; that of survivors clinging to a sinking seaplane and singing to keep up their strength until rescued; of the lad who whistles while passing the graveyard at night. No doubt, you recall some from your own experience. Probably the greatest gift that the priceless heritage of music has to offer us is courage.

Music gives us hope to replace hopelessness. I am reminded of the famous pictures of that name: a girl striding along a stricken world, stumbling a harp and bending low to catch its feeble notes. Without music, hope would indeed be gone for many. From the bomb shelters of London, human beings from tiny tots to very aged men and women found music invaluable.

Music also gives us fortitude to face the "stings and arrows" of life. England especially was made aware of this in wartime. When the war started, England banned all military bands, feeling that the men were needed to carry guns. The idea still persists that music in camps and on the front is a light, even sentimental, thing and not a life saver. But the men found that a bit of music now and then was a tonic. England's wartime music bands, which had been disbanded, were reconstituted by popular demand. There was a resurgence of interest in music, and the British Broadcasting Company reports "a great revival of interest in music," a return to the classics; and "a greater realization of the riches of the national heritage in music and the innate suitability to the rank and file of the British people."

Why does music give us courage? The reasons are physiological and psychological. It has been found to quicken and steady the pulse beat, to induce deeper and more rhythmic breathing, to influence the internal glands. Psychologically, it substitutes hope for discouragement and despair. But one of the chief reasons is that composers invariably put courage into their music. Search through the works of the great composers and you find few that reflect a negative, pessimistic attitude toward life, comparable to the writings of Nietzsche and Schopenhauer in literature. True, most of the classic composers were never too happy. They had their share of money troubles and poor health. One thinks especially of Beethoven, Schumann, Chopin, Rachmaninoff. But their works do not give over unresignedly to suffering and despair—rather they show a triumph of the spirit over the flesh.

Music and Courage

Beethoven is an especially good example. For twenty-five years of his life he was afflicted with deafness, which is, about the worst calamity that can befall a musician. Beethoven tried many cures in vain. He wrote to a friend, "I must live like an exile." If I approach near to people, a hot terror seizes me, a fear that I may be subjected to the danger of having my condition observed." And again later on, "I hope—I must wholly abandon." How could a man who had abandoned hope write such glorious music? Beethoven did his greatest work during the years of his deafness. He never heard a tone of the Ninth Symphony; closing with its Ode to Joy, still, one always gets a great lift out of this music. It is a complete triumph of the soul over the body.

Handel wrote "The Messiah" when his right hand was paralyzed and his money gone. Creditor were hounding him, threatening to put him in jail. Did he give in to doubt, despair, and discouragement? If he had, our music would never have experienced to one of the most hopeful epochs in all music literature, the Hallelujah Chorus.

In the short span of his forty years, Chopin was almost never free from the scourge of tuberculosis. He was slight and frail, a walking physicality. Yet his music is not tinged with any hint of weakness. It is mostly up-curve, tempering with warmth, affirmative in its declaration that life is worth while.

Prelude in A Minor, Op. 28, No. 2
by Frederic Chopin

HERE'S an effective trick to play on your musical friends, especially those who pride themselves on their ability to recognize the "style" of any great composer the moment they hear one of his compositions. Play this strange prelude to them without offering a hint concerning its origin. Then let them guess who wrote it. After a long pause, someone will hazard "Prokofieff" or "Stravinsky." Someone else will offer "Rachmaninoff" or "Scriabin." After much hemming and hawing they will finally confess themselves stumped. Then it's great fun seeing them squirm when you say "Chopin."

Yes, for Chopin this second prelude is an astonishing and baffling piece. It has confused every one of those "romantic" commentators on Chopin's music (and of course by the redoubtable Huneker) who for a hundred years have gone into transports of despair over it. They call it "soulful and sinister," "desperate and exasperating," and claim that in it are concentrated Chopin's "morbid" and "brooding" impulses — his "fatalistic" and "mysterious" impulses. After all, this music seems to epitomize sadness. The melancholy, the horror, the passion — they rise like an irresistible tide. It has been called "the funeral prelude," "a little funeral," "a tiny death-song." Yet, strangely enough, the composer's handling is so perfectly proportioned and so exquisitely finished that the piece may be regarded as the final word in artistic inevitability.

The piece is a bitter pill of powerful concentration. Regarding it unemotionally, the pianist finds it an effective study in dissonances, a fascinating example of the piece "without a key" — its tonality remains vague to the ear — a curious exhibit in melodic expansion and contraction, and a good stretching exercise for the left hand. Small hands tackling the piece will probably have to divide the left hand part into two hands wherever practicable, somewhat like this:

[Small musical example]

At best it is almost impossible to play this left hand strictly legato; so to avoid tenseness I advise bringing the damper pedal to the rescue whenever necessary.

Note particularly the Alto Berce (or meter). With two gentle "swings" in every measure, Chopin eliminates the deadly, dragging, four-four tempo which, persisted in, ruins the continuity of the piece. Play the melody with exaggerated emphasis and with large, free arm movement. Each time it appears it must ring forth like a deep fateful pronouncement of impending doom. This bell-like sonority can be much enhanced by careful pedaling. In the measures 6, 11, 18 and 25 the clouded, distantly jangling effect of the left hand is achieved by an occasional long-held damper pedal, as indicated.

Dragging the tempo is avoided by a gentle, full-arm stress on the left hand on first and third beats of the measure. Hold the vitality of the tone right through to the dominant seventh chord in the second to last measure. Then roll the final A minor chord slowly, heavily, and ominously.

Opportunities for the American Composer
(Continued from Page 720)

Strangely enough, it was this very success as a song-writer which did the most to hold me back when I endeavored to secure the performances of larger works. The first major work I attempted was an American Indian opera in three acts, a pioneer effort. This was "Dooma," as yet unproduced, though an aria from it has won a prize. Mrs. Eberhart and I worked on it for six years. The aria was furnished by Francis La Flesche, the Omaha Indian ethnologist. It contains many genuine American Indian tunes, some to be sung just as we received them from their primitive sources.

Because I was a successful song writer, a few unkind critics began to say that I was incapable of doing any other form of music. This naturally worked against me when the opera was submitted for production. "Dooma" was definitely turned down. The formal reason given was that racial subjects were not acceptable, though American subjects were welcome.

A New Era Begins
At the time of the last World War there was a patriotic revival during which people suddenly became conscious of the American composer and wanted to hear more of his music. People who formerly had ignored him, were now anxious to court him. Many of these, however, promptly forgot him after the war was over and returned to praising exclusively the music of foreigners. About this time Mr. Gatti-Casazza of the Metropolitan Opera House asked A. Walter Kramer if he knew of any American opera they could produce. Mr. Kramer wired me and, fortunately, Mrs. Eberhart and I had just completed what was intended to be a chamber opera. This was, in a sense, an echo from the past, for in our first "Ice-breaking" song, the Tryst (which was based on an Indian and Western idea) the new man's name was Shaenous. When we wrote our opera we decided to name the heroine Shaenous. It was an auspicious name, for to our immense surprise, the Metropolitan accepted the opera. It was produced in 1918 with the American cast on which I had insisted. The audience gave it a fine reception and critics were enthusiastic. There were more than a score of curtain calls.

About 1914 I wrote my first chamber music; a "Trio in D Major." I claim that the third movement, in which I used idealized ragtime, was the first inclusion of such rhythms into American chamber music. From then on I wrote a great deal of chamber and orchestral music.

You ask whether conditions have changed for the American composer since I began to write. I find that I still have the same things to contend with. By that I mean that almost every American composer still has to fight to get his better works before the public. Foreign conductors, who rapidly become more tolerant, are still not too inclined to give us more than sporadic hearings. The situation is improving, for many conductors are adopting the healthy plan of presenting at least one American work on every program. I also boldly make the statement that our fine American conductors could do more to champion American music. It is true that they have done a great deal, yet I think they too should insist upon playing one native work on every concert. A policy of this sort, consistently followed, would react in their favor as well as in ours.

As for the concert halls, most American and European managers (and many American artists) are still not very enterprising about putting new American things into their programs. Either through indifference or lethargy or lack of courage they pass them by and "conciliate" the public by repeating the hackneyed repertory. It is only as we can educate and demonstrate to the public what really is worth listening to that we can hope for a real improvement. I am of the opinion that the radio, rather than the concert hall, will do most to popularize the American composer. When American music gets on the radio it reaches millions of people who would probably never go to hear it in a concert. The records also are a big help.

When the modern movement in music began, a number of composers turned almost exclusively to ultra-modernism in an effort to be different. This retarded the general acceptance of American music because, whether right or wrong, the public associated "American" with novelty and strangeness. Gradually a reaction set in and to-day the composer who is thoroughly musical and original, whatever his style, is welcomed.

I have been asked if I think that American composers should adapt themselves to commercial demands and write to order. My answer is an emphatic no. A composer who writes merely for popularity does not deserve help or encouragement. If he has ideas that will stand the test of time he should write them; if not, he will be forgotten.

Now I have no regular hours for composing. I feel that a composer has a duty to his correspondents and the one whom the country of a personal reply, so I try to take care of business and personal mail myself. This sometimes occupies so much time as to interfere with all creative urge. But I plan well if I find plenty of time for creative work, though I never have any set rules. Sometimes I start work in the morning, and sometimes after my office work is finished. Once in a while I work at night, though I consider night work physically wrong for creative artists. I work both at the piano and away from it. Usually I get ideas at the piano even for symphonic works, and sketch them on form for use later. After concert or symphony rehearsals of the opera or college it is the same. For example, I once did all the scoring of my first symphony "Pennsylvania" conceived at and written from the Island of St. Croix in the Virgin Islands, West Indies, at the MacDowell Colony. The Cacophony of "Noise"
Exercise: Say "ree" "ree" "ree" "wah" "ah" each time allowing the mind to attend specifically to the anatomical region peculiarly aroused by each phonetic. (Ah" as explained, must not be too much mentally particularized in the back mouth in disassociation from the other regions.) Some pause. During this pause think of and name the anatomical regions as four compartments or rooms whose characteristics sounds in their full amount may be taught as one unit, or totally give a correlated, coordinated, organized one that is complete, coherent, and musically logical and beautiful. Now with clear enunciation and control "ah" or "oh" as with the intention of fulfilling these requirements. Success means the realization of the two "waves" in correct proportion. If you succeed, you are ready to proceed with words, phrases, sentences, and the several qualities of speech used in interpretation such as the orators, mental, and musical qualities. When the trained ear of the teacher has become accustomed to the tone of each region, he is then able to hear any such characteristic out-land or, what is harder, detect its absence or diminished functioning. In other words, maximum voice is analogous to the cooperative action of the cylinders of an automobile. For one or more cylinders are inactive the car is often noisy and it will not have the full power, but when all cylinders are so related and function the car and the voice are developed.

“Indeed, there is hardly time to work out the score! This is the routine for turning out picture music. The picture itself is planned, photographed, and cut, and the release date is set. While this work goes on, the composer is given a copy of the plot scenario, from which he may derive ideas for basic thematic material. He cannot possibly begin composing, however, until the picture is done and those scenes for which music is desired are marked. When the picture is done and cut, both in its rough and final versions, the composer is called in for actual work. This moment generally occurs some three weeks before the advertised release date. Within those three weeks, the entire musical setting must be thought out, written down, arranged, orchestrated, approved, taken through its complex process of recording, and sent out in national distribution, along with the already completed film. This routine, I believe, is the best argument for solid musical craftsmanship!

The First Step

“The first step in the musical routine is viewing the finished picture. The film is run through silently, and the composer watches it. Beside him he has a pencil, a paper, and a stopwatch. The scenes needing music have been indicated to him — the producer determines where music shall come in; while the composer may suggest suitable scenes, he does not have final say about it — and his first task is to mark down the exact lengths of these scenes, in seconds. The romantic scene may need six seconds of suitable music; the cowboy scene may need ten seconds, the storm scenes, twelve seconds, and so on. In background music, a scene that runs into minutes of music is considered long.

“When the composer goes away with his time notations, and turns out suitable thematic types of exactly the desired length. When the themes are done, the producer may often rush to hear them, approving, condemning, or suggesting alternations in effect, as his taste inclines. By this time, some of the pieces three weeks have gone by. For this reason, the most competent composer may often lack the sheer physical time necessary to complete his own orchestrations. In that case, five or six experienced orchestrators are called in. The composer gives them the most general indications as to what he wants by way of orchestral color, and the orchestrators go to work — often for twenty-four hours a day.

“When the score is ready, the recording date is set, and the musical director takes over, with the composer and the orchestrators present for any possible last-minute changes. Recording is done in one of two ways. Either the silent film is projected and the musical director records the music along with it, thus securing perfect synchronization immediately; or (in cases when this cannot be accomplished technically) the recording is made alone and later fitted to the film by a process of exact timing. This is done by cuing the score in seconds and synchronizing the recording to the already-timed seconds of film scenes. It can happen that the synchronization is not perfect. Then a re-take is made, the musical director speeding up or slowing down his playing, as the case demands, to fit the film.

“When the recording is completed, the music-track is cut. The film is released through again and the music-track, already timed, is matched to the exact film of film which it must accompany. In scenes that have no music, blanks are indicated. Finally, the whole is glued together. In some cases the score goes through with the track of sound effects. Then the work goes to the dubbing-room, where the final re-recording takes place. Here the operators have the one visual film to be combined with perhaps six reels of music and sound effect tracks, already perfectly timed, which must be combined into one unit.

The Final Stage

“Since the dubbing is the final stage, it requires extreme care. The sound engineers and the musical staff run through two or three rehearsals, to make certain that the second and third takes are no different from the first. The picture then is sent to the laboratory where it receives a final check. It is then ready for release.

“It would define genius as the right man in the right place at the right time. We know, of course, that many instances of the true being required by time, and we shall never be able to number the many musical films which have been made by men who were never given the leisure or the chance to do better work than they did. Yet so far as picture music demands steady, reliable craftsmanship, the routine described is the practical way of getting the maximum results in the minimum time.”

Radio, Today and Yesterday

Radio plays such a vitally important role in our lives today that it is, hard to believe that during the last war, radio as we know it did not exist. Today, the soldier can keep his own portable radio at the side of his cot or listen to amplified programs or all sorts in the camp auditoriums. On his own radio, he can dial for programs of good music that he has always liked and can still continue to enjoy. Imagine living in your barracks back in 1917 and 1918 and hearing the Philadelphian Orchestra and the New York Philharmonic-Symphony and comparing the importance of such men as Arturo Toscanini and Eugene Ormandy, to mention only two. Such a fabulous prediction, seemed so far fetched that people generally laughed.

Also, collections of fine records are distributed to the men in the European theater and to camps in the U. S. A. this gives to the soldiers, at first hand, the joy and comfort of music. The soldiers sing — and they dance — and they listen to the phonograph and the radio. Today, in our camps, music is continually the evidence of a longing to be home again, for the familiar tunes of the last war were not all forgotten. We have been giving some of our best musicians to the armed forces, but often the soldiers have been their best audience.

The American Red Cross has furnished many of the musical programs to the men overseas. This organization has given of its best, both in personnel and in equipment. The YMCA, too, has been of great service to the men. All these organizations have exemplary records and have aided the morale of our fighting men enormously.

In the last war, songs such as "Pack Up Your Troubles," "Over There," and many another have become part of the nation's heritage. In this war, the variety of music and the scope of musical offerings have been wider. The men over there have had the opportunity to listen to a great range of musical genres.

The influence of music is not limited to entertainment alone. It offers a glimpse of home, a message of solidarity, and sometimes even a spur to courage. To mention only a few. You will hear them say over and over again: “Music softens the ear of the soldier, gives a new courage to the man in battle, or the man in camp.”

Tin-Pan Alley Works Hard

To be sure, the work in Tin Pan Alley went on in the last war, and songs came out of the mills of melody; and in addition there are the compositions of our modern masters which have added to the music of this war. There have been new tunes that have given solace, and songs that have remained on the charts for long periods. The old values of melody and harmony still hold, even though the public taste may change from season to season.

Also, there are other ways of bringing music to the men in camps — musical shows, and other forms of entertainment. The camp show is still a vital factor in keeping the morale of the troops at a high level. Later, of course, the DJs and the radio will play an even greater part. The basic fact remains that music is necessary, music is part of our life, and there is no field in which music is more effective than in the army.

All these musical attractions of more legitimate proportions help to explain why there isn't so much singing as in the last war. Do not, however, get the idea from what has been mentioned above that the soldiers have lost their taste for songs. Far from it! Soldiers of free countries will always sing. There are glee clubs at most of the camps, and musicians at the helm, and camp song-fests are regular features. Every encouragement is given the soldier's interest in singing. As for those songs that have become favorites, it is not unusual for a favorite to have been a favorite for some time.

To mention only a few. You hear the songs of the last war, with the old melodies and the new ones which, incidentally, still have their echoes. In a way, the soldiers of this present war have been hearing and singing the old and the new. More and more, they have taken to the music which has been broadcast and distributed and which has been pressed into records. It keeps them in touch with home and with the things they hold dear.

The accused "thing in music education is the musically unambitious performance.

The child needs to participate in much music with others. He will love and benefit by games that really teach music. One teacher who had only a few pupils occasionally planned a musical tea in which the pupils played for each other. Appeal to the child's imagination to give meaning to his playing. He can hear the raindrop on the pane, the robin in the cherry tree, the elves on the stair as painted in the music. Encourage that. Let him impress some of his ideas to keep before him continually the conception of music as an inner experience given outer expression in beautiful sound.

Be sure that he is ready to learn. Then proceed from the known to the unknown. If he comes in tired from play, or called from play, take a little time to interest him in his music.

Then try to see that he isn't called from play the next time. A regular time for practice and for his lesson will usually avoid this. Friends will come at convenient times. Don't overstrain on practice. Do so only produces tension and hampers progress. The child should have relaxed but thoughtful practice as his aim.

If the child is too like music study he must be given the satisfaction of success. See that his music is simple enough to be well done, yet give him some material that throws down a challenge that he is eager to meet. From the first his music must be interesting and worth while to him. Let him hear good music. We play or sing because we have heard music which is so beautiful that we long to imitate it. The artist has made it approach a high mark, but rather than being discouraged we are stimulated to try to reach the same goal. Use your knowledge as you endeavor to share your love of music, and watch the change. The child who hated music will become one who, regardless of the degree of his skill, feels a warm, personal response to the beauty in music and who sees it with new eyes as the friend of a lifetime.

A Different System

Our Russian system of singing is somewhat different from that of any other country. Even professional art-singing makes use of the national characteristics that may be found in the singing of the people. We make use, in our songs, of wordless singing, or humming. Also, we accept the use of the falsetto as a legitimate part of our vocal ensembles. In our own work we have six parts, or voice choirs, (instead of the conventional four—although some of our arrangements call for twelve parts): falsetto, first tenor, second tenor, baritone, bass, and contra-bass. This permits of wonderful varieties of range, of course, and enables our male chorus to offer selections that would normally call for the highest female voices. In our group there are lyric tenors who have taken the falsetto part for twenty-three years and are still "going strong." The danger of the falsetto is like everything else in vocal work—its forcing or abuse. No one without a perfect command of head resonance should attempt it.

Humming for Resonance

Our traditional preference for humming is an excellent thing for the development of resonance. Even singers who make no use of this at all in their normal work might do well to practice it, in moderation, for its value in placing the voice forward. The use of humming is, as a resonance exercise, to feel the vibration of the tone in the lips and the maxilla. If this vibration is not clearly felt (as a buzzing tickling), the voice is incorrectly resonated.

During the summer months, however, we practice hard, both in program building and in vocal technic. For six summer weeks we practice eight hours a day—for four in the morning and four in the afternoon. It is then that we do the most strenuous work and develop our qualities of tone and the distribution of parts so that the leading voices come with ease. We give much attention to the selection of the right tempi and to the proper phrasing. The choir is constantly drilled in breathing, the proper use of the palate, and the practice of vowel uniformity.

"Another peculiarity of our ensemble is the use of the so-called 'open' vowels in many of our songs. In this style we make our vowels large and evidently shallow, in order to secure the proper brilliancy of tone. In the more serious church music, on the other hand, we make the vowels smaller and the tone more concentrated.

"Finally, the voice, as we use it, must above all be musical. We strive for a fine line of beauty in the tone and for a perfect ensemble. That is the backbone of the work. The rest is color and effect."

6. Keep your registration simple. Try not to shift hands on your manuals, or change several stops at once on different manuals until you become expert enough to do so without losing your whole attention to the music.

8. Use Bach sparingly in the average small church. Chorales, choral preludes, and movements of suites are better choices than the big preludes, fugues, and toccatas.

9. Try to keep your embarrassment under control. When you make a mistake—and you will—always don't let it weaken the rest of your playing—forget it and go on. No one will blame you for making a mistake, but you may be blamed if you make the same one twice! One way to cut down on mistakes is to be faithful to your practice time. Remember that your private work is just as important as your public playing in attaining professional poise and alertness. So don't skimp! If you can practice on an organ free of charge, go and be delighted to make the most of the opportunity.

10. Find the best tempo for the service as a whole and adhere to it. Never forget that the organist's function is to serve the congregation. You must do more than play—you must lead them. Memorize the hymns as far as you can so the congregation will feel your leadership. Avoid excessive display. A slow service played evenly and well is better than one of constant tempo changes.

11. Study your accompaniment. Learn to make the melodies sound well and to support the singers. Practice accompanying with various registrations to get the best balance.

12. Take advantage of all learning opportunities. Listen to fine organists when you can, and ask for advice. Keep growing musically and technically.

The Trail of the Tempered Scale

(Continued from Page 721)

With the coming of the keyboard instruments, and the encroachment of harmony upon counterpoint, the need of tempering became imperative, even though the church authorities frowned.

Accidentals crept into our music chiefly to enable the church modes to produce better harmony, especially for a final cadence, or "Amen" progression, as we use a G-sharp in the dominant chord preceding the final f-minor.

Modes and Scales

A scale is not a mode. It is an arrangement of scale-steps from which modes are formed. Our diatonic scale has two half-steps in it, and our major and minor modes put them into different places in each of their tetrachords: C-D-E-F; G-A-B-C in the major; A-B-C-D; E-F-G-A in the relative minor. Accidentals permit rearranging the position of the half-steps so as to transform major modes into minor or vice versa. By degrees all twelve of the church modes were thus whittled down to two, major and minor, both better suited for harmony.

The church, however, would not willingly sacrifice the pure-tone scale, natural to voices. But five black keys crept onto the keyboard surreptitiously, forming a very ill-tempered chromatic scale. An even-tempered scale was suggested by Bartolo Ramos, a Spanish monk, in 1482, but the consequent distortion of the pure-tone scale forbade its use. The even-tempered scale had to wait the further revival of Greek learning and the full bloom of the Renaissance.

The real theory of the better tone-tempered scale was Giosetto Zarlino, who preceded Monteverde as organist of St. Mark's, Venice. Opera was coming, and keyboard instruments demanded better own chamber music. Zarlino revived the theories of Claudius Ptolemy, and the interest of even new ideas, including the need for an even-tempered scale. A terrific storm arose between the new harmonists and the old modalists. The harmonists won, but even then the tempered scale was modified into a compromise meantone scale suggested by Mersenne, a French scientist. Music, of course, was still finely vocal, and the mean-tone scale had a minimum tempering, so as nearly to preserve the pure-tone scale while permitting modulation into a few neighboring keys.

The freedom of the keys, twelve major and twelve minor, was not established until Bach proved the artistic value of the even-tempered scale with his "Forty-eight Preludes and Fugues." He opened immense fields of unexplored harmony. Thus the old trial of the tempered scale broadened into a great highway down which we have all traveled ever since the genius of Sebastian Bach was first recognized.

How to Teach Your Child Absolute Pitch

by Esther Marshall

This article is published as it was presented to us as a carefully observed account of an American mother who has had exceptional success in developing absolute pitch in her children. The plan outlined here has been found effective under normal family conditions by a number of teachers who have tried it.

I am sometimes asked, "Isn't absolute pitch an inherited quality?" I do not believe that it is necessarily hereditary. My husband, a professional musician, who has a good sense of pitch, does not have absolute pitch. Our two children, a boy and a girl, were given ordinary training and did not have absolute pitch at first. Evidently it is acquired, or is it acquired by training? It is recognized by educators that almost every child can be trained to speak a foreign language without accent if trained at an early age. The same principle applies to pitch.

After reading the article in The Etude (June, 1937) on "Can Perfect Pitch Be Acquired?" it occurred to me that if the ear could be trained to know and recognize definite tones, then the child, by constant, systematic presentation, might develop absolute pitch. I myself am one of those people who are born with a poor sense of pitch, but I have always tried to make music a part of the children's lives. When I took piano lessons at home with my children, I found that the younger child responds more quickly. While I have no professional musical training, yet I was able to teach by persistence. My conclusion is that, though the thing is easier for some children than others, it can be developed by persistent training and correct technique.

Making the Organ Talk

by Marvin Anderson

THE EXPRESSION "He surely can make it talk!" sometimes is used in referring to a musical who produces sensational results on his instrument. The clever organist, however, can make his instrument talk in a different and almost literate sense.

In listening to the playing of certain excellent (but not famous) organists I have noticed that when they play hymns the organ seems to form not only the music but also the words of the hymn. An analysis of such results shows that this effect is produced by skillful phrasing and by dividing and subdividing each phrase. While this, perhaps, is done unconsciously by some organists, it is a skill which can be attained or improved by deliberate attention to certain rules.

First of all the organist must give careful attention to the punctuation of the text. The organist should interpret the punctuation of the line; periods and commas demand a definite rhythmic release and slight pause at the close of the clause. Also the long note, sung or declaimed at the end of a phrase, must receive special emphasis. When a phrase ends with a sustaining note, the organist should give that note sufficient weight to suggest the end of the sentence. In a measure of three, for instance, a phrase that ends on the third beat will often sound more conclusive if there is a slight retardation and emphasis on that last note, as if the words of the text had been spoken. Conversely, a comma calls for only a brief pause and may permit a small lifting of the voice or a diminuendo and quick renewal of motion.

It is well for the organist to think of the hymns he plays as if they were prose to be read. By dividing the phrases according to the grammar and sense of the words, the organist can make the instrument "speak." This is not merely a mechanical division: it must come from an understanding of the text. A line that is grammatically one sentence should be phrased as one musical sentence; a colon or semicolon shows a closer relationship than a period. Emphasize the important words in the phrase; do not allow all words to receive equal weight. The principal words, the nouns and verbs and the words that carry the thought, should be prominent. A musical accent on these words will make the meaning clear.

In many hymns one discovers that the composer has made evident provision for the text by using a recurring rhythmic figure at the end of lines. The organist should take advantage of these musical "commas" and "periods" to give clarity to the text. In certain stanzas, especially where there is a refrain, a slight broadening on the phrase that leads to the refrain will enhance the sense of arrival. Similarly, when the text contains a question or an exclamation, an appropriate change in registration and articulation gives the effect of a spoken question or exclamation.

Voice-leading and articulation also are important. Legato playing is necessary in many hymn styles, but there are times when a detached articulation or slight separation of notes will reproduce the inflection of the spoken word. In faster hymns, use of a small amount of articulation and lightness of touch will bring out the more conversational character of the text. The choice of stops is likewise significant. A warm foundation, flutes for simplicity, and reeds for emphasis are useful. The organist must avoid the temptation to employ too much tone or too many colors at once. Simplicity often is the most effective means of making the text intelligible. Avoid continuous use of loud foundations that mask the inner lines and words.

Another device is to vary the registration within the stanza to suit the meaning. If the text moves from a humbler thought to one more exultant, a gradual addition of reeds or principals will mirror the sentiment. When the stanza asks for supplication or pleading, thinner stops, flutes and celestes, will make a sympathetic effect. When the stanza proclaims victory or joy, full diapason and reeds will give the proper exultation.

It is inadvisable to make every phrase a climax by using heavy registration. Instead the organist should provide a series of peaks and valleys so that the true climaxes have impact. Restraint is too often the secret of real eloquence. Do not overdo accentuation. Practice the art of holding back until the proper moment.

In short, to make the organ "talk" the organist must:

1. Phrase according to the punctuation and sense of the text.
2. Emphasize the important words by slight accentuation.
3. Use articulation to mimic speech inflections.
4. Choose registration to reflect the emotional content.
5. Shape the stanza so that climaxes are meaningful by contrast.

With study and care an organist can bring out the full meaning of a hymn and make his instrument a speaking partner in worship.

Blending the Registers
by Herbert Wendell Austin

REGISTERS are the so-called divisions of the voice which result from the readjustment that is made in the vocal cords as the pitch proceeds up or down the scale. The trained voice takes its lowest tones in the chest register, its medium tones in the medium register, and its upper tones in the head (falsetto) register. As such a voice passes from one register to another in the course of a song, the blending of the registers is so finely done that it is difficult for the ear to detect the change. This scientific blending of the registers makes possible a wide, musical, vocal range.

In the chest register the flow of breath is forward and without much impact against the parts of the mouth. The falsetto voice brings the tones forward on the breath and places them against the hard palate, or upper teeth, for resonance. As the falsetto proceeds into its higher ranges, there is the sensation that the tones are in the head rather than in the throat, the resulting tones often being called head tones. Students need much practice on the registers. At first the breaks between the registers are apt to be quite noticeable, but with patient practice the tones will begin to blend together and finally the "three voices" will sound quite like one. It is a vocal achievement worth all the labor invested in it.

I did not start out to teach our daughters absolute pitch. They seemed anxious to learn to play the piano, and we had to have them in a class. The first time we took them to the piano the older girl played by ear. When she was just past four I bought her an instruction book and started to teach the piano tuned to International pitch. Because of my lack of tonality, I was unable to rely on my own singing or playing. Consequently, I merely taught her names of the notes and how to read music in the same way an older child would be taught. After a month or two she was reading simple tunes in her book, and I noticed that she knew when she hit a wrong note, although she was not looking at her hands. I turned her back to the piano and found that she could name any note played, and within the range of notes she had learned something over two octaves. As she learned more notes, her range increased, and as chords were introduced into her pieces, she learned to distinguish two or three notes at a time. After a year and a half of study she could play one note on the keyboard and two, three or four notes played simultaneously anywhere except at the extreme ends of the register; probably she will eventually hear those. She can recognize the tones of a violent discord as easily as of a concord. She can sing any tone within her range in perfect pitch, and can play by ear the tunes she learned to sing in kindergarten. These tones were taught entirely without a piano. She is able also to recognize most tunes played on other instruments, if they are within the same pitch as our piano. While she is a bright child for her age, she seemed to possess no other musical ability until we started to teach her. Her sense of rhythm was poor, and she did not try to sing in time with other children.

After the foregoing experience, I decided that possibly children are better to learn with pianos; otherwise the variety of pitches in a child's daily life might confuse him. Accordingly, I then proceeded to teach the other two children to listen and name notes. I may be mistaken, but the methods detailed above seem to be adapted to three children of hearing age.
