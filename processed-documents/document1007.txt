THE POWER OF MUSIC.  
A well-known writer has said: "Silent music, more than words, cures everything." This is very true. Music has so broad an influence that it is difficult to know where to start or from what angle to treat the subject. There is one thing, however, that must be said for it, and that is, it is the most powerful means of expressing the emotions. 

In the circle of music, the teacher is patient; a book may be read; the listener is often moved by it; and when there is music at all, it must be heard with an enthusiasm which is appreciated or not. I can see that neither artist nor audience longs for the old fashioned way, and here is where the recital exercise may often fail to touch the heart. 

It is only by the thorough training of vocal expression that music may really quite assist, and to some it may sound as though the question of public performance is the main thought in mind. 

WHAT PUPILS THINK OF THEIR TEACHERS.  
E. H. S.  

At the annual meeting of the Teachers' National Association in Philadelphia, the subject of teaching was first given prominence, and various papers were read on this important subject, bringing forth discussions of the leading principles of the education of the mind and the musical aptitude that was apparent in the majority of the eminent teachers present. 

The founder of The Etude, so generally appreciated for the value of the magazine, might provide a probable place for the consideration of the abilities of each pupil, and this contribution may be allowed as a complete interpreter of the voice of others. 

To "come out as a teacher" is not always a comforting thing, and may be extremely helpful. In the spirit of this, I have gathered some thoughtful pupils representing various sections of the country, the following brief criticisms relate to the work of their former teachers, and include their elaboration of the sincerity shown. 

"My teacher lacked enthusiasm and did not strengthen me if I had." The latter statement was also true of many professors, and certainly the proper method is one of greatest importance. 

"I would like to understand how to sing better." This is a real difficulty that is often encountered, as the student cannot fail of coming along either to believe in you and in his own abilities. 

"My teacher never told me how to practice." Probably this teacher never had realized the full meaning of Latin mentality, "in tempore, in longa, dies venit." But added to this yet, shows the dangers of age as greater than in previous years. 

"I was influenced too much by my teacher. If I did this, I was a place I was not obliged to learn." Here the teacher did not comprehend his mission. He was evidently not as a teacher, trusted and respected by the pupil, as pleasant friends, who were ready for a demonstration, make a story called a lesson in terms of regulations. 

The teacher is placed in hopes of the account of Parnassus, who will not allow his pupil to wander into perilous places, nor into regions inhabited by the great artist.  

"I was there particularly about concert fingers." This often appears when the artist restricts the liberty of a pupil who performs works that do not reach or even correct the patterns. 

Errors of "never near" for musical study and the corresponding amount of work appear.

THE ETUDE

HAS WON SUCCESS ON ITS OWN MERITS and in a field peculiarly its own. Its aim has been, from the initial number, to encourage a love for higher class of music—to give forth helpful hints to the struggling teacher and ambitious amateur—to treat only practical subjects that deal directly with the study of music. It is alike helpful to teacher and pupil. It advocates no special theories. It treats every subject in the art, practically. It is not a journal for current musical events. Its articles have a permanent value. Personalities or the lauding of pet theories have no place in the columns of THE ETUDE. It is devoted wholly to the interests of the musical profession and study of music.

There are in THE ETUDE several permanent departments, but the main portion of the journal is devoted to original articles by our leading teachers. Mr. Mathews will continue to conduct his "Practical Letters to Teachers," which have proved so stimulating to most of our teachers. The Question and Answer Department will be conducted, as in the past, by no one editor but the entire corps, and a number of specialists, as the case demands. Mrs. Trebar will continue to have charge of the Musical Items, in which mention is made of the principal musical events at home and abroad A column will be given to Concert Programs, more for the purpose of making known the compositions suitable for public performance. Mr. Fillmore will have charge of New Publications Department, in which is reviewed the principal new works as they are issued.

The music of THE ETUDE alone is worth many times the subscription price of the journal. There will be from 12 to 16 pages of choice music in each issue. The compositions will always be of merit and within the range of ordinary players. We intend to make the music more of an object of the journal in the future. The pieces are always to be had in regular sheet music form.

There will be no radical change made in any part of the journal. The field is broad enough, untold riches lie still unexplored, so there is no need to go seeking for attractions. Mr. Presser will still have charge of the journal and all matter passes through his hands before publication.

THE ETUDE relies for its support on the subscriptions received. The profession has been very liberal in its patronage and we hope to deserve the same in the year before us. To all those who desire to extend the circulation of THE ETUDE we will send free a bundle of sample copies. To those who desire to act as agents will send for a special circular which we have issued for this purpose.
