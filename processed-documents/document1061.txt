Oh, Mother by the manger
With Jesus on Thine Arm,
Guide Thou our sons this holy night;
Keep them from battle's harm.

Thou know'st the pangs of sorrow
And what we have to give
To show the world that sacrifice
Will help mankind to live.

All hail the song of angels!
From out the midnight sky,
All hail good will and peace on earth,
That must come from on high!

Grant that another Christmas
Will mark the end of war,
Bring cheer and joy to us again,
And love for evermore.

The star forever leads us on
Towards Heaven's holy light.
Bless us, dear Lord, this day of days,
Guide Thou our fight for right.

James Francis Cooke

[Introductory paragraph about providing readers with authoritative opinions on post-war musical conditions and that Mr. Wulsin's opinions are timely — EDITOR'S NOTE.]

[Photograph caption beneath portrait of a man:]
LUCIEN WULSIN

CARROLL GLENN

[Photograph of a young woman playing violin — caption:]
CARROLL GLENN

“I THINK I’d rather be interviewed for The Etude than for any other magazine in the world,” exclaimed Carroll Glenn who, though barely of voting age, has established a name for herself. There are four major musical awards in America, and Miss Glenn has won all of them. There used to be a feeling that a “woman violinist” was a commercial question-mark, and Miss Glenn has thoroughly exploded it with a professional schedule of over sixty concerts in one year, twenty-eight of them with major orchestras.

Miss Glenn hails from a small town in South Carolina, with a population of about 7000 and only local music teachers. She received her earliest instruction from her mother and from The Etude, the articles of which stimulated her musical desire. Her mother had had a burning desire to play the violin, but had no teacher until she went away to school in Washington. Mindful of her own early longings for instruction, Mrs. Glenn sent as many as nine other children in the town and began her own small daughter’s lessons when Carroll was four. “I didn’t study seriously until I was five!” Within a year, Carroll was ready for advanced instruction, and mother and daughter spent every Saturday riding sixty-five miles to Columbia for lessons . . . on a quarter-sized fiddle from Sears, Roebuck. At twelve, Miss Glenn won the first of her scholarships at the Juilliard School, where she studied with the noted teacher, Eduard Dethier. At sixteen she won the coveted Naumburg Award which gave her a New York debut. A year later she won the Town Hall Endowment Award offered to the American artist under thirty whom the New York critics consider “the most promising.” Two years later Miss Glenn captured two important awards in one. The National Federation of Music Clubs offered a prize of $1000 to the most gifted young violinist, and the Peabody Award, a gold medal, established a prize which has gone to the most outstanding student of the country. Miss Glenn also had the good fortune to win the award of the Schubert Orchestra when she appeared with the New York Philharmonic and the Philadelphia orchestras. From her twelfth year on, she has “financed” her own studies and career through scholarships and prizes — and she points to as the evidence of the rich musical possibilities available to American students.

“The girl violinist has special problems; she must approximate by adaptability what the man has by nature. She must develop her hand for strength and flexibility; she must acquire sufficient stretch, and she must learn to control weight for a fine, big tone. Every violinist must master these techniques, be sure, but the girl needs to work harder at them because her hand is naturally smaller. For that reason, it is advisable to get her started on her studies as early as possible. The student who begins work at high school age is already under a serious handicap, for by that time she has had begun to become ‘set’ and the (unnatural) position of the instrument offers difficulties in itself. It should be emphasized, however, that the fact of being a girl offers no obstacles to the serious student. Nothing about violin playing requires brute force! The source of a big tone is not strength but controlled weight — and the average girl’s arm can release more natural weight on a violin and bow than the instrument can bear! The ‘trick’ is to relax the release natural body weight through a relaxed arm and to focus it around the top joint of the middle finger that guides the bow. Weight must come from above downwards and not like the drive of an athletic stroke, but softly with flexion in the middle finger. Thus, by controlling the distribution of body weight through a relaxed arm, one acquires tone without the expenditure of strength or force.

“It is the bowing arm that needs watching! Someone wisely said that the right hand is the true artist! Anyone who cautions, corrects, and normally will left hand fingers to do the work will be out of luck; the left hand must do its share. But the right hand needs to master the movement, the ability of the wrist to give and take, the movement of the knuckles, and the exact wrist action. The string is supported by the finger and the bow must be held and applied evenly through the entire length of the bow and at every point along the length, without allowing the arm to become stiff through the sheer mechanical lack of balancing pressure. Here the trick is to keep the right arm perfectly relaxed, and never under any circumstance to raise the right shoulder. The moment the shoulder is raised, tension results.”

Bowing “Schools”

“In the most general way, and allowing for any number of individual adaptations, there are three schools of bowing. The German school advocates a low bowing arm, kept close to the body. The French school uses a high arm with the elbow out and centers weight in the wrist. The Franco-Belgian school (that of Ysaye, Vieuxtemps, and Kreisler) uses the modified technique of balance by the fingers and an extremely flexible wrist. Having been taught this method myself (and with most helpful results), I naturally believe it to be the best — both in results and in speed of production, and therefore the only one which I recommend as the most worthwhile.”

“Mr. Dethier gave me many excellent exercises for developing flexibility and strength of wrist. One of the best is to play scales in thirds (double stops) in tight rhythm, crossing the strings in specato bowing. That is to say, play the first note on the lower string and the next on the upper, and so on, but with the accent on the upper string. Continue until flexibility of wrist is secure; then reverse the process. This exercise is shown above. (Continued on Page 824).”

ONE DAY not long ago I was discussing with an American composer whose works are widely performed, the availability of articles on harmony, counterpoint, and composition. I said to him, "What do your composers want to know?" His reply was direct and to the point: "They want to know 'why.' Why can you do this or that? Why can you do some things and not others?" He turned to the piano and played a series of chromatic progressions from one of his own compositions. "Why?" he asked, "Why can you use those chords?"

First of all, it must be explained that harmony and counterpoint are inextricably bound together. There are, to be sure, a few compositions that consist only of harmony and tune, with no counterpoint, chords and no moving parts except the tune—but they are rare.

"Counterpoint," in many minds means a weaving of parts—fugues and the like. It is far more than that. It is any moving part that introduces a secondary melody, a dissonant note, or such notes as result in the formation of an altered chord—where the basic harmony is temporarily changed.

And what is basic harmony? Usually it is one of the prime chords of the key; tonic, dominant, or sub-dominant. If the chords on the second, third, sixth, or seventh of the key are used, they will be, in most cases, either alterations of these basic harmonies or temporary transpositions into related keys. Basic harmony and tune are also, like harmony and counterpoint, inextricably bound together; each makes the other, neither can exist without the other. In all that follows, these points must be kept continually in mind.

This very simple example will serve to illustrate how counterpoint may create a harmony. This is harmonic-counterpoint.

A. The tune with its basic harmony. B. Altered harmonies made by counter-melodies; the bass and each of the inner parts, should be examined separately. C. A counterpoint varying the bass of B. D. Various chords, and chord-progressions, are thus created (Ex.—E-minor, F-minor, F-sharp 7, E-flat augmented sixth, and so on), but they must not be analyzed as separate chords, or in parts. They are not chord-progressions but are passages which are taught in the normal harmony. They are harmonies resulting from the careful writing of melodic parts, all of which belong to the same basic harmony.

SECURED EXPRESSLY FOR THE ETUDE BY JAY MEYER

Dr. James Rowland Angell is widely regarded as one of the ablest men in the field of American scholarship. He was born at Burlington, Vermont, the son of Prof. James Burrill Angell, former president of the University of Michigan, who later for a time was Minister to China and who earlier performed diplomatic as well as educational duties which helped to make his distinguished father's reputation.

Trained as a psychologist and interested fundamentally in the field of education, Dr. Angell has been associated for many years with the Chicago schools, the University of Chicago, and foreign universities. He served as president of the Carnegie Corporation, 1920-1927; he was president of Yale University, 1921-1937. He has been an educational counselor for the National Broadcasting Company.

Dr. Angell has been one of the many recognized as authorities on the progressive movement in education, and though he may be known to the general public as the head of institutions, he has been more directly associated with educational advancement on a broad field than perhaps any other American public authority. He is director of the New York Life Insurance Company.—Editor's Note.

IF, IN THESE CHAOTIC TIMES, music does nothing more than help to remove some of the emotional tension of the hour, it is serving a patriotic purpose of significant value. In the future the pattern for education in general will be subject to great mutations. As in everything else, education, from the kindergarten to the university, may be entirely national, because changing conditions require new methods. This is particularly true in the field of music, for the radio, the talking machine and the motion picture have revolutionized the means of communication in a way which will affect the imagination. These media can be used to reach the field of the individual teacher and the music, but they are no substitute for education if they are not used wisely. In the whole area of education, the introduction of radio receivers into millions of homes has advanced the possibility of making music more universal and accessible than ever before.

Two lines of procedure have opened themselves. The first is that of coordination within existing educational institutions—schools, colleges, conservatories, and universities. The second is that of reaching the self-help student in his home, and the private teacher is the most important medium. Because education is primarily the work of the home and of the private teacher, they must receive greater support by public bodies and by the state.

Thus, in these days there must be a more natural alliance between the schools and the new commercial agencies who have a share in musical education. The matter is by no means simple and demands wise constructive planning. The historic institutions of music are the conservatories. They must continue to be of value, but they cannot alone provide the necessary training for millions of young people, and their tradition must be somewhat modified to meet the national and international technical demands.

The private music teacher is the most important of the many contributors to musical education. His work is of tremendous importance, and in these years when the broadcasting companies, the phonograph firms, and other agencies have been giving opportunities for mass demonstration, he must be in close touch with the new methods and the new repertoire. Practically speaking, the teacher now can and must supplement the student's development by using the best records and the best radio programs as vehicles of instruction. These devices will permit the teacher to present examples of great music which the individual student might not otherwise have heard.

During the earlier years in America, when music was a more limited study, conservatories played a leading part. When the Boston society of music established the New England Conservatory in Boston, and musical education began to take on a more regularized form.

The private music teacher did not at that time have the group facilities for illustration which conservatory life afforded. Now, however, their contact with well-trained private music teachers who are in touch with the best records and with the best radio programs, can coordinate his work in a way which may give the teacher special advantages when he aspires to supplement the student's private lessons. These advantages are not limited to the interpretations of the composer, for the largest places at his disposal of the interpretations of the greatest masters of the times. At the beginning of this country these advantages for the individual student could not have been procured save at enormous expense. Yet in these days, unless the student's work is intelligently directed the teacher can secure this vital supplementary work, his whole musical structure cannot be cooperative. The broad broadcasting companies have been eager to cooperate with the teacher in the colleges and conservatories; also they are anxious to bring their own product. To this end a large number of inexpensive books and guides have been issued at very low cost, and more of this work may be more intelligently co-related.

Distinguished Pioneers

If the teachers of America knew what the broad-casting companies have invested in symphony, choral, and chamber music concerts, made available to schools and colleges with very slight expense, they would be staggered by the amount. It runs into millions of dollars.

In the field of music aided by the talking machine, the first editors of Dr. Frances Elliott Clark have had nation-wide recognition and employment. For four decades this tireless and enterprising music supervisor, starting with the Victor Company, secured the selection and publication of materials which could be used in the schools for illustrative purposes. At that time the use of records in schools was quickly developing and the demand for better records and better pedagogical material was insistent. It is inevitable that such organized efforts should continue and that the task which the national broadcasting companies carry on in the United States will be increasingly useful.

In these days there are three great agencies which must be coordinated: the conservatories, the schools, and the broadcasting companies. The effectiveness of music education will depend largely upon the ability of these agencies to work together for the public good. The teacher must realize that his work will be enhanced, not supplanted, by these co-operative influences; and, on the other hand, that the commercial agencies must recognize their responsibilities to the educational field and to the integrity of musical standards.

Past handicaps, such as restricted facilities for the private teacher and the scarcity of good records and programs for school use, are gradually being removed. The problem of organization is now the chief task. If we can coordinate the efforts of the conservatories, the public schools, the private studios, and the broadcasting and recording industries, the result will be a national musical system adequate to the needs of tomorrow.

millions of man hours and untold loss and annoyance to itself.

Note these simple opportunities:

other kind of store to shop:

(a) Make up your mind definitely in advance just what you plan to buy.
Better still, write it down in accurate detail.

(b) Have your money ready.

(c) Take as little of the salesman's time as possible with conversation not
directly connected with the transaction.

(d) Never get excited or annoyed if things are not just as you wish them.

doing his best. Remember, this is war. Carry all portable bundles.

were needed as much as now. You know in advance pretty much what you

Patriotic Music — and Bands

A Conference with

Dr. Edwin Franko Goldman

Distinguished American Bandmaster and Composer

SECURED EXPRESSLY FOR THE ETUDE BY STEPHEN WEST

IN THE MATTER of bands and band music, there is no greater authority than Dr. Edwin Franko Goldman, whose distinguished career advances the tradition of the "Five Franko children" (one of whom was his mother) who toured Europe and America in the late 'sixties. John Philip Sousa once told Dr. Goldman that his own first taste of good music grew out of a concert that the Franko children gave in Washington years ago. Today, Sousa's mantle of eminence has fallen on Dr. Goldman's shoulders. This past summer the Goldman Band gave its 145th concert when it opened its twenty-sixth year of performances on the Mall in Central Park, New York City. The Etude has asked Dr. Goldman to give his views on the scope of patriotic music that is flooding the country today, and to tell how bands can help to serve music in war times.

"It is encouraging to note the nation's desire to express itself through patriotic music; but it is less encouraging to see the form that some of this expression takes. People seem to think that a few trumpet flourishes and a desire to be patriotic is all it requires to turn out a fine song, words and music complete. Now nothing could be farther from the truth! If the business of setting down words-and-music is to mean anything, it must be done with the specialist's knowledge and the expert's care. Hardly a day goes by that I am not approached by some well-intentioned amateur who assures me that he has just 'dashed off a number' more inspiring than Elgar's Pomp and Circumstance, and won't I do something about it. So far this year I have examined nearly three hundred such 'inspirations' — and they are as far from expressing patriotic art as the limmericks of a lovesick schoolboy are from the poems of Heine. Now I am heartily in sympathy with every American who has patriotic fervor — but my honest advice to immature composers is to learn their business before they attempt to express themselves in music.

The Song Writer's Needs

"The writing of songs involves a lot more than the ability to speak the language! It requires a specialist's knowledge of English construction, prosody, meter, diction, vocal line, human psychology — also it requires experience in writing lyrics. The writing of music (any kind of music) requires a thorough knowledge of composition, harmony, arrangement, vocal line, and the intricate business of fitting words to music. Theoretically, I suppose it might be possible for an inexperienced amateur to stumble upon all this through 'inspiration' — but in practice, I have never yet seen it happen. My best advice is to look skeptically upon 'inspiration.' Turn your patriotic sentiments to practical use by buying War Bonds, and leave song-writing alone until you have learned how to write song.

"The really good patriotic song demands certain values. The music must have a not-too-complicated, singable tune and the pulse must be of marked, sure, meaningful rhythm. The words must be hearty, genuine (that is, not manufactured for the sake of writing patriotic words!) understandable, and charged with valid, honest patriotism. Let us examine Praise the Lord and Pass the Ammunition — the most successful patriotic song to have grown out of the present war so far — and try to see what makes it 'click.' The words are completely simple, unaffected, and hearty. Best of all, they grew out of an actual happening, and thus serve to express something real and timely, which is very different from being 'made up' out of synthetic sentiment. The tune has marked rhythmic swing and (what, I am sure, most people don't realize) it has the simple, natural, singable impulse of the old hymn tunes. Without being in the least derivative, it suggests the general pattern of Glory, Glory, Hallelujah! Effects like those make the song live — and they are seldom 'hit upon' by accident. Until the amateur has learned how to turn out a song with similar qualities, let him wait with sending it to his manager.

"This is the age for bands, and every least member of every least band can do his share towards making his organization serve the cause of music. The first thing for him to remember, however, is that it is the cause of music that must be served, and not the band for its own sake! There are a number of improvements that have still to be made in our approach to bands and band music.

The Student Band

"For one thing, the student band needs a great deal more individual instruction, with the result that there is but little time for perfecting the work of any one of them. Now the effect of the band is secured only when the instruments play together, and you can't possibly make a fine ensemble tune out of mediocre individual tones. Practicing won't help if one doesn't practice correctly — and the correct way can be taught only through slow, gradual, painstaking, individual care.

"Again, many school bandmasters are apt to overdo the band by crowding it with more instruments than it needs. It's a mistake to think that a band can be made by combining all the instruments that can be blown into! If the band has too many instruments of similar tone, the resulting sound is muddy and blurred — none of the instruments give clear, crisp definition. If your band has cornets and trumpets, don't crowd in flugelhorns — even though you have someone on hand who can play them! Don't make the mistake of thinking that every band piece needs an English horn part. The English horn is strictly an effect, not a basic one. The same is true of the harp, of the battery section, etc. I see the problem, the difficulty grows out of assembling a school band first, and then letting it play anything and everything regardless of the musical wishes of the composer. I have seen bands make use of a regular fife-and-drum corps effect in works by Schubert and Mendelssohn that call for no drums at all. And why? Because there are six young drummers in the band who want to have something to do! Now that is not a musical approach, and it is doing our band progress great harm.

The Organist and the Orchestra

by

Warren D. Allen

This is the second of a series of three brilliant articles on the organ by Dr. Warren D. Allen, Head of the Music Department of Stanford University, California. Organ students will find these articles very informative. — Editor's Note.

IN A PRECEDING article in The Etude for November, it was pointed out that the development of the organ went hand in hand with the organization of the choirs and orchestras with which it was used, but that the over-development of the organ as a solo instrument has left it high and dry in unhealthy isolation. The symphony orchestra and the a cappella choir have taken the place, in our musical life, of that which was filled in the nineteenth century by the municipal organ, the church organ, the school organ, and the residence organ. Organists in ever-increasing numbers have turned to orchestral conducting, choral direction, music education, and research. The organ builder has turned from the "romantic" organ (imitation of dynamic voices and orchestra) to the imitation of Bach's "classical" organ, often with no dynamic possibilities whatever.

The purpose of this discussion is to show how the organist, tired of trying to imitate the orchestra, can cooperate with it, not only in the concerto for organ but in concertos with organ, in chamber music, and in the church orchestra.

The Concerto for Organ

George Frederick Handel, in adapting himself to new conditions in England, was able to put his dramatic powers into the composition of oratorio, after Italian opera had been laughed off the stage. He also adapted himself to the English organ and made it the companion of the orchestra.

The English organ, in the early 1700's, had no pedals; the German organs used by Bach in those same years did have them. Hence it was Handel who wrote no solo music for the English organ, and Bach wrote volumes of it for the German organ. For the same reason, also, Handel found it possible to use the English manual organs in concerti. With these manuals the organist has one, two, three or eight different flue stops, the strings, flutes, and oboes of the orchestra, while the bassoons and double basses in the latter provide the heavy basis when we lack the help of the English organs. Over in Germany, on the other hand, Bach's organ "concertos" were either for the organ alone, in all its completeness, or for the orchestra without any aid from the organ. Handel's concertos, however, indicate some possibilities for organ-orchestra combinations, for the present day. These possibilities are worthy of consideration in any community which has small organs and orchestras which might be used together.

Concertos with Organ

Organ concertos in the nineteenth century, from those of Rheinberger to those of Guilmant, made no adaptations to the "world's greatest music." Maybe it was because "the world's greatest music" crowded the orchestra in scope and volume. Or two occasions, when playing Leon Boellmann's very graceful, "Fantaisie Dialoguée" for organ with the San Francisco Symphony Orchestra, the conductor, Alfred Hertz, was very fearful, and rightly so, that I might step on a forte pedal and drown out his men completely. It was possible to use only a part of the organ each time; the municipal organs of San Francisco and Sacramento, when opened up full blast, are too much for any orchestra. Cooperation is still possible, however, with small organs and small orchestras, as it was in Handel's day.

The organ can replace the orchestra effectively in accompanying concertos for other instruments. James T. Charles used to accompany instrumentalists in many masterpieces of concerto literature in his recitals at Bailey Hall, Cornell University. In years past, not only the slow movements, but sometimes entire concertos have been feasible and effective with organ in my recitals at the Stanford Memorial Church. The oboe concerto by Handel, the clarinet concerto by Mozart, the violin concertos by Bach (especially the "Double Concerto"), and concertos by Mendelssohn, Bruch, Vieuxtemps, and others offer excellent examples. Even the piano concertos by Liszt and Grieg have come off successfully, but the piano-organ combination is peculiarly sensitive to temperature changes. Obviously, the problem of playing an orchestral accompaniment on the organ belongs under the heading of organ transcriptions.

Chamber Music with Organ

Here we enter a vast realm of possibilities, many of which have hardly been explored. The organ has been developed to Gargantuan proportions since the days of the old "portatives," which could be carried to church or to a neighbor's house under an arm. Planing from the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance show how extensive was the use of these little organs in church service and civic functions, even in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. We have much to learn in the use of small organs in chamber music. Whole books on the possibilities could be written about them. The possibilities suggest, with marvelous reality available. It is also necessary only to use the small organs of the present day and the possibilities of our large organs, to realize what can be done if we have imagination.

First of all there is the idea of the organ as an accompanying instrument for instruments. Mozart's sonatas for piano and violin are just now being published in this country. The Spanish composer Soler is yet to become well known here, but his music for organ and violin is even more interesting than the Mozart sonatas. Music for piano and organ by César Franck ("Prelude, Fugue and Variation") and Joseph Cokey, to name only two composers, illustrates the possibilities in that field. The success of the harpsichord combination is proverbial, of course, but as the music for harpsichord and modern instruments grew, more music was written with the American organ in mind.

By dint of transcription, the organ can take over much of the chamber music originally composed for harpsichord and pianoforte. Old chamber music, calling for harpsichord, frequently sounds better with a crisp, clean-cut organ accompaniment with soft mutation stops, than it could possibly sound with the pianoforte.

First and foremost on the list of chamber music suitable for organ is the chamber suite of the eighteenth century. At least two of the Brandenburg Concertos by J. S. Bach (the second and third) and many of the works in this form by Corelli, Vivaldi, and Handel are stunning with organ. A small string orchestra and soloists in every case. Bach's "Second Brandenburg Concerto" can be done without orchestra; the flute, oboe, trumpet, and violin combine beautifully with organ. There is one proviso here, however. If no bass viol is available, the organ must have a soft 16' on one of the manuals, preferably a double open on the Great, with enough "ping" to enable the organist to play rapid bass voices clearly.

This use of a manual 16' for the continuo is desirable, even in some of Bach's organ works, where the left-hand part is suggestive of Bach's orchestral bass parts. When these are too rapid for pedal performance, the manual 16' proves its worth over and over again. This is especially true in old chamber-orchestra music. One other essential is, of course, bright fundamental registers, with no 16' tone in right-hand passages.

Renaissance organs not usable for such eighteenth-century music, organs whose soft stops are all flutes and strings and vox humana, can never please the ears of effective imitative where color, rather than sparkle, is needed. Many sonatas are usable in part, at least, from those by Bach and Handel down to those by Brahms and modern composers.

by
Maynard Klein

Abundant Material

The great volume of choral production through past ages makes the problem of selecting music very difficult. What appears the best at the moment may not seem so later. With constant study, taste will change.

We present an outline of the most important periods of choral production, as well as a list of the most important types of compositions that should be included in a good choral library.

School Bands in Wartime

WILLIAM D. REVELLI

WITH THE PASSING of each succeeding day we find more and more of our high school, college, and university bands suffering from loss of leadership, student personnel, curricular conflicts, and numerous other difficulties brought about by the war. The oft-predicted shortage of school music teachers and conductors is now an actual and serious reality. Most of the schools have curtailed their musical activities in one form or another. In some instances, due to the lack of faculty personnel, it has been necessary to discontinue the instrumental program for the duration.

In addition to the loss of many teachers through the draft and enlistments, the indefinite draft status of musicians has also been responsible for many successful music educators leaving the teaching field for defense and war production occupations. These positions, in addition to being more attractive from a lucrative standpoint, are recognized by the government as deferable occupations definitely contributing to the winning of the war. As a result of these facts, the teaching personnel of our field has dwindled until the situation requires the consideration and attention of all those concerned with the maintenance of the instrumental program in our schools.

Student Shortage

While the shortage of teaching personnel has grossly affected the standards and activities of our school bands, the lack of student personnel is of even more serious significance. Today we find a large majority of high school students preoccupied with classes and worthy extra-curricular activities pertaining to the war program. Many of these boys and girls are attending high school on only a part-time schedule and thereby are availing themselves for part-time work in war production industries and other essential occupations. This situation, plus the drastic changes being made in the school curriculum, plus the emphasis centered on pre-induction courses, have made it necessary for many students to confine their school activities to strictly academic and military training courses.

Hence, at a period when school bands are in a position to make their greatest contribution to the community, state, and nation, they are experiencing great difficulties in maintaining sufficient personnel to function at all.

If such conditions were but temporary, then, perhaps we would have less reason for being alarmed. However, such might not be true. Should our school bands continue to lose their conductors, students, and curricular standing, then their status in the "post-war era" could well be difficult to re-establish.

A Difficult Situation

Should our bands continue to decrease by number and quality in the next year as they have in the past six months, then the diligent and effective work of the thousands of music educators who have built one of education's most outstanding monuments will have been in vain. This program, which has been universally recognized as having made a great contribution to the educational program of the nation, must not crumble. In spite of war, it must continue to function—yes, even more effectively today than ever before. Its future is in the hands of those teaching and conducting our school bands today.

Violins and Factory Fiddles

A Good Violin Necessary to a Good Student

by J. Wm. McGohan

MANY PERSONS have in their possession violins which have been in their families for several generations. Each of us, with a proud possessor, claims this fact with an enthusiastic declaration that his is a genuine Stradivarius, dated "Seventeen Hundred," and so on. As a matter of fact, most of these instruments are mere factory fiddles with little or no value. They have, printed in small type on separate labels at the bottom of the crude "Strad" labels, the statement "Made in Germany," or whatever the country in which they were made.

It is strange, indeed, that so many persons, otherwise fairly well educated, ignore the fact that Stradivari and most of his pupils worked in Cremona, Italy, and towns adjoining Cremona, also, that they fail to notice the cheap, hard, shellac-like varnish and poor grain of wood that invariably go with the average of the factory violins. The general excellence of the Cremona masterpieces had not spread very far among makers until the early part of the nineteenth century. At intervals there were for a time attempts to cheapen and reduce the price. In the last half century many cheap instruments appeared on the market. The craftsmen and the public suffered from this nefarious practice.

It is far better for a student to purchase a new instrument from a reputable dealer who sells the product of a modern maker, not ashamed to brand his work with his own name, than to take chances on the crude workmanship of doubtful origin. It is true that some factory-made instruments in the beginning are very good instruments in the beginning. It is also true that many of them can be corrected by expert violin-makers and made into fine violins. This used to be more by accident than by design. Many good, conscientious craftsmen today have a large experience in this work and no doubt many students who buy such violins have them rebuilt and in many instances the result is very satisfactory. I have known many violins which were considered worthless and absolutely unworthy of repair, which have been made into fine, delightful instruments by proper workmanship and artistic attention.

However, if an old violin comes into your hands and you have no expert friend who is a competent critic, have it examined by a competent, experienced maker. He will inform you as to the advisability of repairing the instrument. It may be worth the expense; it may be a total waste of money. Only by so doing can one be successful. If an artist cannot properly interpret "The Language of the Soul" on a poor violin, a pupil cannot hope to acquire even the fundamentals. A good teacher, a good instrument, and the love of music, and hard work are the four cornerstones of a lasting musical structure.

"I have quite a lot of trouble playing rapid passages in thirds, such as appear in the Sibelius "Concerto" and in Paganini's 26th Caprice (duo edition). It augurs well for your musical future, and I am sure you are no worse a soldier for your intelligent interest in violin playing.

Your question is valuable, for it brings up a principle that is essential to good practice and good teaching—none other, in fact, than the famous Napoleon principle of "Divide and Conquer"! In other words, most technical problems are the result, not of one single difficulty, but of the close association of two or more separate difficulties. A passage of thirds is an excellent example of this, for here three problems are combined: (1) the correct raising and depressing of the fingers in any one position; (2) the change of position on the same pair of strings; and (3) the change of positions when crossing strings. If you will devote twenty minutes a day for three months to mastering those separate difficulties, you would be able to play the passage of thirds with ease at the end of that time.

Your approach should be as follows: For the first month, practice in one position at a time, using each pair of strings and all possible combinations of major and minor thirds, for example,

[small musical examples and fingerings shown]

Use the first five positions—going higher on the A and E strings, if you wish—practicing very slowly at first, so that your hand may be both strong and relaxed. Later, gradually increase the speed. By the end of the month you should have developed a good double trill. Some combinations of thirds you will find more difficult than others; give special attention to these. During this month you must not allow yourself even one scale run—you must not be tempted to play a passage "to see if it has improved." If you will persevere you cannot fail. Then up every two or three days try the next group until you are sure of the combinations.

It will help if you write a list of the various combinations of thirds, and refer to it now and then. Always remember, when you are playing any exercise or passage in thirds, that your left thumb must be lying back along the underside of the neck—the knuckle of the first finger being free of the neck—and your elbow well under the shoulder. This position of the hand and arm is essential to develop the necessary coordination.

Practice shifting between the first and third, second and fourth, third and fifth positions, using each pair of strings and all possible combinations of major and minor intervals. Make sure, too, that the bowing is flexible and the wrist relaxed.

In an ascending passage, always have your first and third fingers over the strings on which they are to fall and the second and fourth fingers in a similar manner. Always keep your fingers as near the strings as possible. Here again use each pair of strings, various interval combinations, and all positions up to the fifth.

It is in this manner that thirds should be taught to young students—who usually have no real preparation for them until the Kreutzer "Studies" are taken up. This, together with the small type, should be practiced, on and off, until the two combined can be played with facility. Then practice the passage in the third type, spending at least three months on it before combining it with the other two. If this approach is followed, the results will be in direct proportion to the time he is ready for Kreutzer; indeed, he will probably be able to play them with almost automatic accuracy.

A Bow Arm "Tremor"

"For years I have been troubled with a "tremor" in my bow arm, and when practicing or in performances the arm will tremble or shake at the frog of the bow. Sometimes I find that incorrect posture is the cause, and again it occurs when I am fatigued. Can you advise me how to overcome this defect?—A.B., Ohio.

You are not alone in your difficulty. Many violinists have the same trouble, and some spend large sums of money with doctors and osteopaths trying to get rid of it. In nearly every case, the root of the trouble is in the player's bowing technique. It may be due to too much arm tension, an anxious, gripping attitude, or wrong weight distribution. If you will find a good teacher and work under his direction, you will soon correct the weakness. The technique should be simple and natural; keep the wrist flexible, the fingers alert, and the thumb free to act as a cushion. Avoid rigid arm action. Practice slow strokes on the whole bow up and down, keeping the arm relaxed and the wrist yielding. Gradually increase speed and length of stroke as control is gained.

Particularly pay attention to relaxation and flexibility in every exercise you try.

If you easily play a rapid detaché at the frog of the bow, using the wrist and fingers only, and keeping your elbow at the level of the frog? If not, you should develop it, starting slowly at first and being sure that your arm, though motionless, is not rigid or tense. Also you should hold the bow so that the top of the bow lifts off the string after each stroke, and using the momentary pause to relax hand and arm completely.

Have you a fluent control of the whole bow? Start with a passage which skips strings, for example take any of the Kreutzer studies; if you have any doubt, take the seventh study of Kreutzer or the eleventh of Mazas and play it in the following manner:

[short musical excerpt illustrated]

You should be able to produce a fiery accent and to take the entire length of the bow with great rapidity. If the results are not satisfactory, take about half the bow rapidly and then slow up somewhat for the last portion. The accent should be clean and not produced by sudden jerk of the wrist. As you play the bow in separate strokes, keep the hand at the speed level of the stroke, not the wrist; the arm should be steady and the fingers flexible. After three or four repetitions the movement should become automatic and the tremor should disappear.

In addition to the foregoing exercises which deal with technique, be sure that you do not hold a mental tension. Your musical thought must be calm. When you begin to practice, put the instrument aside and do some physical exercises for ten minutes to relax the muscles of the right arm. Shake your arm gently down at your side and then let it hang loosely. Breathe deeply and evenly. These simple measures will help to free the arm from nervous tension and should be done before every practice period.

In general I should say that if you have a good teacher and can put in at least an hour of concentrated practice each day, by the end of a year you might be able to play many tunes, folk songs, and popular selections common to the fairs and on the social programs of the day. You should, however, set small goals with the required practice and thus be encouraged. But if you expect to attain virtuoso ability in a short time you will be disappointed.

A. Your case is typical of thousands, and I advise you and any others like you who may happen to read this reply to give music a good try. People used to think no one could learn. Piano playing is a fine discipline that calls for faith, patient work, and daily increments. There is no reason to give up. I have seen many men, women, and aging who early part of life took it up seriously and achieved a measure of accomplishment that brought them much happiness.

Piano or Harp

Q. Will you kindly give me an honest answer to this question? Can one woman as well as men become concert artists? I am fifteen years of age and have been studying both piano and harp. The time has come when I must concentrate on one instrument and each of my teachers says I can play on the opposite instrument. So how I am to decide? — Name withheld.

A. The answer to your question is definitely yes. Women can be and are concert artists. But if one is an excellent young musician who has a notion to play the harp, I like the low instrument equally well. It is not the instrument under which the musician will make his reputation but the great gifts of the musician. Your questions are essentially as follows: (1) Can a woman become an artist and (2) Which shall I study, piano or harp? When the first question is answered no equivocal yes. Plenty of women have been or are concert artists, and if you have the requisite musical and intellectual ability and if you have the character and determination to sacrifice almost everything else to a career, then you may look forward to being a public pianist.

The second part of your question is much more difficult and it cannot be answered so categorically. It is like asking me which is better, a horse or a cow, and of course I should reply to such a question that it depends on what you want from the animal. Likewise in the case of harp and piano. They are quite different and each has its own peculiar function. The harp is more apt to be used in an orchestra, it is full of romance, it is difficult enough to challenge the fingers and the ear. But the solo literature for the harp is decidedly limited compared with the piano, and its capacity to evoke deep emotional expression is smaller than that of the piano because the piano has far more sonorities for something unique and each of these instruments has advantages that the other does not have and you must decide on the one or the other solely after coming to know them both and what their other uses for the modern artists. Then your decision will give you the greatest possible satisfaction.

An Instrument for Fun?

Q. I would like to buy a piano for home use only. I have played a little and would like some instrument that isn't too expensive and yet will be musical to learn on. I am not thinking of playing professionally. What would you advise? — A. S.

A. There is a marvelous instrument in a great many homes and it is called the spinet. You may prefer an upright, but for domestic use a modest spinet will be adequate. Get one well regulated and tuned.

The Process of Weight Release
in Piano Playing

by Tobias Matthay

Eminent English Teacher of Pianoforte

I HAVE BEEN ASKED to answer certain questions relative to "weight-touch" in pianoforte playing, and gladly do so.

Some recent writers have mistakenly essayed to go further than I have done in my books, and have gone so far out of their way to try to make other believe that one can influence the operation of a muscle by focusing one's thought upon that muscle. To me, this strongly suggests "eye-wash." Many students, however, do want to know how the actual process of "weight-release" is effected and how it is gauged. Also they would like to know how this process gives such amazing certainty of control.

Well, the first thing to realize definitely is that one can never provoke a muscle to contract or relax by commanding the muscle to act. If you are not so convinced, try it yourself. Permitting your forearm to rest comfortably upon a table. Now, command the muscles to move, or command any particular muscle to move. You will soon discover that it is as impossible to cause movement directly as it is to cause the pupil of your eyes to grow smaller or larger through commanding them to obey you.

Now, with your arm in the same position, command your forefinger to rise. Instantly it obeys your will, and you suddenly realize that you are dealing with members such as your fingers, your hands, your forearm, your arm, and that the muscles which operate them coordinate in the marvelous way designed by nature.

Anatomy or Pedagogy?

Notwithstanding all this we find writers of books on piano playing, and lecturers upon the art, talking learnedly and pedantically upon this or that muscle. This may give the student a kind of pseudo-knowledge of anatomy, but it will not improve his piano playing one whit. Therefore it is quite useless and, in fact harmful, to try to think of this or that particular muscle or muscle which are concerned in piano playing. Of the million-and-one things with which the music student must concern himself, please, please do not ask him to carry along the burden of a complicated knowledge of muscles. One modern author on technique goes so far as to rely on pictures of the muscles implicated. It may be interesting to the lay reader, but it is quite useless pedagogically.

An Act of Omission

Weight-release is effected by an act of omission. Omit, leave out, eliminate—to the required extent—the up-holding action of the arm for the duration of any descent. Thus the arm then forms a weight; the brief momentary action of finger and hand resisting the descending weight tends to ease not necessary but the least impediment any movement during the combined process. Obviously it gives certainty and ease, because it eliminates all disturbing and ineffective action, uses and thus the hand becomes more accurate.

With regard to "weight versus muscular initiative" I have said before that movements I do not require the pupil to move the instrument except in cases of persistent stiffness, when persistent study will right them.

Indeed, it is futile to try to teach touch, actions, or any other gymnastic proficiency, by providing the pupil with what may be the most precise possible catalogue of the localities of the concerned muscles, since it is impossible for us to induce any munce to act by trying directly to will its action, and we can only obtain its cooperation by willing the requisite limb-actions and relaxations. Knowledge of the required exercises and possibilities of the various portions of the limb is the only knowledge that will help us. All we can do is to learn to recognize and remember the sensations which accompany the desirable and undesirable changes of limb motion. That is the only way, and it is therefore a matter of Psychology all along.

How, then, do we effect weight-release? It is a matter of eliminating to the proper degree the muscular action which holds up the arms. For instance, to exert the thumb inwards, it is of no use making yourself aware that it is the muscle the base of the thumb that helps in the process; the only result will be that you will stiffen the limb! The main reason why "weight-touch" is so effective is that its use eliminates stiffness and, therefore, defeatsness in our actions.

Music and Study

WHEN THE MUSICAL HISTORY of our times comes to be written, an important and colourful section will be devoted to the modern ballet. Our pictorial, dancing age is one of light and color, music and motion, as never before; and at the turn of the century the keynote of this development was struck by the magical achievements of Diaghileff and his Russian Ballet.

True, the coming of the screen-arts with technicolour, speech, and music have enormously augmented popular interest in the combined arts; but without offense to the movies far it may be truly said that the dancing incidental to such productions emphasises the physical and acrobatic aspects, while modern ballet attempts to maintain those higher levels in which dancing and mime interpret life and subject matter of the theatres. The difference between these two levels reveals itself in the music appropriate to each. So far the screen has produced nothing to equal the ballets of Stravinsky, Ravel, and others, following the earlier achievements of Delibes, Tchaikowsky, Borodine, and their kind.

Two elements distinguish the modern ballet as initiated by Diaghileff: First, the imaginative collaboration of all the arts and artists concerned in preparation and production; and, second, the special emphasis placed on music as the inspirational source, rather than as the servile accompaniment of spectacle or star-performance. This differentiates modern ballet from that of the past as well as from the screen art.

The principles of modern ballet were laid down by Michel Fokine, Diaghileff's choreographer, who was an able musician as well as a dancer. He was, while still a student in St. Petersburg, "Dancing should be interpretative. It should not degenerate into mere gymnastics. . . . For such interpretative dancing, the music must be equally inspired." And again: "The ballet must have complete unity of expression, a unity which is made up of harmonious blending of the three elements—music, painting, and the plastic arts." These and other precepts were ignored at the time by the Imperial Theatres in St. Petersburg (Leningrad), but are now accepted wholeheartedly by such painters and the world-famous, by Stravinsky, and by Nijinsky, Anna Pavlova, and others.

The Inspiration for "Petrouchka"

Many of the ballets originated in a musical idea. Stravinsky tells how the idea for "Petrouchka" came to him while composing a concerted work of another kind. "I had in mind a distinct picture of a puppet suddenly endowed with life, experiencing the passion of the orchestra with diabolic and comic ardor," he writes. In truth realisers of music to the dance were forced to recreate the theatre in their minds; the music of Diaghileff's Ballets Russes—orchestrated for stage purposes—was not only to provide motives and atmosphere but to create the dramatic flow and accent essential in the composite art. In many of the earlier ballets music and scenario were equally the work of the impresario, usually Diaghileff himself. Diaghileff believed that harmony and hospitality when used together could give a composition that unity of detail which the choreography in the theatre and in the small areas of stage alike produced.

The history of the ballet in the days of Louis XIV has been, until recently, somewhat vague. It is largely trans- lated in small books called "Ballet," Arnold Haskell says. "Music has been its score, the drama its book, and the paintings of the past can be seen on the walls of museums and in the collections of private houses." The origin of the modern ballet, like the music of the modern drama, is to be sought in the folkloric and historical past and in the national dance of France. Ballet take many forms and varies from one country to another.

Its original classical or mythological period followed by Romantic, National, Impressionist periods, and so forth. It came into association with opera, especially in France, but also had a life of its own, fostered by an Academy of Dancing founded by Louis XIV, later merged into the school attached to the Grand Opera.

Certain conventions had to be established or accepted, and steps such as the pirouette and the toe dance gave the ballet its own kind of virtuosity. In time, the star-system came in and, as in opera, at the expense of the music. This was often selected at random to suit the exhibitions of dancers. There was little thought for unity of design or of feeling. Such ballets were mere entertainments, akin to musical comedy or vaudeville.

At first all dancers were male and often masked. Ladies of the court had danced at Versailles, but their example was not immediately followed. Costumes for women were a problem, for hoop-skirts were in vogue and ladies' underwear was mostly hardware. This was a weighty consideration in dancing, and simulations of aerial flight forced the use of pulleys and wires before fairies or angels could get off the ground. Innovations were resented on grounds of purity, but skirts were gradually shortened and relieved of ballast until at last some Malliot dared to introduce tights. These met with such favor that even the Pope permitted them in theatres under his jurisdiction. His Holiness insisted, however, that the legs of the dancer should be avoided in favor of a celestial blue. No really satisfactory costume for ballerinas was achieved until the American Isadora Duncan introduced flowing draperies and bare feet.

The Turning Point

The turning-point in ballet history came in 1830 when "La Sylphide" (not to be confused with mm. Lamlaine's "Les Sylphides") ushered in the romantic movement. It was the first of many ballets in which sylphs, fauns, satyrs and other super-natural beings mingled in the affairs of mortals. This was followed in the fifties and sixties by family ballets, and it was to this period that "Giselle" belongs, with music by Adolphe Adam. It is the only important ballet of this period to come down to us intact and it has not become the "flawless" of the dancing repertory. All ballerinas aspire to enact the role the story was provided by no less than Theophile Gautier, and was first produced at the Theatre de l'Académie Royale de Musique, with music by Adolphe Adam. The story of the drowned peasant girl's revenge on her faithless lover is the standard dramatic form of the romantic ballet and it, with other ballets of the period, preserved many of the traditions of court ballet.

Adolphe (-Charles) Adam (1803-1856) in his own day in Paris was one of the most successful writers of light operas, the best known of which was "Le Postillon de Longjumeau," which is still heard in the French capital. Adam taught at the Paris Conservatoire for several years. His best known work is his Cantique de Noël (O Holy Night), which appears here in an effective piano arrangement. Grade 4. Prelude in B Major (No. 11)
by Frédéric Chopin

THE CHOPIN PRELUDES are like an artist's portfolio of finished and unfinished sketches, soft pastels, sharp line drawings, vivid characterizations, brilliant splashes of color—miscellaneous memoranda of all kinds. One of the briefest sketches, the Prelude in B major, is like a falling star dissolved into tone, its iridescent beauty, alas, too swiftly melted. At first its contours must be etched with utmost clarity, giving way in the end to soft, shimmering star-darkness. Only in the final measures is the slightest hint of slowing-up permissible; Chopin himself has not indicated a ritardando. Here the music itself slows up, both in design and notation; consequently, an added ritardando is not only unnecessary but reprehensible.

Although the Prelude is to be played legato, it should also be practiced very lightly with bright non-leggiero touch, and with featherweight elbow tip. This will insure clarity and ease versus the muddiness and "squeeze" resulting from the excessive overlapping legato advocated by some editions. For further smoothness I advise practicing the Prelude in three-four meter as well as in six-eight.

Much of the charm of the Prelude comes from subtle rubato, such as playing the second-measure motive and its repetitions in Measures 6, 15, and 22 with slight hesitations; and be sure to reiterate very sensitively this lovely motive in the third measure which, with variations, appears no less than eleven times:

It is physically unsound and musically unwise to strive for range for its own sake. Watch the middle voice carefully, and the range will seem to develop as if by magic. Further, there must be perfect evenness between the different registers of range. If the least break shows in proceeding from low to middle registers, or from middle to high, basic vocal emission is faulty.

Breath Conservation Important
"The other point which I believe to be essential is the capacity and the budgeting of the breath. Capacity can be developed by sound breathing exercises—which, incidentally, should be practiced before any work in actual singing is begun. The matter of budgeting the breath requires careful thought. The singer should learn to analyze each song he sings and to discover just where its climax lies. Both musically and emotionally, each song builds up to a climax. Determine where this climax occurs, and build towards it, releasing most breath at its peak, and keeping the preceding and subsequent phrases in balanced proportion. This, of course, requires thought. The actual matter of how much breath to give on each phrase depends, of course, on individual interpretation as well as on individual capacity. On the whole, however, I may say that the firmer the support of the breath, the nearer the emissions become. Again, the vocalization should ride on the firmly supported breath, naturally and freely. Never should the breath be any pushing or forcing, beyond the natural bellows-like push of the great abdominal muscles themselves. The trick, perhaps, is to learn to sing, not with the breath but over it, in a large, free arching of tone that feels perfectly relaxed. This can be accomplished only when the basic support of the breath is firm and sure. Singing over the breath makes for clear, free, well-resonated tone, singing with the breath makes for shaky, uneven, uncontrolled tone.

"The wise vocalist begins each day's work with scales—and he sings them early in the day, before he has done much talking or any other singing, and while the voice is still fresh, natural, and free. The vocalizing that is done on a fresh voice counts for just twice as much help to the voice as any other.

"As to the ultimate success one achieves in singing, I believe that complete individuality of approach wins half the battle. That includes a number of other points! First of all, the singer must explore the history and the tradition of every song he studies. He must do more than sing the song—he must reveal its basic idea. This, of course, presupposes that he has the idea well in hand himself. By exploring types, styles, and kinds of music, then, the singer gradually learns which forms belong to him and which are alien to his interpretative and musical powers. In other words, he learns his own limitations. This is of the greatest importance. The young singer must explore all kinds of music before he is in a position to know where his strongest (and weakest) points lie. And once he has learned what he can and cannot do to advantage, he should profit from this discovery. One of the greatest mistakes a singer can make is to attempt a song because it is "the rage" or because some other singer makes a great success with it—if it is not essentially his own song. Build your career in terms of your own powers rather than according to the pattern of someone else's success. It is a known historic fact that one of the greatest crops of ruined tenor voices in vocal history followed the Caruso era—because other tenors, who had different powers and abilities from those of Caruso, persisted in using their voices and singing the songs as he did. Not that there was anything basically wrong in Caruso's methods—quite the reverse! But the methods that suited him were unique, as was his voice and his art; others, in imitating him, passed the frontiers of their own limitations and got lost.

"In all branches of vocal work—learn to think clearly and then to proceed with philosophy!"

Of course, when it is done for its own sake, we can carry it too far; but unanimity is all-important in choral work. "Prepare for the attack!" warns Roberton further. "Mentalize the note and the word and come on them both together! Many singers who know the note are quite indifferent about the word. The result is a foozled start."

Indeed, the effect on the audience is ragged in the extreme, and the resulting attitude on the part of the chorus soon is evident in the assumption—"any old thing will do." If the opening of a song is sure, confidence is immediately generated in the part of the singers, and smoothness and rhythm and coordination will naturally follow. Unanimity has a very strong bearing on the exact ensemble. The proper use of vowels and consonants, of expansion and contraction of tone, of unanimity of attack, and of movement and release is a preëminent element of choir singing.

The upward intervals, do-re and do-la, in invariably tend to flatten. Even with choirs accustomed to singing in tune, occasions will arise when, for no apparent reason, the choruses gets out of control. Then we should see that all ascending scale passages are sufficiently sharpened. Or as Fathter Firm would say, "Keep practice between ascending tones and a wide, staccato descending tones narrow." In discussing this phase of singing, Dr. Cole makes a point that is as expressive of joy: do-fa of a consonant; do-sol of power and do-la of expectation. But there are musicians who might differ with him on this.

The last cause of flatting—the most pernicious and common of our entire list—is the tremolo. Such a defect can ruin the entire effect of smoothly blended voices in choral singing. A pleading voice may be all right in a solo situation, but the human voice is a wonderful an instrument to be used with great care. When properly trained the choir can be an extraordinarily expressive instrument. But one must be constantly on guard and cultivate a sure attack, a full, homogeneous tone and the utmost regard for intonation. It is this which makes all the difference between a fine choir and a simply ordinary one. We should control single tones as carefully as other instruments; and the choir must practice intelligently, not in slabs or snippets, but in musical sense.

VOICE QUESTIONS
Answered by DR. NICHOLAS DOUTY

No question will be answered in THE ETUDE unless accompanied by the full name and address of the inquirer. Only initials, or pseudonyms given, will be published.

Should the Teacher and the Pupil
Have the Same Type of Voice?
Q. If a person has an alto voice is it better to choose a teacher having an alto voice or is it better to choose a teacher having the same voice as the pupil? This, of course, applies to high or low voices. — Mrs. J. D.
A. A good teacher, one who understands the basic principles of singing and who can impart them to his pupils, should be helpful in any type of voice. However, some pupils can only by imitated. Especially at the beginning of study, a teacher who characterizes the voice in such a fashion as to have a voice similar to his own is a better instructor than is a teacher who does not. Because the pupil is apt to imitate the faults of his mentor as well as his virtues.

Two Letters Concerning Smoking.
Is It Bad for the Voice?
Q. I have heard that smoking is bad for the voice. Is this true if the singer is not a heavy smoker, but takes only an occasional smoke?—Mrs. G. W.
A. I would advise any one who sings to avoid smoking to a greater extent. However, it is the degree of smoking that is important. Persons who are habitual smokers often have congested throats and irritated vocal cords. The worry of frequent coughing and throat clearing may interfere with the steadiness and the general health of the vocal organs.

I Am Tenor, But I Am in Doubt.
Which Voice Should I Take?
Q. Am I an occasional tenor who will certainly do the better work if I become a baritone? Or, must I become a light tenor? I have been training for tenor and all my songs are in the high voice. I am 25 years old and my voice is well developed. My teacher thinks I could go either way. — L. S.
A. An occasional singer will certainly do the better work if he changes his voice into the one for which his physical equipment is most suited. This usually means he should sing the voice that offers the most ease. If you have been singing highest notes with facility, and if your timbre is bright and ringing, you are probably a true tenor. If you have been training hard and your top voice has been forced, and you now feel at home in the middle register, you might be a baritone. You must avoid hasty conclusions. The line between voices is not always distinct, and often is narrow.

Pronunciation in Singing
Q. Is it right to use the short I pronounced as in "hit" for sung English words? I have been told it is wrong to use this short sound and that the long E should be used. — G. A. T.
A. Vowel sounds in singing should be given in pure form as much as possible. The short I (as in hit) is used in some words; some teachers advocate the wider or more open sound for singers. It is not absolutely necessary to use the long E for all occasions, but to sing in a style of uniform vowel production is of prime importance. The vowel used must be in accordance with the language and with the style of the piece.

When Voice Teachers Should Sing
for Pupils
Q. Is it necessary for a teacher to sing before pupils at lessons? Some teachers sing at every lesson while others never sing at all. What is the best method? — Miss M. K.
A. It is desirable for teachers to sing occasionally in order to demonstrate tone, phrasing, and musical ideas. However, a teacher should not overuse this privilege, for the teacher who sings constantly may hinder the pupil's independence. Demonstration is good when needed.

Is It Wise to Sing Under Muscle Strain?
Q. Is it true that when a singer sings under nervous tension, he will go sharp or flat? I find that when I am nervous I tend to go sharp. Is this due to muscle strain? — L. R.
A. Nervous tension frequently causes the singer to go sharp, because of extra muscular tension which shortens the vocal cords slightly and raises the pitch. It is essential to relax and to sing with natural support.

Whistling
Q. I am fourteen—two years on age, have studied with various teachers and have had a few exhibitions on the amateur programs. I whistle a little, but some people say this is an impediment to the voice. Is it true?— J. R.
A. There is a decided difference in whistling. Some whistle as a hobby and are not hurt by it; others whistle with excessive force which may irritate the throat. In general, moderate whistling does not harm the voice provided the throat is not strained.

The Need for the Church Orchestra
In a small but enterprising California church, visitors often remark, “Why is the music of this church so much more thrilling than that of my church at home? It sounds so different?” The answer is in the fact that choir, organ, and congregation are supported by an orchestra which is an integral factor in the service. The director is one of those rare men who catches the imagination of students in school on weekdays and in church on Sundays, and who knows how to blend voices and instruments in cooperative music-making.

Why is it that this does not happen more often in American churches? There are three reasons: First, the domination of the organ, already described, aided and abetted by architects who build organ galleries with barely enough room for some singers, much less an orchestra.

Second, the over-departmentalizing of music education, with a cappella choirs that scorn accompaniments, organists who aim to be virtuosi, and prize-winning school orchestras and bands that never stoop to accompany anyone.

Third, and most important of all, the old puritanical prejudices against instruments in church. And these prejudices are not merely those handed down to us by the Calvinist Puritans and Pilgrims who thought the violin was the devil’s instrument; these prejudices are also enforced by the Anglican or Episcopal Puritans who never tolerate instruments other than the organ, and by the Catholic Puritans who follow the dictates of Rome in excluding strings, reeds, and brass from the sanctuary.

The historical fact is that symphonies and chamber music originated and flourished in music centers where the church orchestras were as important as the opera orchestras. Bach’s orchestra was almost as important as his choir in the Thomaskirche at Leipzig. We have to thank the Lutherans for their unique liberality in admitting instruments to the choir loft. Theirs were not the only church orchestras, however. The medieval church rang with the sounds of trumpets and all manner of instruments. The richness of Catholic orchestras in European churches was always notable—outside of the Vatican. The farther North and West, the bigger the orchestras, especially in the baroque services of Venice, Salzburg, Vienna, Dresden, and Paris. This was true in England also until the Puritans banned “popish” music and substituted psalm-tunes. There was a revival of church orchestras after the Restoration, but the aesthetic Puritans of eighteenth-century England threw them out again.

Americans, for the most part, have retained the old prejudices of European puritans, to the great detriment of our musical life. Even the American Lutherans have lost the old ancestral zest for “a joyful noise.” A brass choir, even on Easter, would scandalize the Lutherans as much as any other puritans.

The aesthetic puritans are the worst of all; to exalt a “baroque” organ without a baroque orchestra, to give it some life and vitality, shows historical ignorance and musical stupidity. Our organists, directors, choir directors, and music committees should read with profit the Chronicles. To see how inspiring a musical service could be in the days of earlier masters, the organ galleries would open new vistas. But if you indulge in exclamations of this kind: “Now! that F—sharp; watch your time; hold that note longer; F—natural in the left hand; third finger on G—sharp,” you will create chaos in the pupil’s mind, and he will forget most of your comments before he reaches home.

When necessary to stop a pupil in the middle of a piece, it is better to have him start again at the beginning of the section in which he was playing. Never permit a pupil to change an incorrect G—natural to G—sharp and go on from there, because this creates a wrong progression.

For at least a week before a public performance a teacher should be wary of criticism. Last-minute corrections are likely to disturb the whole of a pupil and may result in a breakdown at the recital. It is especially important to avoid making a suggestion that will conflict with the way the pupil has practiced the selection. Unless something is radically wrong, it is better not to attempt a change at such a time. Polishing touches are more dangerous, but they can often be administered with care.

Don’t Wreck—Build

Thus we see that an educator can usually chart the progress of his pupils with greater ease by the use of kindly, constructive, and encouraging criticism. But it is equally necessary to teach the pupil to use the editorial eye. If he is to progress he must learn to correct himself and to know when to stop. No amount of external criticism will ever take the place of the pupil’s own intelligent self-criticism.

It is often wise to stress the good qualities of a pupil’s work before mentioning the poor ones. Praise when genuine can do far more than constant fault-finding. If the pupil feels that the criticism is intended to help, he will respond to it. If not, it may be resented and thus defeat its own purpose.

We must always remember that the end in view is not merely the perfect performance of a piece but the development of the pupil as a musician. That being the case, the teacher who teaches the art of self-correction and self-expression will render the greatest service. To demand perfection in every detail all the time is to discourage and to stunt growth. A wise teacher will build toward perfection little by little, and will encourage the pupil to love the music as much as to play it correctly.

If we do these things, we shall have fewer broken-down performers and more true musicians.

ORGAN AND CHOIR QUESTIONS
Answered by HENRY S. FRY, Mus. Doc.
Ex-Dean of the Pennsylvania Chapter of the A. G. O.

No question will be answered in THE ETUDE unless accompanied by the full name and address of the inquirer. Only initials, or pseudonyms, given, will be published. Naturally, in fairness, to all friends and advertisers, we can express no opinion as to the relative merits or quality of various instruments.

Q. I am a very young (fifteen) assistant organist in a small church, but my work is important to me because of the choir. The organist has been sent to a sanatorium for a nervous breakdown, and the choir is in a sad way. The vestry wants me to continue and thinks I am older than I am. I have only been in my present position about three months, and I have taken very little formal training. I have been used to the piano and harmonium only. My problem is: what would you do with a hymn; should you play the old hymn tune or the setting; should you use a pedal or not? Also, what is the best way to accompany the choir—what registrations should be used? Would you advise reeds? —E. P., N. J.

A. You are in a trying position, but you should keep calm and accept the kindness of your vestry. Play only such things as you feel quite sure of. In most small churches simplicity is the best course. Unless asked to use elaborate registrations, play the hymn in the way that best supports the choir and congregation. Pedal should be used when the bass of the hymn tune is strong and when it will not swamp the choir. For accompaniment, an 8-foot foundation with a soft 8-foot and a quiet 4-foot for the upper voices is usually satisfactory. Reeds should be used sparingly, if at all; a subduing reed effect on soft verses and the addition of a Trumpet or Oboe for a firm chorus may be effective. Above all, do not attempt things you have not the technical control for. The idea is to make the hymn serve the words and the congregation, not to show off the organist.

Q. I have just been offered the position of organist-choirmaster in a small church. The choir is weak but willing, and there is an organ which is fairly new and in good condition. I would like your advice about the organizing and training of a choir. Shall I insist upon reading music, or try to get them to learn by ear? I have not the time for many rehearsals. —W. T., Pa.

A. Learn to work with the materials you have. Insist upon correct pitch and good tone production. Teach the parts by ear if it is necessary at first, but gradually introduce sight-reading and the use of printed music. Rehearse regularly and economically. Use simple anthems and motets at first, and choose hymns that the congregation can sing. Encourage friendly social intercourse among your singers to maintain morale. Above all, be patient.

Q. I have been asked to become a member of the American Guild of Organists but am not sure whether the membership is worth while. What are the advantages of joining? —R. B., Md.

A. Membership in the Guild is a professional asset. It brings you into association with colleagues, furnishes opportunities for professional improvement, and gives you status before churches, schools and the public. The Guild sponsors examinations and certification, maintains standards, publishes information, and conducts local activities that are educational and helpful.

"Basically, there are three kinds

Thereafter, geography played a

The Piano When Peace Comes

pianos in fine or fair condition in the United States. The very best care should be taken of these instruments and teachers everywhere should assume the responsibility of promoting music as well as at times, so that there will be no unused or "dead" pianos at a time when every instrument should be employed.

Piano manufacture requires a scientific type of artisan or technician who cannot be produced in a fortnight. It is important for the finely trained technicians for the industry gradually to educate new and younger types of men and women to carry on the craft which has come into being through some two centuries of growth.

Of equal importance is that the teachers of music realize the great opportunity which is confronting them. Literally millions of workers, young and old, who all their lives have aspired to a knowledge of music, now find themselves for the first time with the means to pay for music lessons. Music teachers should work unceasingly, night and day, to keep up interest in this group. The largest group of accessions to this field of music comes from children in the early school age; that is, from five to twelve years. The child who has had enough musical training in this period to secure a good grounding in music develops, in many instances, a musical devotion for a lifetime. The very parents who cannot sing or play realize that they have lost by their neglect, and, with a right kind of presentation from the teacher, will start their children in music before the high school period arrives, with its multiplicity of demands upon the pupil's spare time. If the pupil is well started in music he will find musical opportunities in high school, and it is less likely that he will be turned away from his music by so-called amusements and pleasures, which sometimes result in tragedy and years of remorse to the very parents who have protested, "Why that couldn't happen to my child!"

A Startling Disclosure

Recently the Federal Bureau of Investigation has been pointing very definitely to the startling criminal tendencies of the children in the pre-

high school age. The unheard of behavior of these youngsters also has been a serious problem to teachers and parents. It is very certain that children who have had the benefit of a good musical training in the home are more likely to escape the dangers that have been created by the exploitation of thugs and loose characters in some papers and some movies. Music study is one of the finest "holds" upon the character of a child which these all-too-hectic times provide. Moreover, music opens social opportunities which may be very precious to lifetime assets of the son and daughter. Parenthood means something far more than merely providing food, clothing, and shelter. "Have you ever watched a mother bird teaching a fledgling how to fly? The wise parent provides for the time that the child grows into those days when it must leave the nest and fly with its own wings. Music has helped many a young man and young woman into a rise to a fairer position in life."

In the "New World" that scientists and inventors sanguinely tell us we may expect after the war, we cannot foresee how much the piano may be affected. For instance, we hear incessantly of the employment of plastics in manufacture. Up to this time the wide use of plastics in piano manufacture has been none too promising because their cost is far greater and the advantages offered by the new materials are not sufficient to justify large increase in cost. The piano is a distinctive and well stabilized instrument and has available a truly marvelous literature, written expressly for it. The popular demand for pianos in various designs and shapes does not change the main principles of a string percussion instrument with a sonorous tone, which may be very "piano" or very "forte" at the will of the player. The tone, once sounded by the blow of the hammer, diminishes until it dies out. When this great literature of the piano is heard on other instruments, it takes on an entirely different character and complexion. The sustaining pedal which releases a flood of sympathetic harmonies, making a background for the marvellous contrapuntal, is entirely distinctive with the piano.

Electronic Developments

Many people have been intrigued and bewildered by new inventions, and have accepted as normal and beautiful, electrically produced musical tones which, if measured by standards of real musical quality, would not be accepted. The deficiency in radio is usually in the loud-speaker as well as in the operator's adjustment of the apparatus. We are all called upon now and then to listen to amplifiers and public address systems which make the voice of the speaker quite unrecognizable.

Already we have electronic instruments producing tones close to some of the good tones of a fine pipe organ, and also other tones of a highly distinctive character. These instruments are being sold at a much lower cost than a pipe organ of similar power and scope. Organists naturally turn to the full pipe organ, but these electrotonic instruments satisfy thousands at a great economic advantage.

It is probable that some day an electronic piano will be developed which will have advantages over the present instrument either technically or economic, or both, as has been the case with some other musical instruments, notably the pipe organ. To date this has not been the case. No loud-speaker on the general market seems to be able to reproduce faithfully the range and variety of harmonics produced by the piano with its string-percussion tone, great variety of impact tones, and rapid fade-away of the string vibration.

The tone of the electronic piano, when measured as a piano tone, is inferior to that of an ordinary piano, and the electronic instrument costs considerably more than an inexpensive piano. You see, the economic situation is reversed from that of the pipe organ.

The piano is the evolution of centuries. It is not a claptrap device which has come into existence like a mushroom. It has a permanence and a stability which insure the belief that the future of the instrument is for reaching and secure. It is marvelously adjustable to totally different types of music. Bach and Handel are far removed from Debussy and Ravel, yet a fine piano accommodates itself to the music of both types of composers. It also satisfies those who choose to use it as an anvil on which to pound out bourgeois-vogue.

Will pianos be cheaper when peace comes? I have seen nothing in mechanical development, materials, or processes which leads me to believe that good pianos will be cheaper after the war than they were before Pearl Harbor. Value for value, the piano is one of the least expensive of musical instruments. A good piano stands an enormous amount of wear and tear. Considering the years that it lasts and considering the price, a piano costs about one-fifth as much per year of use as an automobile. Take a piano which costs eighteen hundred dollars and lasts thirty years; the "use-year" cost is sixty dollars a year. An automobile costing eighteen hundred dollars is usually ready for the junk heap at the end of six years. Its use-year cost is, therefore, three hundred dollars.

The piano, after the war, will come into higher, artistic, educational, and sociological eminence than seems inevitable.

It is probable that some day an electronic piano will be developed which will have advantages over the present instrument either technically or economic, or both, as has been the case with some other musical instruments, notably the pipe organ. To date this has not been the case. No loud-speaker on the general market seems to be able to reproduce faithfully the range and variety of harmonics produced by the piano with its string-percussion tone, great variety of impact tones, and rapid fade-away of the string vibration.

The tone of the electronic piano, when measured as a piano tone, is inferior to that of an ordinary piano, and the electronic instrument costs considerably more than an inexpensive piano. You see, the economic situation is reversed from that of the pipe organ.

The piano is the evolution of centuries. It is not a claptrap device which has come into existence like a mushroom. It has a permanence and a stability which insure the belief that the future of the instrument is for reaching and secure. It is marvelously adjustable to totally different types of music. Bach and Handel are far removed from Debussy and Ravel, yet a fine piano accommodates itself to the music of both types of composers. It also satisfies those who choose to use it as an anvil on which to pound out bourgeois-vogue.

Will pianos be cheaper when peace comes? I have seen nothing in mechanical development, materials, or processes which leads me to believe that good pianos will be cheaper after the war than they were before Pearl Harbor. Value for value, the piano is one of the least expensive of musical instruments. A good piano stands an enormous amount of wear and tear. Considering the years that it lasts and considering the price, a piano costs about one-fifth as much per year of use as an automobile. Take a piano which costs eighteen hundred dollars and lasts thirty years; the "use-year" cost is sixty dollars a year. An automobile costing eighteen hundred dollars is usually ready for the junk heap at the end of six years. Its use-year cost is, therefore, three hundred dollars.

The piano, after the war, will come into higher, artistic, educational, and sociological eminence than seems inevitable.

Building a Choral Library
(Continued from Page 831)

The case may be that “we cannot see the forest because of the trees,” but we honestly feel that many of the things sung in our choirs have no inspiration from an artistic standpoint. This is a strong statement, to be sure, but it can be easily proved by a slight inspection of some of the material offered for our groups to buy and sing.

Are good things being published? The answer is yes! But an endless amount of time must be spent in seeking out the best. Music cannot be selected by a list of rules alone. The one making the selection must possess musicianship and taste, the ability which defines cataloging. However, a few questions may be of help in choosing music now when so great an amount is being produced in the name of patriotism. Is it the result of honest inspiration? Is it musically sincere and convincing? Is the text worth while? Are American idioms being used? Does the choral writing follow the best traditions? Will the composition satisfy the demand of sound music education and at the same time do the things which is expected of a singer during war? Only through tireless searching will these questions be answered in the affirmative.

American Choral Works

It is comparatively easy to select music from the past, as time has eliminated much of the things which are unworthy of attention. Making a selection from the contemporary production is more difficult, since the choice depends on the help which the passing of time will give. We are enthusiastic about the performance of our American choral works (and our choirs give several Southern first-performances each year), but with the active time so short in the average choir, who can doubt our groups as being up-to-date? There are so many excellent modern choral works by American composers that the difficulty is one of selection. Works by Randall Thompson, Aaron Copland, Samuel Barber and others deserve careful consideration. Much fine choral material has been published during the last few years and the progressive director will make an effort to secure as much of this as possible for his library.

About Arrangements

It is now necessary to discuss the type of choral music which has maintained the greatest space on amateur and school programs: arrangements. Arrangements of all sorts — folksongs, solo songs, operatic selections, madrigals, spirituals, sections of instrumental compositions, and almost every other type of composition which was ever conceived — it is in the selection of these arrangements that the choir director should be most particular. Unfortunately, a great deal of study and understanding are necessary before a wise selection can be made. The composer’s name alone should not be considered. Voices should be chosen with good judgment. We do not recommend the use of arrangements, aside from folksongs, anthem or standard pieces. Great care must be exercised in using only those arrangements of folksongs which have been made by men who have a feeling for the native folk-song style and who have conserved the spirit of the original. In many of the popular arrangements, because of a desire to produce something colorful, the original spirit is lost. The voice producing a modern effect will frequently erase the simple charm of the original and this is a mistake.

Service Music

Much of the service music now in use is the best medium for obtaining choral ability which conforms to church music standards. An excellent service setting should not be beyond the ability of the choir and yet must be a considerable contribution to the music of the house of worship. The use of the anthem of English and Continental churches has been used for much good effect. Often our own composers may be the best to supply new service music. The writing of fine modern anthems is not beyond the reach of many American composers who are only waiting for a demand.

Selecting an Essay

We occasionally recommend students for the writing of essays on the choral arts. The study of choral literature may lead to the selection of good works. In selecting an essay for the choir library we must keep in mind the standard of balance between the vocal parts, the character of the text and the possibilities of performance by the particular group. The present day needs a broadening of the choral repertoire and a diversification of type. A good choral library will include the masterworks of the past, the important modern works, and the best of the arrangements.

Summary

A choral library must be built up with care and judgment. The director should select music that is honest in inspiration, musically sound, and suitable for his forces. American music can and should occupy a large place in our library. Arrangements should be used with discretion. The goal is a library which will give the choir an opportunity to sing the greatest music at the highest possible level of musical interpretation.

The singing ability of any given group is known best by its director; therefore, it is the responsibility of the director to use the selection of vocal literature to the best advantage. The problem of choral music should be given careful study and by so doing the choral library of the future will be one to which any director may point with pride.

The Confessions of a Church Organist

To The Editor:

Our church is the prime necessity of our old organist. He is a man of the old school — conservative in his ideas, and yet he is a man who has served his church faithfully for many years. He is content to remain in the background, not seeking notoriety, but helpful in every respect. He is patient with the choir and anxious to do his duty.

My confession is not a long one; it is only to show that while I sometimes feel my ideas may be old-fashioned, I am still anxious to do the best that I can for the music of the church. I have played the organ for a long time and I have many cherished recollections of the old masters, but I have also welcomed many of the new works which have come to my attention. In my opinion, it is possible to do both; to be faithful to the traditions and yet not to be blind to the new and useful things which modern composers have given us.

When I found that many people thought that my playing was dry I set myself to the task of working for more color, more variety and better phrasing. The organ has many stops and many shades of tone and a careful use of these will add a great deal to the effectiveness of the service. I am not of the opinion that the organist should be a showman — far from it. The true organist will control the pageant of sound so that it will not interfere with the singing and the service. The organ is an aid to worship and it must always be so used.

A word about rehearsals — the organist should be present at choir rehearsals when special accompaniments are required. The accompaniment is an art and must be studied. Too often the choir rehearses alone and the organist is not heard until the service. This is a mistake. The organist and choir must work together if a fine musical result is to be obtained.

— AN OLD ORGANIST

Embouchure for Alto Clarinet

Q. What is the best embouchure for the alto clarinet? I have seen many players blow with the mouthpiece almost entirely in the mouth and others playing with the mouthpiece held lightly at the lips. What is the correct way?
— N. H., Massachusetts.

A. The alto clarinet is held with a fairly free embouchure, similar to a tenor saxophone. The mouthpiece is placed on the lower lip with not too much insertion. Study should be given to producing a firm, resonant tone with a steady air stream. The embouchure must be flexible and not so rigid that it prevents good vibrato when needed. Practice long tones and overtones are useful in developing a satisfactory embouchure.

Trombone and Flute

Q. I am a trombonist and wish to learn to play a woodwind instrument, preferably the flute. Will the study of flute impair my trombone embouchure? — W. K., Vermont.

A. It is most difficult to forecast just what effect the study of woodwind instruments will have upon the embouchure of the student of brass instruments. Many of our modern wind players are proficient on several instruments. The change from one to the other seems to have little or no effect upon their embouchure. I suggest you try the flute. If it proves harmful to your trombone playing, stop it. I predict you will find no ill effects from playing the flute.

The Clarinet “Break”

Q. What is the “break” on the clarinet and how can one smooth it out? — C. S., L.A., Calif.

A. The “break” is the register change between the chalumeau and throat registers and is caused by the different acoustical lengths of the instrument for those registers. The tone in the chalumeau is produced by the lower harmonics and in the throat by the higher partials. This requires a slight change of embouchure and air. There are a number of practice devices for crossing the break. The simplest is to practice scales very slowly over the break, paying careful attention to fingerings and to the position of the lips. Alternate fingerings are often used for a smooth transition. Practice long tones focusing on evenness of tone on the notes adjacent to the break. Keep the air steady, do not bite, and adjust lip and oral cavity to produce a seamless change. (Followed by short musical examples for practice.)
