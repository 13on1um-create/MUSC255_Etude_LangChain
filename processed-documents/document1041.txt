(Decorative drop-cap begins the article.)

THE nobler love of fellowship which marked the advent of our Master, Jesus Christ, demands the joy and power of music in its loftiest expression.

Music and Christmas have become inseparable. It is the day for the most festive, the most joyous anthems and carols of the Christian church. It is the heralding of a new and glorious era — the greatest epoch in the history of man.

Wonder it is, that most of the great composers have not devoted their finest efforts to this festival day. Bach with his Christmas Oratorio and Handel with the Messiah stand out before all others. Nothing of Beethoven, Schubert, Haydn, Mozart, Schumann, Brahms, Verdi, Tchaikowsky or Wagner has equaled these master inspirations for the music of Noel.

Christmas music should be joyous, jubilant, triumphant. It should ring with the wondrous spirit of brotherly love which heralded the coming of the master.

"BEHOLD I BRING YOU GOOD TIDINGS OF GREAT JOY WHICH SHALL BE TO ALL PEOPLE."

"All people" — not to one or two sects; but to all people. Christmas Cheer and Christmas Spirit must go out to "all people" or we lose the very heart of the great festival. Christmas music must be for everybody.

Let us have good cheer and lots and lots of the merriest kind of music.
It won't happen unless we all arrange for it. Every moment we spend in preparation for a musical Christmas will bring joy to ourselves and all people around us.

Let Us Make This a Really
Musical Christmas

The Mystery of Beautiful Piano Tone

Three articles that have appeared lately in THE ETUDE describe the serious consideration of all of our readers who are concerned in making their piano-playing more beautiful.

They are the articles in the Josef Lhévinne Series, the articles by Kleczynski on "The Study of Chopin" and the interview with Vladimir De Pachmann in this issue. If you will read between the lines in all of these articles you may discover what might be termed the "Chopin Method" of playing the piano. Chopin would probably rise in his grave at the term; but nevertheless he seemed to have fairly definite ideas upon touch and gave his friends and pupils these ideas.

If the records are representative of Chopin's real mind, De Pachmann has instinctively adopted the idea of playing which the Polish-French master sought in his own work.

Lhévinne, working independently and introspectively, has evolved similar principles and insists that upon these good tone may be built and largely depends.

One of the first principles is that the blow of the finger upon the key, whether it be from a stroke or from pressure, must be thoroughly cushioned. That is, the part of the finger that touches the key should be as resilient as possible. If we strike the keys on the very tips of the fingers there is a thin hard cushion in comparison with the large ball a little behind the tips. In other words to produce beautiful, mellifluous piano tone we use not the very tips the finger tips descend upon the keys, with a straight line but they must come down in somewhat oblique position, so that a larger and more springing part of the first joint covers the key surface.

Lhévinne insists that the finger move only at the metacarpal joint, that is, the joint where the finger joins the body of the hand.

For one, for over a quarter of a century, has been in close personal communication with practically all of the great pianists of the world. He has observed minutely their playing in public and in private, innumerable times. He has noted that those who have been famous for their lovely tone are those who play through carefully thought-out principles, or through instinct, played in the manner we have described.

If this simple principle is correct, it will alter the customary methods of elementary instruction very slightly. But this slight change "makes all the difference in the world." The teacher instead of telling the pupils to play on the finger tips directly behind the finger nails will tell them to play with that broader and softer portion of the first finger joint. True, this may at first even aggravate that bugbear of all teachers of children, "the collapse of the first joint" but in the long run better results will be obtained. We do not play the piano with claws but with hands.

We feel very strongly that, if these fundamental principles, advocated in the three articles mentioned, are comprehended and broadly applied, much piano playing in America will become vastly more beautiful.

Just one glance at the hands of Mr. Paderewski, shown in the illustration in this issue of THE ETUDE, will serve to confirm everything we have stated in this editorial. Unquestionably the leading performers of the present seek their beautiful singing tone in this manner of hand position.

Offertories

JOHN ROSS PRATTEN, in "Better Music in Our Churches," just issued by the Methodist Book Concern, intimates that music has a very compelling power in drawing money from the pew holders' pockets during offertories. He says, "Organists, if you have none, a mystic, ethereal echo, prepare to use it now." Quite right, Mr. Prattent: we have known many of the most pious of all the pew holders who required a musical aesthetic to permit the painful operation of separating them from their money-paid consciences. Instead of giving lavishly and modestly they show every evidence of being victims of extraction. Mr. Prattent wisely adds, by way of caution to the organist, "Don't improvise unless you feel that your improvising is acceptable in heaven and in the pews as well."

The Tempo of the Times

The tempo of the great American symphony of 1873 was somewhere around M.M. = 42 Largo molto Comodo. The tempo to-day is M.M. = 2000 Prestoissimo Harshissimo and as much 'faster' as you choose. Because many of us fail to realize the tempo of the times this little editorial may not come amiss. Fifty years ago speeded America up to the point where Velocity has become a kind of pagan deity in our country.

Teachers must recognize that the American child born to-day has facilities for the accumulation of knowledge in all of his branches, so that the average youngster of ten or twelve knows far more about certain things than did his Granddaddy when he graduated from College. Note that we specify 'certain' things. It will always take time to acquire the great mass of classical and scientific learning that marks an educated man — but the moving pictures, the automobile, the talking machine, the radio, the flood of books and papers and magazines distribute information upon a multitude of subjects in a manner utterly unknown fifty years ago. With this has come the adjustment of the child mind to the tempo of the times. The child in a half-hour at the movies is accustomed to the globe that took his grandfather a year to circumnavigate in his splendid old schooner.

In music the whole situation has been revolutionized by the talking machine, the radio and the player piano. The child in the prairie town actually has more opportunities than did his grandfather in the metropolis of 1873.

Irvin S. Cobb in a recent issue of Hearst's International caught the idea in his entertainingly amusing way. Cobb says: "Our ancestors lived at the end of a golf swing as blume. We live at sixty. Slow molasses was the symbol for them; greased lightning is ours. The telephone marked one period of our development, the saxophone marks the present one.

We have progressed from a jog to a canter, from a canter to a gallop and a gallop to a runaway; and even now when we say we've 'screamed' the child of 1873, who was content to read the pathetically dull Rolio Books, now demands to be instructed with books done by men and women with vigorous minds and keen sensibilities. The same applies to music.

Consider the difference in the musical tempo of the times. The sweet girl graduate of 1878, who cornered her musical teacher with a performance of Dvorak's "Rusalka," now thinks nothing of Liszt Rhapsody on the Chopin E flat Polonaise. The youth who put on his musical armor of the seventies, and sat gallantly outside the affluent war-horse, Haydn's Fifth Sonata, may play everyone pieces by Debussy, Ravel, Roger and Company, casting sympathetic glances at the high-browed audience incapable of comprehending their beauties.

The teacher and the musician who fails to catch his personal metronome to the tempo of the times is likely to suffer. On the other hand there can be no cause for regret that the musical life of the nation has broadened so immensely. The modern musician of to-day must be content to be versatile, and the public is more exacting than ever before in its demands.

Possibly we shall yet progress long enough to discover that we are losing in the matter of sheer musicality by running headlong after mere mechanical speed; but the great fact remains that the world will always demand new and amplified sensation, and that the creator who keeps pace with the times will be the man of the hour.

The Secret of a Beautiful Tone

"It will be remembered that in the previous section of this series a prominent pianist stated that one secret of tone production to determine the 'secret' of a beautiful tone. In this connection we must recall that considerable attention was given to the matter of individuality. In every 'pianistic' instrument there must always be a certain amount of personal color. The secret of tone must be founded upon personal control rather than upon any mechanical device. The first thing to be done is to see that the finger is placed upon the key in such a manner that it can act freely, yet with perfect command. The 'cushioning' of the blow is of the first importance. If the finger strikes the key with a tense or angular movement we shall have no singing quality. The fall must be elastic and free and must be produced from the weight of the arm; the finger must act as the conductor, and the last impulse must come from the tip of the finger. The arm must not be rigid. The wrist must not be thrown up high so that the arm hangs. There must be a natural, easy balance, and the finger must complete the final action upon the key.

"Some teachers insist upon laying the arm weight directly upon the hand and finger. This is a mistake. The weight should be a result of the arm's posture and movement, but the final touch must be transmitted by the finger. The see-saw action is a thing to be avoided. The movement must be continuous and well coordinated. Much depends upon the distribution of the weight of the hand over the fingers. The more the hand is properly arched the easier it is to obtain evenness in tone and an equal quality of touch over the entire keyboard.

"This is just one of the elements of good pianistic tone; there are others which must be studied in progressive order and which will be developed in subsequent sections of this series. There are many schools of playing and many methods of instruction. We cannot here attempt to discuss even the principal points of the various methods; I shall only endeavor to give the greater part of my attention to those principles which lie at the very foundation of all successful playing."

The Part the Wrist Plays in a Good Tone

"Very few teachers realize the part the wrist plays in the production of a good tone. If the wrist be completely stiff and held in a high position, the playing will be forced and mechanical and the tone will be produced more by the weight of the arm than by any true musical control. For a beautiful tone the wrist should be slightly flexible and in such position that the finger-fall will be cushioned, and the hand will have a slight elasticity. This slight spring which resides in the wrist is the medium by which we transmit to the finger the weight of the arm, and yet prevent a dead, 'thudded' stroke. The wrist must never be an absorber of sound. For this reason it is next to impossible to produce a good singing tone with a stiff wrist; the result will always be hard or metallic. An elastic wrist lends to the intensity and brilliancy of the tone as it may be desired.

The Boney Structure of the Hand

By Eugene F. Marks

"The workman is known by his tools." The physician must know each bone and chemical and the action of each of these on every organ of the human body. Not until the education of the musician be made as intelligent concerning the body as this other profession is today can he be expected to be a finished craftsman.

The bones not only serve to support the softer structures of the body, but they also articulate and act as levers. And it is this leverage power, as exhibited in the arms, hands and fingers, which gives the accomplished pianist, especially in the development and power and speed. A piano possesses remarkable strength, estimated to equal nearly twice that of oak the same size, and is capable, also, of a greater degree of resistance in a crushing strain.

In studying the action of the hands and fingers in piano-playing, one should consider that it is upon the boney structure of the hands, namely, the bones for finger strength and stability of touch, and carefully note that articulation is a mechanical power and not entirely a question. Any bone deformity is liable to affect tone-production. In the skeleton of the hand it will be found that the palm possesses five long, strong bones, one for each finger, numbered from the thumb side (medial) towards the little finger (lateral). From this remarkable numbering it is easy to discover how music terms adhere to the scientific nomenclature, and why the thumb is designated by 1, and the other digits by 2, 3, 4, 5, according to their location from the thumb side. Of the five bones (metacarpals) forming the palm, that of the thumb is the shortest and stoutest; the second, the longest; while the others show a gradual reduction in length and size. It is owing to this gradual reduction in size and length that the fourth and fifth fingers require special attention. The fewer bones of the palm complicate to the fingers in close proximity to each other, while that of the thumb is separate and distinct from the others, holding a position all its own, which allows a movement peculiar to itself, and unpossessed by the other bones of the palm.

The finger bones (phalanges) are fourteen in number; two for the thumb and three for each finger. These are named numerically from the joints of articulation with the bones (proximal) and forming the plane of the end of the fingers, and not conversely for the rest of the finger to the palm of the hand; as many young pupils suppose. The first bone (phalange) of the fingers is the shortest and longest, consequently the strongest, and is the bone which gives stability to the fingers.

(Phalange) is similar to the first, excepting it is smaller in size, and its use in piano-playing is to give reliability to the curve of the fingers. The third bone, or terminal phalange, is the smallest of the three, and is somewhat spatulated at the distal extremity, which naturally gives security of touch to the tip of the finger. In the thumb the second bone is omitted, and the other two resemble the first and third phalanges of the fingers. These two phalanges working in harmony with the palm (metacarpal) bone of the thumb allow the placing of the thumb under the palm, as in scale passages; and also furnishes the quality of extension, as exemplified on octaves, chords, and other extended fingers.

The action of the muscles has been purposely ignored, in order to show that the bones (which ordinarily receive scant attention) have an important part in the production of tone and in the quality of touch.

Self-Realization

HAS it ever occurred to you that it might be well to indulge in independent thinking concerning your study and general progress? Has it ever occurred to you, that unless you do so, the chances of your attaining more than a respectable mediocrity are decidedly slim? History and experience teach us that only those who think for themselves are actively prominent or pre-eminent in any department of human activity. How erroneous, then, is the attitude of many students of pianoforte who, because they are studying with some prominent or well-known pedagogue, they too must achieve as notable results as some students of this master have attained? Let it be said, as I say often, that the pupil is also a differentiating one; he never traversed through blind faith in any teacher. Faith in the efficient teacher's ability to lead you along the best paths, there must be; but never blind faith.

The purpose here is to discuss the vital elements of musical education generally and pianistic progress in particular. The central junction always, will be, "Strive to know thyself." That is the ideal and practical goal. Try for the largest number of the world's greatest masters were self-taught. Necessity—inner as well as external—primed complete them to strike out for themselves. In so doing they very frequently discovered new and better methods than those in vogue. Only in this manner has human progress ever been effected: only in this manner has human experience been enriched. Self-realization, then, with the aid of an artistic teacher, should produce that originality which the world ever seeks and indeed counts as genius. The very first commandment to the serious student is: "Strive to know thyself."

Vital Questions

What really, then, is your ultimate goal? Are you steadily approaching it? These are the keys to the solution of your problem of self-realization. Have you ever tried to answer these? Are you analyzing because your parents, relatives and friends think you are gifted, or are you studying because you yourself know that you have music? Is there an inner urge which simply impels you to study, irrespective of the other's think and say concerning your attainments? Are you equally a real soul hunger for music which can be satisfied only through serious study and self-expression? Such questions can and should be answered by you alone.

Studying for Appreciation

Without any doubt the vast majority of students are, consciously or unconsciously, pursuing their study to gain a better understanding, appreciation, and a keener love of music. That is, assuredly, a most laudable purpose. Thousands of students gain these ends without great advances in the power to execute. But do not large numbers of students teach that, because they love music, they may as well have the power to interpret? This does not necessarily follow. If all students who love music were destined to become interpreters, there would be far fewer listeners than at present. Every normal human being responds to the musical appeal. Even animals do. It is axiomatic that to love music is to possess mental and moral culture. The fact, however, that you are studying for your own personal pleasure and enlightenment does not excuse you from understanding the basic principles of piano playing which apply, as well, to those who are striving for the concert stage.

Studying for Self-Expression

Every normal human being has an innate desire to express himself in his individual manner. It need not necessarily be through the medium of sound. Numerous other mediums are at your disposal. If you have chosen the musical medium, it is incumbent upon you to take an inventory from time to time of the progress you have made along this line. The word of your teacher is not sufficient. You must see and hear for yourself, and if necessary ask for a recital or examination. The old methods of the Paris Conservatory which insisted that students should play publicly at stated times and under the eye of the patronizing jury were productive of excellent results. It is an unfortunate fact that the present day custom of shielding students from public criticism retards their development. It is the gift of God to be able to withstand criticism and even to profit by it. When you think that you are really progressing, like all spiritual growth, cannot be delegated entirely to another human being. If you do not see evidence of steady progress, if natural obstacles do not yield to endeavor, you had better make a change in teachers, study independently or discontinue study entirely.

Limits and Limitations of Students

There is no gainsaying the fact that every individual has limitations in his possibilities for development. These are biological as well as psychological. Heredity and environment are the two most powerful factors determining our lives and places in the world. All things being equal, the talented individuals can never attain the greatest heights open to the genius. But it never follows that every person is a potential genius. If you think that you could reasonably, persistently and consistently achieve, you invariably outstrip the undisciplined pupil. Mature individuality shows in the way. Deep down in our heart of hearts we realize from time to time whether the work in hand is satisfying a vital need or not. We find happiness and success in proportion to our consciousness of growth and achievement.

Unheard Practice By William W. Kessler

HAVE you ever practiced without listening? That is, have you ever practiced without employing your auditory imagination? If not, then note one of the best means toward developing musical memory as well as adding composition, to bring the ear into play.

This ability is not so hard to acquire. First try it with scales and arpeggios. Study every detail of these while notating the fingering, the phrasing, the pedaling, etc. You can reproduce them in your mind. The pianist will have an instrument to test the accuracy of your touch practice and especially if it has been "heard" within. Then utilize the next exercise: The two together, and so on till the whole person is under control, and finally the whole composition.

At first it may be best to study each hand separately, and then the two together or get the complete harmony. Leave off the instrument a few minutes; then try to recall the musical picture and to play it again.

This silent study develops ability in several directions. (1) Wherever one may be, he can read a new composition and hear it mentally, thus getting an intelligent idea of what it will sound like. (2) It improves accuracy in reading. (3) It enlarges the sight reading capacity. (4) It develops the memory, making it work much more rapidly, and thus making possible a much larger repertoire.

(5) It increases the capacity of the composer, making it possible for him to write out his ideas at any time, without recourse to an instrument. This was the method of most of the master composers.

This power may be of slow growth; it may necessitate much patient study; but the rewards will more than repay all such effort.

Play As You Think—Think As You Play By Ella Marie Powers

The principle of auto-suggestion can often be applied with success in teaching the piano. More especially may the teacher aid the nervous pupil by the imparting to him of its principles.

For example, an advanced pupil was a nervous young lady. One day, making her lesson, she stated that she had practiced her solo over and over again, but a certain part of it was always a failure. Then she added, "I just cannot play it, and I know I never will be able to do that part well."

The Failure Thought

Now, the reason for her repeated failure was that she constantly held in her mind the thought that her fingers would not strike the correct notes. The thought of failure was never in the right place.

"I never can strike that high note and get back to the right low note on time!" she exclaimed. In despair, I took the matter into my hands. I made her stop and tell me the exact place to touch. She did so. Then I said to her: "Think of the moment when the high note is past the fingers; think of the second note after the high note and of the place for it. Now strike the correct lower note and continue." She did so and almost instantly she had the right feeling and knew the movement. From then on everything went well.

Think Success

I explained to her that I felt quite sure that, if she would keep in her mind the thought that she could play it correctly, then her finger would strike the right thing, and immediately and easily fall to the lower or follow with the right motion. The fact that she held the thought would control the attention and thus the realization would follow inseparably.

"Now, isn't this success," I urged. "Your will has made the reaction; your body must obey the wish." The teacher should be full of these little suggestions. Now, teach that idea clearly. Think only of positive accuracy and success. The same thought will make the fingers work correctly. Keep thinking of the exact place, which the mind will hold firmly. The more you think of the parts, the more sure will be the power to overcome the trouble. The habit of suggestion generates the most valuable musical forces.

"I Can Play It"

"Now," I said, "for more than a week, I have been thinking this part of your solo was impossible for you; consequently, you have not been able to play it, because what we think becomes true to us. Now, think again, and say aloud, 'I can play it.'"

"Good!" I exclaimed, when she had complied. "Say it, now, 'I can.' And now try." "I can," said the girl softly, but with a great deal of spirit, and the effect was surprising. Afterward, the teacher directed a few helpful suggestions to correct other mistakes and the student went away with a new feeling of possibility and courage. Very often the change is largely mental. Try it.

How Easy!

You will play that part correctly in no time, if you try to think it as you play. Keep saying, "I can," "I will," and "I must" to yourself. For every time you are discouraged, say, "I can play it," and smile. Your fingers will obey your will. Very often the pupils play so badly because they haven't the concentration necessary to keep the mental image as they play. Keep your mind upon the point you want to attain, and every little success will give strength for further advancement.

The Remarkable Life of George Frideric Handel

New Aspects of the Dramatic Experiences of the Composer of "THE MESSIAH," as Told by the Able English Critic NEWMAN FLOWER By NEWMAN FLOWER

(Editor's Note—The following material is abstracted in part from one of the most notable books ever published, George Frideric Handel and His Times, by Newman Flower, Houghton, Mifflin Company, a work of 376 pages with many illustrations; extracts herewith are reprinted by kind permission.)

Of all the great composers of history, certain ones quite naturally stand out by force of their striking individuality, as well as by their compositions. Among these may be reckoned Beethoven, Wagner, Paganini, Liszt and a very marked degree, George Frideric Handel. It is therefore with delight that we investigate new phases of the life of this most energetic and active musician, who, after long and hard experiment, came to such a position as he occupied: the more so in that he was, and he continues to be, one of the most popular of composers, whose music still delights millions of admirers. Handel, and His Times, by Newman Flower, gives a most interesting account of the career and adventures of the composer, and shows the dramatic experiences which, in some respects, dominated his life and art. The following pages contain extracts from this book.

Handel and His Times

One of the great troubles of history is the habit of attributing the success of men to the softness of their surroundings or to the convenience of their situation. In the case of Handel, studies in the circumstances of his life must be pursued with caution; we must look at his temperament, his energy, his training, his temperament again, and many other things besides.

Handel was as great a man of affairs as he was a musician. He was active in business as well as in art. He and his firm, which engaged in publishing his works, and the rehearsal and the printing of copies, performed more like a modern impresario's office than the simple household of an ordinary musician. Handel's name was a brand which could be produced to draw an audience.

He was always, during his life, full of resource. One of the most remarkable things about him is the way in which he turned calamity into opportunity. When deeply in debt or in danger of failing to meet obligations, he used his energy and ability to re-establish himself. Handel's first period was an apprenticeship of a sort, in which he absorbed the music of his contemporaries and formed his own style. He was much influenced by the Italian opera composers, and travelled to Italy in order to study at first hand. In Italy he established himself as a composer of operas and cantatas, and gained favour at the courts where he sang and composed. His knowledge of vocal writing increased, and intimate acquaintance with the Italian manner enabled him to bring to England the strength of Italian dramatic form.

Handel's life in England was chequered by successes and failures. He was at times triumphant, at others in difficulties. The rivalry in London between composers — particularly with Giovanni Bononcini — is a notable story. The public, the nobility, and the royal household were parts of this drama; financiers and impresarios played their roles. Yet through it all Handel retained his artistic integrity.

Handel in Italy

His travels in Italy were crucial. He came under the influence of Corelli and other leading Italians, and found in Rome and Venice a culture which made his musical potencies grow. He associated with famous singers and with patrons who encouraged his work. There he wrote cantatas, operas, and works of sacred music, accumulating the experience which later served him in London.

His return to Germany and then to England brought new opportunities. He wrote music for the theatres, for the court, and for public entertainment. As each enterprise met with various degrees of success, Handel learned how to manage public taste. His public music was always abundant with invention; his skill in setting words to music and in drawing character from melody was unmatched.

Handel in Berlin (further notes)

In Berlin, he became acquainted with the new forms of courtly music and with musicians of high repute. He found an audience sympathetic to his best talents, and he profited by the discipline and culture which the Prussian court afforded. This shaped his later compositions and strengthened his resolve to excel in whatever venue he undertook.

Handel and Bononcini

One of the most famous rivalries of the day was that between Handel and Giovanni Bononcini, the Italian composer. The clever slogan of the day, "Some say, compared to Bononcini, That Mynheer Handel's but a ninny; Others aver that he is to Handel," reflects the witty public commentary of the time. The rivalry created discussion and argument, but it also stimulated both men to further efforts.

Handel's personal life was modest in many ways. He had little of the swagger of some of his colleagues, but he possessed the confidence of one who knew his power. He cultivated friendships among the nobility and was a frequent visitor at court. His position in society was that of a man widely respected and often sought after for his musical judgment.

The story of the first performance of Messiah in Dublin is one of the most engaging episodes in Handel's career. The hall at Neale's, the eager public, and the interest of listeners combined to produce an event which has since become famous. That performance, and subsequent ones, helped to secure the reputation of the work and of the composer in England and beyond.

From an early age Handel showed a vividness and intensity which were to characterize his work. He was not content with mere technical ability; he strove for emotional result. In his later life Handel consolidated his position. He turned increasingly to oratorio as his chief means of expression, and in so doing he brought English sacred music to a new level. Financially Handel had his ups and downs, but his resourcefulness enabled him to survive and to prosper in spite of setbacks. He managed his finances, and although there were periods of anxiety, his reputation ultimately ensured his stability.

Initiative in Music Study

By L. G. Rorke

Initiative has often been likened to the self-starter in the automobile. It is far more than that. It represents, particularly, it is a state of mind. It represents the eagerness to set one's work in motion, the power, resources, to start one's study plans, to look around for new worlds to conquer.

With every instrument there is a literature of conventional music which thousands of people will on initiative play over and over again, rarely trying to get into another part of the work. This is particularly so with an instrument like the violin or the piano where one gets certain pieces which are built around a few ordinary chords with obvious embellishments, showing about the same originality that one might expect in the old-fashioned, machine-made Nottingham curtains. Yet, contrasts seem to go on year after year playing this empty stuff, with its silly variations, its monotonous double-tonguing, when right at the door are hundreds of real, beautiful music from the great masters which, if effectively played, would be quite as impressive to the average audience as much of the musical trash that they persist in performing. If one would take initiative, however, to start his study.

It takes initiative to start a new program of increased study. It takes initiative to go to college. It takes initiative to get up a concert or a recital. It takes initiative to start a club.

Where does this musical initiative come from? Suppose you have been reading about starting a music study club. You know from hearsay that such clubs are good things. You know that the higher the musical intelligence of your friends or your pupils, the greater will be your enjoyment in music; and, if you are a professional, the larger will be your income. 1. A decision.
2. A plan of your proposed club.
3. A plan of your time to accomplish your purpose.
4. A plan to secure the necessary materials.
5. A plan to cover the slight expense involved.

How to Study Away from the Piano

By Edith Josephine Benson

The following outlines have been helpful to the teacher and the pupil as saving time during the lesson period and in securing a thorough understanding of the composition to be studied. Many pupils understand the instructor's remarks in the lesson, but can not remember them. The first set of questions is designed to help both pupil and teacher by a few simple directions and will be found convenient. A number of memoranda and suggestions may be kept in the teacher's supply of materials.

1. In what key or keys is the composition written?
2. How many times repeated?
3. What is the meter? Where are the accents? On what beats are the weak? Does the meter change?
4. How many rhythmic patterns are there?
5. Does the right hand play entirely in the treble? What are the larger lines and spaces of the treble staff?
6. Name the accidental in each hand.
7. Study the left hand like as if 4.
8. What phrase marks are connected? Study phrase as sentence. Are there any repeated phrases? A phrase is the same thing as a sentence and consists of a thought.
9. Name the periods and analyze some of the phrasing and sentences. What ideas are repeated? Does the first phrase end with a cadence? If so what kind? Are the cadences perfect, imperfect, plagal or interrupted?
10. Where are the modulations going to? At which point is the nearest change? Has the composer made a key relation between parts?
11. With what finger does the composition begin?
12. What finger does the composition begin?
13. Study the fingering.
14. What do you think the piece expresses?
15. The following set may be given to beginners (not the small ones) or to further advanced pupils who have difficulty in reading.
1. Name the key, the form. 2. Name the entrance of the bass, the first, the first, the third, the fourth, the third, the fourth. Name the first space, the third, the fourth, the second, the second (etc.) The signs, the first line, the third space, the fifth line, the second line the first. This is the first time the study is similar. 3. Name the three different kinds of cadences and find all those on the keyboard.
4. Study separately the right hand and the left hand.
5. Follow the hands simultaneously. 6. Practice all the hard spots with slow motion and gradually increase the speed.
7. Write out the marking and the fingering. 8. Practice all the big stretches with the arm in a relaxed state.
9. Use a metronome. If you cannot afford one, beat time yourself.
10. Play slowly and with a song-like quality. It is better to practice slowly and with perfect musicality than to practice quick and mechanical.
11. Learn the notes thoroughly. 12. Practice measures that give trouble, working backward and forward.
13. Make suggestive analysis. 14. Work on your style. 15. It is helpful to leave some time each day for study at sight.

The Teachers' Round Table

Conducted by PROF. CLARENCE G. HAMILTON, M.A.

This department is designed to help the teacher upon questions pertaining to "How to Teach," "What to Teach," etc., and not technical problems pertaining to Musical Theory, History, etc., all of which properly belong to the Musical Questions Answered department. Full name and address must accompany all inquiries.

Encouraging Ambition

A young student who has evidently made considerable progress in technique shows signs of her she loves music and has a strong ambition to go on. Her teacher of course should not attempt to force her into public performance; but there are ways to encourage without pushing. I have always had my pupils present one piece at a time at the pupils' recitals, and in a number of my pupils I have found it helpful to suggest that she might sing or play at a church service. If this is done with tact it usually brings light up to the student as well as to the parents, and makes the student realize that she must study harder to develop her art—or make her see that there is no call for public performance. Too many pupils imagine that they must finally give a recital or else they have got nowhere. The wise teacher will use all the encouragement possible and not force the rest. The gradual winning out is usually the most lasting effect.

Strength of Tone

A young lady who has evidently acquired considerable technical expertness, writes:

Although my mother insists upon tone as being the one great thing I strive for, I can not help wondering if I am getting the kind of tone that is acceptable in recital playing. I have a very small hand and a light tone. My teacher tells me it is the result of a small chest or lungs. Also, the throat which if too tense would produce a thinness. What would you suggest that I do to develop a fuller tone?

A fault confessed is half redressed. The fact that your teacher is watching the effect of finger and arm technique upon the tone is much to the student's advantage.

It is a good plan to try and play the volume out of the arm and shoulder rather than from the fingers. The muscles of the fingers are small and tend to get tired. Play as much as you can with the forearm. This will bring a larger tone. If you can use more weight through the arm, you will have a fuller tone. Another point is that you should not squeeze. The wrist should be flexible and the arm relaxed. Avoid striking with the fingers.

In practicing the teacher will show the pupil how to keep the wrist and elbow flexible. So practice scales with a light touch, ask the pupil to feel the weight of the arm in the wrist—not the fingers.

Scale-Tones

What are the scale-tones that are the most obvious to study? I ask, reward and technique.

Concerning the tonic and subdominant—The tonic is an important "and best" singer of importance besides; and the scale degrees are the important ones. One must practice them carefully and see that they stand out as fixed points. The fourth and the fifth are also important. The leading tone is necessary for changes. There is a distinct objection to allowing any of the scale tones to become weak. This means while practicing scales, arpeggios and other technical exercises keep the tonic and the fifth clear and firm. Do not let the passing outdoor notes get the better of the main tones. Practice the scale as a musical line.

Difficulties in Reading Notes

Please send me a little advice about how to help students who have difficulty reading notes. If they will only have some regular habits and proper practice they will soon find that the eyes, voice and fingers will be in better accord. Sight-reading is improved by having to read regularly. One must teach the pupils to look ahead and not to dwell. Use small studies and make the reading quick. Also, aim to make them read more than they play in the lesson. This will keep the eye more in the keyboard and create better association.

Small Hands vs. Octaves

I have a pupil, a boy 14 years old, who can not play octaves easily. His hand is small. What can I do?

There are many ways to accomplish the effect of octaves without actually using full normal reach. A careful arrangement of shapes and fingerings will often obviate the necessity. Also, practice strengthening and stretching exercises will help. Use double-note exercises and then gradually introduce octaves. The important thing is to train the hand to accept a wider stretch without strain.

What instruments should an artist have in his studio? The small number of these instruments is not so surprising if we consider the necessary qualifications, some of which are:

A POOR PIANO TEACHER NAMED BRAHMS

As some musicians know, the Brahms transcriptions of the Hungarian dances resulted from the chance that in his youth Brahms acted as accompanist to Remenyi, the Hungarian gypsy violinist. The two met in Hamburg, 1852, and Remenyi's accompanist having failed to appear, he asked Auguste Horner to suggest a substitute. Brahms recommended "a poor piano teacher named Brahms." Joachim, himself one of the above mentioned, tells the incident in the book by Gwendolyn Kelley and George P. Upton, collected "Record Reviews, Musicians, Literature and Music."

"About five o'clock of the same day, while practicing in my room, somebody knocked at the door, and in came a youth with a very high soprano voice, that whose features, owing to the dusk of the evening, I could not well discern. I lighted a candle, and then saw that the stranger was one of the boys who, I afterwards learned, had been appearing in the local theatres, and I took him for a theatrical comedian. Brahms, however, soon put aside all my suspicions by taking his place at the piano and playing with such ease and such power that, though young and slight, he seemed already a master. He played the whole sonata through, and concluded with some of his own compositions.

"Some one suggested afterwards that he should be engaged as an accompanist to Remenyi. He was so engaged. And upon the occasion of the concert which was to be given by Remenyi on the following day, the young Brahms, who had not yet reached his majority, took his seat at the piano, and the performance was a success. Remenyi was delighted, and some time later, when Brahms composed the Hungarian dances, he turned them into such arrangements for piano as have since become famous."

PHONOGRAPH VERSUS RADIO

Many music lovers are wondering how far the interest in recorded music will be affected by radio broadcasting. The phonograph gives a permanence of record which the radio cannot equal, and the radio supplies the immediate thrill of public performance and the variety of live broadcasts. Each has its own peculiar functions. The phonograph will remain the way to acquire and to preserve favorite performances, and the radio will continue to be the medium through which new music and new artists are presented to the public. As in many other cases of competing media, it is likely that both will exist side by side for a long time.

THE GIFT OF AUDITORY IMAGERY

Among psychologists, Carl E. Seashore, of the University of Iowa, has been a leader in the study of the auditory imagination. He finds that many persons have a vivid "inner ear" and can imagine music with great clearness and detail. Others possess only a faint auditory imagery, while some apparently have none at all. The question of how much of this faculty is innate and how much can be developed by training is still to be decided. It is certain however, that musical training does increase the vividness and accuracy of auditory imagery in many students, and that the ability to recall music mentally is an important asset to every musician.

LISZT AMONG HIS PUPILS

Bertini Walker, a pianist who had studied under Liszt, in a recent interview gave some interesting reminiscences of the great master. Liszt, he said, was indefatigable in his devotion to technical work and in the cultivation of expressive phrasing. He insisted upon the absolute cleanliness of execution and upon the necessity of producing tone with feeling and variety. Liszt's pupils, he declared, were taught "to make music speak."

IN HONOR OF STEPHEN FOSTER

A recent volume, prepared by the Old Kentucky Home Association, in Bardstown, Kentucky, where Stephen Collins Foster was born, contains valuable fragments of the life and work of the composer, including letters, reminiscences and a collection of favorite songs. The Foster home has been made a museum, and some efforts are being made to provide funds for the restoration and maintenance of the house. A good deal of interest has been aroused by the project and the movement to raise funds for its completion is gaining strength.

MUSIC AFTER MEALS?

An editorial in the New York evening papers questions the practice of playing music after meals in restaurants and hotels. The writer suggests that the custom may be pleasant to some, but that to others it is an annoyance and an intrusion upon the quiet and after-dinner conversation which many patrons desire. It is proposed that music after meals should be made optional in places where the public appetite is so divided.

Cause and Cure of Singer's Colds
By Irving Wilson Voorhees, M. D., New York City

While a "cold" is always an important condition from the doctor's viewpoint, it is not only important, but also of the first magnitude in singers. Everyone knows that if a singer has a cold it is often impossible for him to carry out his program, and, through some perversity of fate, quite often these infections take place just preceding an important engagement. It is for this reason that the brief article about colds is written from the viewpoint of one who has much to do with voice disorders of singers.

Cold Preventions

While preventive medicine is the watchword of the present-day physician, hitherto prevention of colds was regarded as quite impossible. The reason for this attitude is that the nature of colds has not been well understood until recently; and now we know that a cold is an acute infectious disease caused by various organisms which find lodgment somewhere on the respiratory mucous membrane. Some persons are especially susceptible to these infectious features because they are the physicians say "sensitized" to that which is the protective mechanism of the body does not react against exposure to a cold, and, therefore, the symptoms quickly arise upon the slightest precaution. By some authorities it has been thought that frequent colds are influenced by what is known as adenoids, excessive activity of the body fluids. Normally the blood is an alkaline fluid, and, while it never under any condition becomes absolutely acid, its alkalinity is sometimes greatly diminished, and particularly so in certain infectious diseases.

The above subject is elaborated in a book, "Hygiene of the Voice" now on the press of the MacMillan Company.

Then, too, the building up and tearing down processes of the body which commonly are known as metabolism may be out of balance. There are certain glands such as the thyroid in the neck, the adrenals which lie adjacent to the kidneys and various other which are said to have an "intrinsic" secretory action, and, when these are changed, the general system may be affected. The structures in the mouth and throat and the nasal passages are but parts of the general mucous membrane; and where the general condition is poor the individual will be more liable to frequent attacks of catarrhal infections. To change this condition of body and blood toward a normal one is often the means of preventing recurrent infections. All of this is an argument for proper physical hygiene and the special care of the throat and nasal passages; but it is, nevertheless, true that no one is immune.

Lessened Resistance

Lessened resistance is a common cause of all infections. For instance, if the surface of the body is exposed to cold, and the blood is driven inward to the internal organs, bringing about congestion of the abdominal viscera and relaxation of the mucous membranes, the singer often becomes more susceptible to these infectious agents. The habitual use of alcohol, smoking, late hours and other forms of irritation also materially reduce the power of resistance. We must, therefore, counsel moderation in all these things.

Another important point is the prevention of infection by avoiding exposure to those with catarrhal conditions. If one is forced into proximity to a coughing person it is well to take such precautions as the use of a handkerchief, frequent washing of the hands and the avoidance of touching the face with the hands. The singer should try to avoid crowded places during the season of prevalence of colds. A careful guard of the throat, the nose and the entire respiratory tract is desirable.

The Family Doctor's Part

One should avoid self-medication and should seek professional advice when the first symptoms appear. A thorough examination by a physician who knows the throat and nose is important because the singer's voice may be endangered by conditions requiring attention which are not readily recognized by the patient. The physician will examine the nose for deviations of the septum, for enlarged turbinates or for other obstructions which can be remedied. He will also examine the pharynx and larynx. For the singer there is little excuse for any delay in securing expert attention.

Pet Remedies

When a cold has been acquired, what is to be done? Many varieties of treatment have been recommended, both in and out of the medical profession. Nearly everyone has some pet remedy, the most popular among singers being the onion and whiskey. These remedies are dependent upon the fact that they are likely to be locally irritant and increase the blood flow to the surface. They have no specific antiseptic value and should be used only as soothing measures. The discovery of the cause of the cold is most important, because if the cause can be removed the condition will greatly improve. Where there is an associated infection the special treatment should be directed against that infection.

Lessened resistance as a common cause of all infections must be recognized. Attention to diet, sleep and to the general hygiene of the individual is important. There is also the question of local treatment of the nose and throat. Without adequate attention to the nose and its secretions, the singer will remain subject to recurrent attacks.

There are certain special measures which deserve attention. A steady paste brush is valuable for the removal of crusts and secretions from the nose; sprays of sterile isotonic salt solution are often useful; the use of steam inhalations and hot fomentations are also of service. In acute stages the application of local heat in the form of hot fomentations over the sinuses, the use of a radiant heat lamp for brief periods and local massage are all helpful. It sometimes happens that the voice is very bad immediately preceding a recital. This is probably due to the fact that the mucous membrane is much dry and the voice is hoarse because of the laryngeal secretions. Attention to rest of the voice and to local treatment will often effect a cure in a short time.

Let us briefly summarize what I have tried to set forth in the preceding paragraphs:

A cold is an acute respiratory infection, at first local, then constitutional in its effects. While very often a simple matter, it may lead to serious complications, even death. It may be cured promptly and permanently by a quick cold shower with rapid drying and rubbing down. One may then remain in warm, clean underwear and face the elements with a sense of security against catching cold. It must be understood, however, that there are many persons who cannot get along without the assistance of a trained hygienist.

Five Points of Attack

The principle points by which the specialist works are, first, to open the nose; second, to wash out the nose to remove secretions, third, to treat the local infection and, fourth and fifth, to prevent constitutional ill effects. The treatment before mentioned includes the use of irrigations of the nose, attention to the throat and correction of the general physical condition.

Do's

There are some "don'ts" which ought to be strictly observed by singers, because they are liable to offset all efforts made to cure colds. These are: never to sing under the strain of a cold or to attempt long and difficult programs; never to use the voice in frosty weather without protecting the throat; never to expose the voice to sudden changes in temperature and to avoid strenuous efforts during the period of recovery. It must be remembered that the singing voice is the last to return to normal after a cold and the patient should be conservative.

Proper Bathing

Sometimes proper bathing will help in the cure. A cold shower taken after the throat is in the condition of a runny nose is often useful. The patient whose voice is unreliable should not sing. It is better by far to rest the voice and preserve it for a later engagement than to take a risk.

Suggestions for Singing Students
By Arthur L. Manchester

Effortless Effort

The difference between easy singing and the use of pure musical quality and the strenuous production of a harsh, unmusical tone is indicated by the narrow contrast and the practical difference and excessive muscular effort which results in strain. Singing is an easy and natural act. When one delivers the tone by effort, it is unnatural and quickly becomes fatiguing and often the cause of permanent or long-lasting injury to the vocal mechanism.

Five Points of Attack

The principles upon which efficient singing work are first, to open the throat and secure freedom for the voice; second, to avoid undue tension of the throat and neck; third, to secure proper breathing; fourth, to employ the resonance and quality of tone characteristic of the song; fifth, to sing with musical feeling and taste.

It is amazing how often singing students act as though vocalization were a purely mechanical matter. The voice is essentially an instrument of expressivity and it is by the intelligent use of the apparatus that musical results are produced. To secure freedom in singing one must rid the throat of pressure. The throat must be open and the larynx free to vibrate. The work of the diaphragm in supporting the breath should be automatic and unstrained. Students who push the tone forward with throat tension destroy the ease and finish of the tone.

More Specific Points

1. Avoid useless muscular contraction. The throat and neck as a rule should be free from tension.

2. Secure a natural and deep inspiration. The chest and abdominal walls should cooperate. Singing requires a sufficient supply of air which should be placed in the proper parts of the chest.

3. Do not practice with excessive loudness. The tone should be produced for musical reasons and not to cover difficult passages.

4. Keep the jaw, tongue and facial muscles relaxed. Tension of these parts frequently produces a nasal or tight tone.

5. Employ resonance. The proper placing of the voice in the mask of the face converts muscular effort into musical tone.

Singing Speed

The amount of speed and elasticity required of the voice varies with the selections. Do not attempt to sing with speed unless the tone and breathing are under perfect control. A useful exercise is to practice scales and passages slowly until the exact muscular control has been gained and then gradually increase speed.

The act of phonation is an automatic one, attended to by the larynx. All the rest relates to do with it so as to give a certain pitch and the larynx assume the correct mechanism to produce that pitch, provided the breath is correctly used. There is no need for muscular help on the part of the throat, jaw or tongue for normal phonation. The singer should avoid the temptation to strain. Thus, the primary point in singing is the prevention of excessive muscular strain. The singer who achieves success uses a minimum of effort and obtains the maximum of musical effect.

Music, Muscle, Work and Imagination
By Grace May Stutsman

The pianist or violinist who gives up her practicing when household duties become a part of her daily routine has failed to make the proper use of her imagination. Too often, instead of becoming the "driver" who assumes the role of "the trainer," she allows the muscle to become of slave to the arm and the arm to become tired and ineffective. In many households music is relegated to the place of a convenience and is seldom given the time necessary to develop into a real power. The training of the child is neglected when household tasks are allowed to absorb every minute of time that could be set aside for the practice of the instrument. The imagination is the great motor of the real creative musician and when it is not developed such a musician cannot be created.

A certain amount of practice made up of short, determined periods, each followed by a rest, will accomplish more than the one long practice of two or three hours without rest. It is the steady building of the muscle under ordered control that counts. Many a player who might have become useful in her service to music, is robbed of the real joy of making music by the poor use of time and the lack of imagination. Instead of practicing the same piece over and over, make the small things count. Vary the tempo, the rhythm, the accent, and you will find that the piece becomes alive again. When you meet difficulties apply imagination to their removal. Have courage to try new wrist and hand positions, but do not be hasty in changing fundamental habits. The individual who is likely to develop into a real artist is not the one who forces the technical processes mechanically but the one who uses imagination and understanding in practice. The latter will find the muscles obedient to his will and the tone responsive to his ideas.

The Inexperienced Organist and the New Organ
By Ernest L. Mehaffey

The tremendous increase in the number of new organs being installed in churches throughout the country has resulted in a very real problem to those churches not so situated as to have the services of an accomplished organist. Many musical people, capable of performing passably on a piano or reed organ, are confronted with the task of handling a modern pipe organ, with its multiplicity of mechanical accessories, made possible by the perfection of pneumatic and electric action, knowing nothing of how to obtain the best results with the resources at their command. Of course, it is impossible in a short article to give a complete outline of what can be used to advantage in the average small organ, the purpose of this article is merely to make suggestions that have proven of value to many who have been placed in like circumstance.

The Average Small Organ
Let us suppose that a church installs a small organ, costing from $2500 to $3500. This organ will have either pneumatic or electric action, with from seven to ten speaking stops, and from nine to twelve couplers, together with the Swell Pedal, Crescendo Pedal, Great to Pedal Reversible, Sforzando Pedal Combination, Pistons, etc. The usual make-up of such an organ is as follows:

Great Organ
Open Diapason ... 8 ft.
Melodia (or Clarabella) ... 8 ft.
Octave ... 4 ft.
Dulciana ... 8 ft.
Swell Organ
Salicional ... 8 ft.
Stopped Diapason ... 8 ft.
Flute Harmonique ... 8 ft.
Oboe ... 8 ft.
Pedal Organ ..........................16 ft.
Combination ..........................?

Stop Families
These will be the actual speaking stops, and by speaking stops we refer to the voices, and not to the mechanical stops which are characteristic qualities of tone, and which have been grouped into "families," as follows:
Diapason Tone: Principal (Great), 8 ft., Octave (Great), 4 ft., Subbass (Pedal), 16 ft.
Flute Tone: Stopped Flute (Swell), 8 ft., Flute Harmonic (Swell), 8 ft., Open Flute (Swell), 4 ft.
String Tone: Salicional (Swell), 8 ft., Viola (?) 8 ft.
Bourdon Tone: Bourdon (Pedal) 16 ft., string-tones, etc.

It is in the proper use of these different stops, in combining them together, using the couplers, and so on, that the inexperienced organist finds the greatest difficulty.

Let us suppose that Mrs. Jones or Mrs. Smith must learn to play the new organ. They know nothing whatever about the tonal combinations, the couplers, the mechanism of "8," "4," "2," "16," and the like. They must learn, as many others have done, to rely upon a few simple combinations which can be relied upon in nearly every service. Any organ should be made to play with a minimum of fuss and bother, and for the inexperienced person a list of helpful suggestions is worth more than dozens of complicated theories.

Swell to Swell 16 ft., — Couples Swell or Swell to Swell 16 ft. — Couples Swell or — Swell to Swell 4 ft. — Couples Swell or — Swell to Swell 16 ft. — Couples Swell or — Great to Swell 8 ft. — Couples Great or — Swell to Great 8 ft. — Couples Great or — Great to Pedal 8 ft. — Couples Great or — Swell to Pedal 8 ft. — Couples Swell or — Great to Pedal 4 ft. — Couples Great or — Swell to Pedal 4 ft. — Couplers will be listed separately.

Couplers
Let us briefly discuss the proper use of couplers. These are mechanical devices which couple one manual to another or couple manual to pedal. They are invaluable when used with discretion. Do not use all couplers at once. Learn to use the simplest couplings first: Swell to Great, Great to Pedal, Swell to Pedal. When these are well in hand you may use the Coupled Octaves, and the couplers for 4 ft. and 16 ft. registers are best used sparingly.

Swell Pedal
The Swell box or enclosed division of the organ should be used to bring out solo voices or to control effectively the dynamic shading. When playing service music for congregational singing the swell pedal should be used gently, not as a substitute for improper registration, but rather to blend and create an effective leading voice where needed.

Combinations
Great care should be used in the combinations to be used in service playing. If the organ has pistons, use them — but learn to know what each piston does. Too often pistons are used haphazardly and the results are unsatisfactory. With practice, the combinations of Swell and Great, and Swell and Pedal, will become automatic, and the organist will be able to select the proper registration quickly and effectively.

How Henry Ward Beecher Would "Redeem" the Sabbath with Music

Few pastors of the past or present ever laid more stress upon music than did Henry Ward Beecher. The Crescendo Pedal is an accessory which is very useful, but may, like all contrivances, be misused. In the smaller organs it is the order of their power, from the softest stop to the full organ. The changes should be so managed as to add to the effect by easy gradation.

Recovering Lost Ground
in Violin Technic
By Ben Venuto

Several, times during the past few years, the advice of the writer has been asked in regard to the best course of practice for regaining technic after the violin has been laid aside for some time and the player grown rusty. Curiously enough, the form of the question is almost always such as to show a complete misunderstanding of the real requirements. It usually is worded somewhat in this way: "Please tell me the names of two or three difficult solos that I might practice so as to get back my lost technic, as I haven't touched the violin for two (three, four or five) years." To practice "difficult solos" under these circumstances would be about the worst thing to be done. One would be sure to play out of tune, and if at all self-critical, would become discouraged.

The first thing to be done is to go through howing exercises similar to those taught to a beginner at the first few lessons, watching to make sure that the player really is practicing straight bow and otherwise in good form. Being assured of this, next practice long sustained bows, very slowly, trying to make each stroke last a full minute without a break. (This is an exercise used by even the greatest players, to steady their nerves before a public performance.) Next try something quite the contrary—the arco staccato—whole-bow strokes, played with a darting motion as quickly as possible from one end of the stick to the other, with ample rests between. It matters but little what material you use for these exercises; the scale of G is as good as anything. Next take some fingers-exercises on open strings, such as those in Schneidcrich, and practice a few of them with the greatest care as to evenness and correct intonation. Do not raise too many, but give intensive practice to a few. Lastly, look up some books of the easiest etudes which were studied in former years—Kreutzer, Wohlfahrt, or any others, and review them one by one, endeavoring to play them in a much more intelligent and artistic manner than you were able to do when you first studied them. After a few days of this sort of practice, advance yourself ("supposing your earlier studies had reached that grade") into three-octave scales, and the several trillo-studies found in Kreutzer's Etudes. The latter are the finest sort of thing to limber up the fingers.

We have spoken of regaining technic after one has become rusty through ceasing practice for some length of time; but it is worth mentioning that sometimes a player in constant practice will seem to suffer a breakdown of technic, especially in the matter of intonation or of clean and accurate execution in general. When this does not arise, as is sometimes the case, from ill-health, over-fatigue or depressions caused by shows that one has begun attempting music too difficult for his present stage of progress, in such a case, a few days devoted exclusively to the sort of practice we have just described will mend matters. But it is a real injury to spend much time on pieces manifestly beyond your powers; one must be patient and work up to them gradually. The adormal stretches, for instance, often found in Gavotte's and Paganinni, should never be attempted but by those who already have perfect mastery of the more normal use of the fingers; otherwise a tendency to lay out of tune will be developed.

Starting the Violin Beginner
By Mabel Madison Watson

Though many critics will say "There is only one right way to play the violin, but many wrong ones: the still serious to be those forms of various positions recorded as the most advisable schemes of starting the beginner.

Position

An erect and assured bearing gives confidence to both performer and audience. Erectness should be the keynote of tone; check any forward tendency and the actual posture must be one of dignity. The tilt of the head should not be so marked that it interferes with the bow arm. The violin should be held with the same stability as the head, the shoulders square, the left hand support at the chin with the first finger and thumb lightly upward, till the habit is formed.

Until the pupil's position is fairly established he should not be expected to practice with much success. Alone practice should be short, and instruction must be frequent. Rarely will a pupil of the very first stages keep position perfect without aid. Hence, do not expect overmuch from beginners in the first few months.

When the bow-arm begins to show a grade of skill, it will help to introduce the chair position, to play 'seated' and practice the heavier bows, which help to develop the forearm and right wrist mechanism.

As soon as possible one should have the student stand and play, with the balance of each stroke carefully observed. The left hand should be held easily, and the thumb placed lightly, almost turning inward, against the neck' or slightly below' the neck. The finger tips should be curved and the knuckle joints kept near the finger board.

Right Hand Training

Holding and control of the bow would require a treatise in itself. Suffice it to say that the first aim of the teacher must be to instil the use of the whole arm as connected with the breathing and the abdominal muscles. Stiff wrists and shoulders led always to faulty tone. A few lessons on the bow-hold will save months of bad tone.

Left Arm and Hand Position

Obliviously a hand, as the left that my fingers can reach all four strings by direct motions with practically no hand or arm adjustment, will secure maximum speed with minimum effort. Keeping left hand finger work on the G string until this position is established will save years of corrective training further on in the course. The flexibility of young pupils makes a slightly exaggerated left arm position the easier to correct later.

The best method of forming notes and drawing out the tone is the method of slow strokes, a few at a time, till the bow's outer edge (sometimes called the hair) will produce a firm, steady tone. The more the student is asked to practice and the more he enjoys a success, the faster the progress. It is better to have short, frequent lessons than a long one.

Use of the fingers

When the fingers are placed one against the other, the student should be taught that no two may cross; the ring may tend to be pressed against the finger next to it, which is objectionable. Encourage the use of the finger-tips for clean, clear stopping of the strings.

Other points

The shoulders should be kept relaxed; the left wrist should not be contracted. The arm must drop away from the body when shifting from one position to another.

Violin Questions Answered
By Mr. Brune

Q. I am a Foster. Is it impossible for me to give a house or to your features as a violinist without a personal hearing? I should like to play as I have done when about four rhythms and no tone, and about the poorest possessed. Is it ever necessary that I should take violin lessons? If so, what is the best method to begin? Are there any good schools that will take me up and give the proper instruction?—S. R.

A. We have an idea that the trouble in your case is tone production. A boy of ten or twelve years old may be trained to play very well upon the violin in a short time, but if you have played on the piano, you will make much more rapid progress. The young of adult ages are very difficult to train to a good tone, because their hands are already fixed. The teacher should take the trouble to show you the position of the left hand. Place the left arm over the fingerboard with the thumb near the second position. Practice long tones, playing with a steady bow and keep the bow straight. Be careful not to play by jolting. Practice in front of a mirror, to see that you hold the instrument and bow correctly. Use first position in simple scales and arpeggios, and make sure the little finger does not bend under. You cannot get a good tone by playing too high on the string. Play somewhat over the fingerboard for a thin tone, nearer the bridge for a brighter tone. Scales will do much for you; practice them slowly and with a metronome if possible.

Q. What do you mean by "half position?"—J. F.

A. Half position is a position of the left hand where the first finger is placed where the second finger is usually placed in first position, and the second finger falls where the third finger normally would be. It is frequently used in shifting.

Q. I am a Violinist. Would you recommend playing minor scales in thirds and sixths?—E. M.

A. Yes. Minor scales in double stops are very useful for developing the fingers for shifting and intonation.

Q. What is the best bow hold?—L. H.

A. The best bow hold is a comfortable, relaxed hold with the thumb bent and placed between the frog and the stick, the first finger resting on the stick near the thumb, the other fingers curved and the hand kept flexible. Avoid gripping the bow too hard.

Q. Is vibrato necessary?—P. S.

A. Vibrato is an expressive device and when used tastefully it adds warmth to the tone. It should not be overdone and should be developed gradually.
