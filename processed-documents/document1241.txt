MODERN

Piano Educational Works

By Louise Robyn

A singularly successful and notable group of pedagogical works for the instruction of piano pupils and for the development of young musicians, founded upon the author's wide experience as child educator and psychologist.

TECHNIC TALES - Book One

May be used in conjunction with any first grade instruction book for the piano. It contains the most essential principles for beginner piano technique, building up the child's hand so that his finger dexterity is not injured by wrong positions, but is improved by easy studies in simple phrasing and rhythmic reading. The contents are adaptable to any first-year piano course. Price, 75 cents.

TEACHER'S MANUAL TO TECHNIC TALES - BOOK ONE

Is an indispensable book for the teacher. Price, 75 cents.

TECHNIC TALES - Book Two

A continuation of Technic Tales, Book I, for the second year of study at the piano. It contains further technical principles including the trill, arm stroke for single tones and trill, various crossing problems, alternate wrist action, finger extension, velocity tone exercises, repeated tones, two-note slurs, etc. Teachers who use this work with careful attention will have their pupils develop delightful technical facility and natural musicianship. Price, 75 cents.

TEACHER'S MANUAL TO TECHNIC TALES - BOOK TWO

Is published as a convenience to the teacher. Price, 75 cents.

CHORD CRAFTERS Technic Tales - Book Three

The tremendous success of Miss Robyn's "Technic" books, Books 1 and 2, has created a demand for the facility with the pupil's study and preparation of chord forms. These books are especially valuable as bridging lists between technical progressions. Chord Crafters is built upon the same principles as the two previous books and contains many original ideas which will be welcomed by teachers. Price, 75 cents.

THE SNOW QUEEN

A Story Cycle of Piano Pieces for Young Players

Music by P. Tchaikowsky

Story from Hans Christian Andersen

Adaptation by Louise Robyn

It is suggested that teachers use this book with Chord Crafters, Book One, because it gives the pupil an opportunity to develop a sense of interpretation through study of a short cycle of interesting pictorial compositions. The book will be used with great pleasure by the pupil and teacher and will provide the pupil with a delightful introduction to the technical world. Price, 75 cents.

ROBYN ROTE CARDS

Teachers, everywhere, use the Robyn rote-cards with wonderful satisfaction. These small cards are plainly printed and free from words that confuse. They are arranged so that they may be used for sight reading, rhythm drill and for quick interval recognition. Many sizes and forms are available and they are extremely useful for class teaching and sight-reading drills. Price, 75 cents.

THE ROBYN-GURLITT

85 Etudes to Develop Sight Reading, Pedal Technique and Rhythm

With annotations and explanations for the teacher, this book introduces a much needed department for the piano pupil. The author has developed a system that simplifies and clarifies the etude; has made the material progressive and logical, with pointed examples showing right fingering and phrasing. The result is an etude book which will appeal to both teacher and pupil for its originality and practical value. Price, 75 cents.

THE ROBYN-HANON

25 Exercises with Special Annotations and Explanations for the Teacher

From Hanon's Barley Book, The Virtuoso Pianist, Miss Robyn has selected the best daily technic exercises especially adapted to the young pupil, giving the correct fingering, pedalling and phrasing. This book is arranged to meet the needs of modern teaching by excluding the mechanical monotony and presenting in simple form the necessary facility and control. Price, 75 cents.

THE ROBYN-HANKS HARMONY BOOK ONE

By Louise Robyn and Howard Hanks

A modern course for students of any age, in written harmony, keyboard harmony and ear training. It is written for class or private lesson use and is adapted to the pupil who wishes a practical and up-to-date treatment of harmony. The book contains many examples and exercises for voice, piano or vocalized keyboard. Price, 75 cents.

THE ROBYN-HANKS HARMONY BOOK TWO

By Louise Robyn and Howard Hanks

This book continues the development of the material in Book One and includes a Master Key for the teacher. Parts of principle experience have been incorporated, with practical exercises for modern composition and arranging. Price, 75 cents.

THE ROBYN-HANKS HARMONY BOOK THREE

By Louise Robyn and Howard Hanks

This more recent work in the series of practical harmony is the most useful to the pupil who wishes to continue advanced study in composition, arranging and modern harmonic practice and also includes material designed for the teacher. Price, 75 cents.

KINDER CONCERTO

By Joseph Haydn

Arranged for Two Pianos by Louise Robyn

Miss Robyn here gives a carefully graded and most pleasing adaptation of Haydn's famous concerto. The edition is adapted to the needs of children and contains suggestions for phrasing and expression. Price, 75 cents.

KINDER CONCERTO

By Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

An attractive class piece for young pianists. Miss Robyn presents this arrangement from Mozart's famous themes in a musical and pedagogical form. It is especially useful for students who wish an opportunity for ensemble work. Price, 75 cents.

HERE, THERE AND EVERYWHERE IN THE MUSICAL WORLD

EUGENE LIST was the soloist with the New York Philharmonic-Symphony Orchestra on January 1, when the "Concerto for Piano and Orchestra" by Carlos Chavez was given its first performance. Dimitri Mitropoulos was guest conductor. The "Concerto" was repeated January 4th on the regular Sunday afternoon broadcast of the orchestra with Mitropoulos again conducting.

BLACKOUTS APPARENTLY HELD NO TERRORS for musically inclined folks on the Pacific Coast. Reports tell of open-air symphony events patronized as never before. The San Francisco Opera Company has had a most successful season; the San Francisco Symphony Orchestra is having a gala thirtieth anniversary season.

ASTRID VARNEY, twenty-three-year-old singer, in her first year with the Metropolitan Opera Company, has created something more than a sensation with her recent amazing handling of two late minute assignments. First as Sieglinde and then as Brünnhilde, she replaced veteran singers, suddenly indisposed, and without even rehearsals showed herself able to fulfill the roles in a truly amazing manner.

ROBERT HOOPER BOWERS, composer and arranger, conducted for Victor Herbert, first recorded December 27, in New York City. Born in Chambersburg, Pennsylvania, he studied music with Thomas Whinnery and with Frederick Grant Gleason. His chief composition is "A Hero's Lullaby" from "East Is West."

MARTY MORGAN, well known American harpist, died early in December at Prince's Bay, Staten Island, at the age of eighty-one. She had a notable career, having appeared with the Boston, Philadelphia, and other world famous artists.

MARY LEWIS, former Metropolitan Opera soprano, died in New York City on December 24. She had been active on the concert stage and in radio, and had appeared in many important roles in the opera. Mrs. Lewis made her debut in Vienna in 1902. Her debut at the Metropolitan Opera Company was made in 1906 as Mimi in "La Boheme."

THE WORLD PREMIERE of Czecho-slovak, (sic) by Emil Hlobil, composer, probably will be given in 1942. Hlobil has also written chamber works and songs, and his compositions have been widely performed.

THE PHILADELPHIA OPERA COMPANY, under Sylvan Levin, director, gave five performances in Boston, January 7-10. The operas presented were "The Marriage of Figaro," "Pelleas et Melisande," "Der Rosenkavalier," "Die Fledermaus," and "Faust."

JACOB HENRY HALL, veteran normal school music teacher, writer and editor, died on December 28, in Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, at the age of 78. An authority on hymn writers and composers, he was widely known as a conductor of choral groups and as a music methods writer. For many years he was associated with William H. Requardt in music publishing business.

CHARLES HACKETT, distinguished American baritone and teacher, died on January 1st in New York. Born in Ellisston (?), November 4, 1889, he began studying voice in Boston. Later he studied in Italy. One of his first important operatic appearances was at the age of nineteen, when he sang with Lillian Nordica in performances of "Rigoletto." Hacket's musical career embraced concerts and opera, and he was on the faculty of the New England Conservatory of Music. He sang with the Chicago Civic Opera Company in 1923.

THE BOSTON SYMPHONY ORCHESTRA gives its fifty-first season of concerts this year, opening in October and continuing through April. Serge Koussevitzky is the conductor.

THE CINCINNATI SYMPHONY ORCHESTRA is again featured with special programs, and its season under Fritz Reiner is proving most successful.

THE UNIVERSITY OF TEXAS COLLEGE OF FINE ARTS has ordered a thirty-five thousand dollar pipe organ for use in the new music building. The organ, built by E. M. Skinner, will be used for recitals and teaching purposes.

THE RECORD OPERATIC CORPORA-TION (sic) has been formed recently to further the careers of young American artists. The new organization will make and distribute records for artists of the younger school.

Youth and Music

On the American Plan
By Blanche Lemmon

AT THIS VERY HOUR there are dozens of young Americans planning and studying for musical careers. Their names may be Smith or Jones, they may have obtained their musical education in the United States; in fact, they may never have set foot outside the borders of their native land. But they can aspire to high places in opera, concert, radio, movies — the whole entertainment field. They have no barriers to success except perhaps a few who lack initiative.

Thirty, even twenty-five years ago, this was not true. Young American artists could cherish the idea of musical careers in their own country only if funds were available to enable them to go to Europe. Unless their American publicity could carry such statements as "study under Leschetitzky," "debut at the Royal Opera House," "student at the Paris Conservatoire," "has played before Royalty in five countries," or some similar indication of European training and triumph, they stood little chance of attracting audiences in their native land. As for names, only those that sounded exotic had appeal. For American concertgoers. Plain, understandable common names were nothing; "foreign" names were "rich" and "romantic" were necessary. It was an invitation to chicanery.

Fortunately the era of European bias is past, and a new tolerance has taken its place. We now recognize talent for what it is worth, regardless of its source. Though tenacity of purpose, American ability finally has won its own audience, and by the test of comparison it has not been found wanting. To-day our greatest opera companies and our musical organizations of longest rank and achievement permit American-trained artists to membership and ready as they do artists of any other nationality.

A contributing factor to this unprecedented state of affairs has been the work of the National Federation of Music Clubs, which first brought to the attention of the American public the fact that American training was of the best, and which long ago decided that concerning musical, recreational talent should be given a chance to build a career right here in its native country. To substantiate its views it instituted a series of contests that would test young ability and reward outstanding talent, both with honor and with money. It is a plan to which it has adhered for twenty-eight years, with gratifying results.

Worth While Contests
Biennially over this period of time, it has given young singers, pianists and violinists in this country opportunity, through elimination, to match their ability against that of other young musicians, first on their state, second, in district contests, and finally, in a national contest. To those winning first place in each of the three classifications at the final contest a ward of one thousand dollars has been made. In conjunction with state and district competitions there were also prizes for club contestants, and at the district contests concerts were given to selected winners, judges, and the public. The benefits to every instrumentalist in this plan are pre-eminent.

The candidate for national honors must have demonstrated a temperament and a development of technique sufficient to meet the test of the more exacting repertoire of the world's greatest orchestras and opera houses. Contest contestants so selected must be approved by a majority of judges to be eligible for the final competition. The results are, in many cases, surprising and inspiring.

The Philharmonic-Symphony Orchestra in New York City. The list of the many winners is long and impressive and has included names which have become familiar to concert-goers in America and abroad.

Any person expecting to make music a career may compete if he is between eighteen and thirty years of age, is a native or a naturalized American, has received training in the United States, and can guarantee an adequate repertoire. The required repertoire for piano and violin consists of three concerti and two recital programs, lasting one hour each; for voice, three selections with orchestra and two recitals or two programs, lasting an hour each. All selections must be played from memory, and in the vocal contest for vocal honors, two groups of songs must be sung in the original language. The only expense involved is a moderate fee for admission to the contest, and, if the young artist is fortunate enough to get to a district and national contest, he must bear his own transportation expenses.

Coincident with the Young Artists Contest the Federation holds Student Musicians Contests for younger people between the ages of seventeen and twenty-seven. These contests cover six classifications instead of three, as in the Young Artists Contest: piano, violin, man's voice, woman's voice, violoncello and organ. They are held for advanced students who have not yet reached the "artist" class. The awards are certificates signed by State and District Presidents and the National President.

In some states preliminary auditions are held in clubs or cities for the Young Artists Contest; in others, the State Contest is the first step taken. To this we must have made application to the State Contest Chairman, and from it proceed the winners in the three classifications. About a month's time elapses between these two contests, and approximately another month divides the District Contests from the National Contest, which is held conjointly with the Federation's Biennial Convention.

The 1941 Convention
Last year the Federation's Convention was held in Los Angeles and to it went winners from sixteen districts: twelve vocalists, twelve pianists and eight violinists. From these would ordinarily be chosen three pianists and three violinists and one vocalist to compete for final honors at the National Contest. Each year the procedures are somewhat different as the changes in rules and conditions require. The contest is, however, a constant source of stimulation to the artists who enter it, and the results have been of notable significance for American musical life.

(Continued) The final decisions are made at Los Angeles, where the last national audition was conducted. The competition is not only a test of musicianship but of poise and personality. Some winners have developed concert careers comparable with those of artists trained abroad. The proof is in the quality of their performances.

(Caption under lower left portrait)
CARROLL GLENN, Violinist. Winner of the contest conducted by the Federation of Musical Clubs.

(continued column)
In proportion as the participants in the Federation contests have matured their public appearances have improved. Many young artists who gained initial recognition through these contests have gone on to successful careers with the best orchestras and have made important radio appearances. The contests have created opportunities and given impetus to a number of careers which otherwise might have been forced to seek the European market to find an audience.

There are criticisms from time to time that competitions encourage a type of musicianship that is more concerned with showmanship than with deep musical understanding. There is also the charge that the pressure of competition may retard an artist's creative development. Such criticisms are not without merit, but on balance the Federation's work has been of untold value in stimulating musical life in America.

The Federation's contests have been supplemented by scholarships and fellowships enabling winners to continue study in this country or abroad. These awards, together with the prestige conferred, have often been the deciding factor in the career of a young artist. The Federation's policy of encouraging home development has been a vital contribution to American cultural independence.

Why They Succeeded

AFTER THE LATE AND UNLAMENTED depression of two years ago, numbers of musicians came to us in person and others approached us through the mails, asking for our counsel in the matter of securing profitable employment. They wanted to know how to retain their pupils and secure new pupils.

Generalities upon how to succeed are usually not particularly valuable. Probably Andrew Carnegie was right in his advice given upon this page. The real masters in music to-day, the real experts, are so much in demand that many are earning huge incomes yearly. Every real success is an individual success. In most cases the teacher asking for assistance was able, prepared to give fine service and apparently not wanting in enterprise. In a few instances it was possible to diagnose the cause of the teachers' difficulty and to suggest a remedy. The main cause of failure was usually that the teacher had given tragically little concern to the direction in which he desired to go. Many were working for invisible objectives. Such teachers were underless, drifting aimlessly upon a sea which sooner or later brought them to the rocks of disaster.

We put down some memoranda about the problems presented to us, thinking that others might be interested in them. Here they are. The initials are naturally fictitious:

M. L. Up to 1929 this teacher had "plenty of pupils." His pupils had a reputation for doing fine work. The teacher's health, character and social background were excellent. When the banks closed, all of his pupils suddenly discontinued. After this occurred he found that it was almost impossible to get them to start again. What was the difficulty? He had not the common sense to see that the practical thing to do was to share the misfortune of his patrons. Instead, as a sop to his pride, he kept up the former high price he had charged for lessons. What he should have done was to have called his pupils together and told them that he understood the disaster brought about by the depression and that he would be glad to teach them for a fee they could afford. In some cases he should have told individual pupils, confidentially, that he would teach them without any fee whatsoever. In that way he would have saved members

(continued right column)
of his clientele until they were able to resume normal payments. This is precisely what many business men were forced to do in extending credits to financially embarrassed customers.

Moral: Adjust yourself to conditions happily when there is no alternative.

X. de L. This teacher, after a series of misfortunes, became more and more depressed. This was manifested in neglect of dress, facial expression, behavior. There were no basic mental abnormalities other than a violent case of the blues. The teacher was made to see that she would not think of patronizing such a person as she might see reflected in her own mirror. She was advised to cultivate a merrier, happier view of life, take long walks in the open air, attend comedies, bright moving pictures, and read entertaining magazines and books, as well as to consort with cheerful people, instead of visiting physicians in search of tonics. She was advised to adopt a warmer home music to suit her. She started practicing with a new and energetic spirit. In a surprisingly short time she had a fine supporting class, which has grown regularly ever since.

Moral: Business runs toward confident optimism.

G. F. This teacher was ignorant of the simplest economic law, the law of supply and demand. The neighborhood in which she lived had gradually changed. Parents with their children had moved to the suburbs. What was the solution? She was advised to give up the studio in the old part of town. She was also advised to secure an automobile if possible, even though bought upon long terms. She was then to divide the outlying suburban sections into districts and to "cover" one district each day. She was then counseled to go from house to house, ringing doorbells, until she found a house in which her services might be needed. She was duly horrified by this suggestion. She insisted that her professional pride would not permit it. "Besides," she said, "I would not blame anyone for throwing out such an intruder." She was persuaded to see that the depended upon her tact and her lady-like approach in persuading her potential patrons that she was able to offer something which they greatly needed.

Music and Culture

They Fiddle for Fun

By
Nathan Cohen

LAWRENCE TIBBETT STOOD on the stage of the Duluth Armory auditorium and took a bow to the air until from “La Traviata,” as his voice faded into the acoustics of an orchestral flurry, a man from among the second violins rose, waved a mysterious signal to the conductor, bounded off the stage, and out to the company of two policemen at the door.

A bow later the man returned, gingerly made his way through the rows of string players and resumed his post. He smiled at the conductor. The conductor smiled back and nodded knowingly. Tibbettt looked nonplused. He never before had had one of the orchestra walk out on him during a concert.

The fiddler was Dr. Wil A. Ryan, violinist of the Duluth Symphony Orchestra. Dropping his violin and dashing to a hospital was odd stuff to him—and to his colleagues in the orchestra. Once he left a rehearsal, rushed to a hospital, removed an appendix, and was back in time to finish the evening’s musical job.

The emergency which had sent him hurrying from the Tibbet concert was an unexpected arrival in the maternity ward of a Duluth hospital. With the help of two frantic patrolmen at the door, he made the journey just in time. There was no time left to change into his spotless white uniform, which the nurses had ready for him, so he delivered an eight-pound boy, wearing his evening clothes. The mother took one look and said, “Doctor, I didn’t know who was going to be born.”

It the seven years that Paul Lemay has conducted the Duluth Orchestra, he has become accused to having his musicians turn off the platform. With shopkeepers, house painters, real estate salesmen, shoemakers, bakers, dentists and doctors stealing time from business to play Bach, Beethoven and Brahms, the job of being a conductor offered no strict adherence to an orchestral time table.

A Schedule of Surprises

The Duluth Orchestra has been running on a schedule of surprises for ten years. It has had as its guest soloists such concert artists as Heifetz, Rachmaninoff, Elman, Spalding and Hofmann. But when it was born as a stable on a stormy night, no one had expected it to last. It was pure fancy then to think that a small city along the north shore of Lake Superior would support a full-fledged symphony orchestra. What businessmen would give up hard earned depression cash to help a half hundred fiddlers, trumpeters and woodwind players perform a lot of music few of them could understand?

Duluth was putting on one of its famous snow-and-wind festivals out of the night that the local enthusiasts met in an old stable to organize. Two weeks before, Alphon Flaherty and Larry Willits, two violinists out of work, had been drinking bitter cups of coffee over the unhappy siege of the depression. Pictures with sound had swept them and their colleagues out of the theater pit. They couldn’t outdistance the vitaphone. But the threadbare days had made these two realize that fellows like Beethoven, Mozart and Brahms were still being played; that there was something left to fiddle beside the tremolos of Hearts and Flowers and the bristling prestos that had accompanied the chase of the Keystone Kops in the days of silent movies.

So the two set a date to find out how many of their one-time associates were left who remembered what fun it was to zap through a Rossini overture or blow pastoral fanfares out of a French horn. They soon found out. The musical grapevine spread their call through the surrounding countryside. Everybody who ever had blown an “oom-pah” through a Legion band horn or drawn a bow over a string wanted to be in on the orchestra. Up in the Mesaba ore country, seventy miles north; across St. Louis Bay, in Wisconsin; everybody wanted to fiddle, blow or pound away the depression.

Flattened in an old garage. In bygone days it had been a luxurious stable, and the second floor had been the handsome living quarters for servants of one of the town’s finest families. When Pleaten bought the place, he dreamed of remodeling it into the finest music studio in all the North. Its panelled walls had been finished out to the star-studded oak of the Minnesota woods. The floor was fashioned out of birdseye maple. A huge stone fireplace gave the room a rich atmosphere of luxury.

Rehearsal under Difficulty

With the night for the rehearsal came the worst blizzard of the year. The garage was white. The light, which hung out as a guide to the musicians, blinked fitfully through the storm. Upstairs, snow had lifted itself up through the broken window panes. And, down on the floor, on hands and knees, were the two violinists, struggling desperately to start a violin under a four-foot chunk of birch they had jagged in.

“It’ll be an A-string no one shows up,” said the violoncellist, Flaherty.

“I’ll be a clar,” they do,” wagered the other. The door opened, and in walked Alfred Morrin, the Mesaba ore-digging oboe player. He had driven sixty miles through the snow. From Cloquet, a paper-mill town twenty-eight miles up the river, came Lloyd Brisett, a tuba player. From Superior, in Wisconsin, came Oscar Bramsdaver, a clothier who steals away from his shop every afternoon to practice his violin concertos, and Helen Cleveland, a four-foot-eight double bass player who had stormed her way past the driver to get her instrument into the crowded bus. The professionals who hadn’t been in a theater pit for two years turned up en masse. Dr. Ryan arrived with an apology. “A stubborn maternity case,” he said.

When Walter Lange, paper specialties salesman turned conductor, stepped up to tap the stick to start the rehearsal, he faced an orchestra the like of which no other leader ever had met. He could boast of an ore digger, a newspaper publisher, a real estate salesman, a house painter, a surgeon, a dentist, five housewives, a printer, an artist, and, fortunately, two score ex-professionals.

The fine old stable swelled with musical pride that night. When the big burned down, boxes and crates were hauled up and tossed into the fire-place, and when the fire finally burned itself into cool embers, the musicians put on overcoats and huddled, tooted, and drummed until Courtduor Lange’s fingers became so cold he couldn’t hold his stick.

“Boys,” he said, “we’ll try again on Sunday. Bring your fiddles—and don’t forget the cord-wood.”

Enter, or Good Angel

For ten years, they have fiddled for fun, these musicians of Duluth. When their luxury stable got too cold, they gave it up and hiked to a paint shop where pots, barrels and half-completed bill-boards lent color to the musical scene. The morning after a bull-fiddle player went through the head of a barrel of white lead, however, the players scattered themselves through the town in search of an angle who could bless them with a heated hall. The angel they found in A. H. Moe, recorder of the Shrine temple. “You can have it for a song,” he said; and at the next rehearsal he got his own shoes, the Angel’s Serenade, played by musicians who were practicing their symphonic exercises without overcoats and hats for the first time in months.

The boys still talk about their first concert. They got the newspapers to promote it. The colors of the field artillery regiment gave them the Armory auditorium and Ernest Lachmund, a Duluth composer, wrote them a tone poem. Quite appropriately he called it The Adventurers.

Four thousand Duluthians packed themselves into the Armory. When Concertmaster Herbert Mikel led his troupe to their chairs, the xylophone effect that came from fifty pairs of knocking knees would have provided an ideal accompaniment for the dancing skeletons of Danse Macabre. The musicians took their places, nervously rearranged their music and looked out at the familiar faces in the audience. The townsfolk looked up and smiled. They smiled at the man who banded their bread; at the doctor who delivered their babies; at Gudrun Momb, who sold them their gloves at the Glass Block store; at Bob Olander, who painted their houses.

ONE OF THE QUESTIONS most frequently asked by students is how to use the practice period to best advantage. I am glad to answer it, but I wish to make it clear that I am not a vocal teacher. I have no system or "method" to advocate for the work of others; I only give my own convictions and my own work. In my opinion, then, all practicing should begin with work on tone. No matter how many other details of technique are to be taken later, the first step each day must be the warming up of the voice. By warming up, I do not mean technical fluency, but probing for quality. If you have ever watched a violinist begin his daily work, you know that the first thing he does is to draw the bow across the strings, to assure himself of his tonal values. It is even more important that the singer begin in the same way. The violinist, at least, uses his instrument in his hands—it is there, a tangible physical entity, ready to obey his wishes; all he need do is to assure himself that its strings are correctly and musically tuned. The singer needs to examine the purpose of his work in the same way, but in addition he needs to assure himself of the status of his instrument—which is not a tangible thing. The voice is an internal instrument; it is part of the human body and consequently reflects the slightest changes in physical, mental, and nervous vigor. Thus, the first thing the singer should do is to make sure of the quality of his instrument.

Preliminary Practicing

The first singing should, therefore, be for tone quality alone. It should be done slowly, moderately, with no extremes of any sort. In my own work, I begin every day on certain vowels which I keep very slow, always in the middle (o, or), and always on the sound of O—a clear O, not OO, and not the short O as in hot. I begin with the first five notes of the scale, quite like an pianist's five-finger exercises, beginning on a comfortable low note of my middle register, and never going above E or F. Then I bring on the next tone and carry that up for five notes and back; then on the third tone, and so on, until I have encompassed a full octave.

I cannot stress sufficiently that this preliminary practicing must be done slowly, carefully, with open throat, with no tension of any kind, and without any probing of range. Its purpose is solely to explore, to settle, and to warm up the tonal quality of the voice. Next, with the scale following step by step, singing equally on each note and on the long tone, as lf, first the scale, again slowly up in the middle register, and again on A. Next come exercises in thirds. When the tone is well placed, and when it

feels sure and properly arched, I sing the scale somewhat faster, then still more quickly. Next, I sing the scale on all the different vowel sounds. I have found it helpful not to sing the full scale on any one vowel (after the preliminary work on O), but to change the vowel with each note of the scale. For ex- ample, I may complete an octave on O—A—E (ay)—I—ee—OO—oo—oo—ee—aa—O—O—O—O, repeating the variations of vowels on the way some sole and resonance. I find this extremely useful in exploring tone and resonance, and in recognizing the different color of any one vowel. I often take up more than one vowel at a time, frequently O or more often three or four of the other vowels. This is because for my voice, at least, the sound of O sets tone and resonance most naturally. Next in order, then, is practice more elaborate and various exercises, first very slowly, on O; and then more quickly on varied vowel sounds. An exercise which I finally found useful for probing tone-quality and warming up ranges is one in which begins on the intervals of the common chord and goes always a half-tone higher, descending on intervals based on the extra half-tone. Example: Ascending, A, C-sharp, E, A. C-sharp, D; descending, B, G-sharp, E, B, A. Then repeat, beginning on the next half-tone higher, until the range has been comfortably explored. This is an excellent drill in making use of range, quality and intonation.

These are my regular daily exercises—always begun slowly, always used as careful probes of quality and never sung forte. In the preliminary practice, nothing should be exaggerated; strict moderation should be observed in force, in range, in volume—even everything! From this point on, when the voice is warm, well arched and secure, individual points in technique may be begun. I hesitate to speak of these, because no two singers have exactly the same needs—except the preliminary probing and warming up of the voice. I never sing, rehearse, or practice without using these preliminary exercises.

Importance of Coloratura Exercises

For the second step in my practicing, I am guided by the nature of the work I have to do. If, for example, I am to sing Erda in an evening performance, I follow my preliminary work with exercises calculated to adapt the voice to a lower, deeper color. If I am to sing Venus, I need work that will adapt it to higher, brighter color. Always, it is the color of the voice that must be worked; never the range alone.

At the present time, I do not need to practice special exercises in breathing or breath support. I advocate them, however, for students whose vocal habits are not yet secure. Correct breathing and firmness of support are the basis of all good singing—indeed, the quality of the tone and the character of the singing are the best indications as to whether or not further drill in breath and support work is needed. If the singing is correct, it shows that the foundation of breath and support is in good order. The thing to watch for is that all the breath be utilized as tone. If the tone is unsteady, it is a sign that some of the breath is escaping as air—and that means, in turn, that further attention to breath work is needed.

Coloratura exercises should be included in the development of every voice, male and female, regardless of scale or range. However, these drills should never be undertaken until the voice is slowly, simply, exploratory exercises have been sung. Always one must first be sure of the quality of the tone—also, one must be sure that this quality is as secure in rapid work as in slow. The work of singing does not depend on the length of the breath but on the use of the breath. Regardless of the kind of singing one does, the tone quality must always be uniformly secure and correct. Since one cannot control one's work as well in fast passages as in slow ones, the slow, thorough practicing must come first.

I never was any "Joe" to be wooed by applause. I have always wished to be alone in practice; I could not stand the idea of being watched, and I was very gracious to those who praised me. But while such things might be mere mannerism in some adolescent, the habit of withdrawing from public performance often becomes a fixed and most unfortunate trait. The only thing that will cure it is frequent, steady performance of various types before different audiences. Encourage the pupil to take every occasion for playing with composure and with the conviction that his art is a serious and useful one.

A few hints about things for recital or contest preparation: Start the pieces as soon as the selection is made. Take up and "drop" the numbers that are hardest; always do the easier things for warm-up. Do not play the program through to the end in one long continuous performance until you are thoroughly ready — the end often reveals more than we like to see about left-hand weakness, about lack of breath, and other defects. Play your entire program in public whenever possible. Do not make the mistake of practicing only in your room.

Needless to say, I also avoid the "threat" of a recital; I prefer to plan at least one or two concerts a year, but not to make them the sole purpose of the pupil's life. I want the work to be steady, and to proceed without hysteria.

Dynamite in Songs
Tunes That Turned the Tide of History

By Doron K. Antrim

THE HISTORY OF A NATION is written largely in its songs. They reflect, as nothing else, the inner feelings of the people; their temperament, hopes and fears, ways of life. The war time especially, songs are far superior to edicts and oratory in rallying a country to a cause; in stirring its will to win, in sustaining morale. "Wars are won," said General Pershing, "by good songs as well as good soldiers." Down the ages the songs that influenced the course of history built large. Some have even turned the tide of history. Born usually of travail, their effect upon a people in times of crises has been far reaching. It is a few of these that we would discuss here.

God Save the King
The oldest national anthem, the one that influenced the history of more nations than any other, is England's God Save the King. If this song had not appeared when it did, and if it had not steadied the nerves of the English people time and again during his long tenure, English history might have been far different.

On the morning of September 28, 1745, a report reached London which caused consternation. The English forces sent to stop Charles Edward, leader of the Jacobite rebellion, had been routed. A Jacobite invasion of England seemed certain, and there was considerable support for the Jacobite cause there. Unless something could be done quickly the country would be divided by war.

The Anti-Jacobites held that George II, with all his shortcomings, was better for the country than another James. What could they do to squash the rebellion and swing sentiment to their cause? It was felt there was only one recourse — the leader. And strangely when a great need exists, a song is often born to fill it. That same evening Henry Carey sat in Drury Lane Theatre writing. A new number, the words of which he said he had written, was laid aside. Later the news reached town that the Jacobite forces had been defeated, and Carey, seizing his manuscript, hurried into the streets shouting "God save our King!" The crowd repeated the phrase again and again, and the song became at once the national cry.

There has been some dispute as to the authorship of the words, but they were certainly widely used and played a part in steadying England during the trying days of the '45 rebellion. The tune, in truth, is one of the oldest in the world. It is based upon a much earlier air and perhaps owes its origin to an Italian or French source. However that may be, its effect upon English history cannot be denied.

Triumphant. It is probably the best known tune in the world.

The French Revolution
Another song that played a decisive part in world history is the French Marseillaise. Captain Rouget de Lisle, amateur violinist and ardent patriot, struck it off in the white heat of his ardor overnight to rally recruits for the French Revolution. "Vive la nation!" read the poster the morning of April 24, 1792. In Marseilles, calling for volunteers. A soldier was singing de Lisle's song in the streets and passing out copies. In two days nine hundred men had joined. They marched to Paris singing the song, then on to the downfall of the Tuileries. France had become a Republic, and the march became the Marseillaise.

This song proved to be dynamite. It quickly spread all over Europe, becoming a symbol of revolt, being banned in a number of countries. Its influence in inciting people to break with crowned heads was considerable.

As a martial air, de Lisle, who wrote both words and music, achieved a masterpiece in the tune he caught the nervous, impetuous temperament of his countrymen. Its urgent rhythm, its use of syncopation, its high note in the first line, like a dawn sword, flashing in the sun, are not devices of an amateur such as de Lisle, but of an experienced composer.

Belgium Wins Independence
It was also by a song that Belgium won its independence. The night of August 25, 1830, a performance of Daniel Auber's opera La Muette de Portici at the Théâtre de la Monnaie in Brussels stirred the audience to revolt. The pathetic story of the opera aroused patriotic feeling. The people rushed into the streets crying for freedom. The riot spread; in a few days the Belgians were fighting for independence from Holland.

Why Not Beat Time?
By J. Clarence Cook

BEATING TIME with the foot is not sanctioned by many violin teachers. Probably they fear the student's avail will contract an uncontrollable habit that will manifest itself on the stage at recitals. Nevertheless, when it comes to a question of inculcating correct rhythm into the work of beginners on the violin, the foot proves to be the best time keeper in the world; for the simple reason that it beats time continually in its natural function of walking.

Let us realize at once that it is far more difficult to acquire good "time" on the violin than on the piano. Elementary studies and pieces for the piano generally have a rhythmic figure, or accompaniment, in the left hand that in itself constitutes a metronomic background and makes any other kind of beating or counting almost unnecessary. The violin student, however, is not only without this mechanical aid in the music he plays, but is also further handicapped by the irregular motions of his bow arm, which continually conflict with the actual beats of the music. A very good book for children is "Fiddling for Fun," by Rob Roy Peery. It is based on what might be called the better method of the violin than most of the older books.

four quarter measure as standard units. Write a line of quarter notes on the open A string and explain that each quarter note gets one beat. But what does the term "beat" mean to the child? Absolutely nothing, so far as music is concerned. We must affiliate the quarter note with something that falls within the range of his experience. Now ask him to walk leisurely across the room, counting his steps aloud. Have him stop at the eighth step. (It is just as well to begin immediately training his sense to the musical rhythm.) Explain that his steps are quarter notes; that is, they represent the speed at which quarter notes will be sounded. The average child grasps this idea quickly. It is unique and interesting to him. Next have him count off the steps standing still, that is, marking time. Finally teach him with tap the beats with his right foot alone, taking pains to retain approximately the same speed. We have now established in the child's mind a unit of time that is completely intelligible to him.

We assume that the pupil has already been taught to hold his violin and to bow on the open strings, so his next task will be to play the line of quarter notes and beat time to them. See that he follows the notes on the paper with his eyes. To ensure this doing, it is well at first to point to the notes as he plays them.

All this may seem to the adult like a tiresome process, but if we enter into the child's psychology, as every real teacher should, it becomes apparent that we are thereby giving a very wonderful and complex aid. We are unifying in that fresh young mind a threefold process, for we are establishing a definite relationship between the little black notes on the paper, the beating of the child's foot, and the audible production of the tones on his violin. And the term "beats" is justified by the fact that it is actually beating them with his foot. When he can play quarter notes and successfully beat time to them, teach him to beat half notes, dotted half notes, and whole notes. Of course, he need not be confined all this time to the open strings. The combinations learned so far may be utilized in many charming melodies, and it is to be presumed that his lessons in time, proper and in beat progression, will be the things in which his creative powers in rhythm will develop.

The dotted quarter, followed by an eighth note, presents one of the most difficult, and at the same time one of the most important lessons in the child's early development. Begin by writing a line of eighth notes and place over each note alternately the down and up bow signs. Have him play these notes with very short strokes, beating on the down bows and raising his foot on the up bows. Teaching him to take cognizance of the upward as well as the downward motion of his foot is going to prove very useful later on in his practice routine.

When taking up the study of analysis, many students are confused in distinguishing between the two very important elements of composition: namely, transition and modulation. Almost any piece of music which is once written will generally contain many instances of these two ingredients, so to speak.

What is the difference between transition and modulation? Transition is the borrowing of a new key momentarily. In other words, it is the art of setting one key inside another key. Transitory means fleeting; hence the temporary appearance of a new key, which obediently returns to the original key immediately after it has served its purpose of introducing a new color into the composition. Many of these transitions may occur in a single phrase.

Here is an illustration showing a few transitional changes in the course of an eight measure period:

[Musical example labeled Ex. 1 with small staves and harmonic annotations, showing modulation steps V of V etc.]

It is erroneous to suppose that all of these borrowed chords are modalizations. The new key is temporarily suggested, but, as will be noted, it is quickly followed either by a chord belonging to the original key or by a harmonization identified with still another borrowed key. Before the ear has had time to become accustomed to the introduction of a new tonality, the scene is shifted again into another color. This lends almost a kaleidoscopic effect to the music, making it more complicated, but at the same time more interesting because of its various vocabulary. A piece of music which contains many transitions is naturally more difficult to transpose at sight — for the average reader — than is a composition which adheres more closely to the original key. At the same time, this constant shifting lends color and interest to music which might otherwise become dull and monotonous.

Transition and modulation serve different purposes; therefore one cannot be said to be an adequate substitute for the other. Each is designed to create a different effect.

A composition which contains a few modulations is much more clever and interesting if these modulations are brought about without seeming to strive for effect or to strain the credulity of the listener. These changes must be smooth and by all means should not jump erratically or unexpectedly to and from various keys, whether with the voice of extraneous relationships.

What are closely related keys? They are the dominant and subdominant majors, their relative minors, and the relative minor of the given major key. In computing the first relationship keys to a minor tonic, the order is naturally reversed. It is easily understood why these keys are termed first relationships, because there are so few new accidentals with which to contend.

The second cousins are those whose tonics occur in the natural scale (aside from those already mentioned) or some of the chromatically altered keys. For example, in starting from C-major, the keys of D, E, A and B or D-flat, E-flat, A-flat and B-flat will afford the desired second relationships.

What are the extraneous relationships? From the starting point of C-major they are C-sharp, D-sharp, F-sharp and G-sharp, either major or minor modes. These are considered distantly related because of the difficulties involved in the many added accidentals.

It is safe to suppose, then, that all augmented and diminished intervals furnish the required extraneous relationships. Because of the intricacies of the new sharps, double sharps, flats and double flats inserted, it is natural that these keys cannot be so easily approached by modulation through the common device known as the pivot chord. Therefore, it is necessary to evolve other means which will create a smooth, well oiled impression upon the listener. This may be effected by the use of the modulatory cadence, or in other words, by the melding of one dominant formation in the original key into a chord of dominant quality in the desired key.

Example 4 shows the contrast of the pivot chord modulation between tonality near to one another and more melodically cadenced modulation which is generally employed between keys more or less foreign in relationship. In this instance the modulation is from C to G.

The Fascinating Art of Practicing
By Andor Foldes

The brilliant Hungarian Piano Virtuoso

Andor Foldes, sensationally successful Hungarian pianist, was born at Budapest less than thirty years ago. He played with the Royal Hungarian Philharmonic Orchestra at the age of seven, making a pronounced success. Later he studied with Ernst von Dohnanyi and played with many of the leading orchestras of Europe. Prominent contemporary composers have written piano concerti for him. His European successes have been repeated in America. —Editor's Note.

CAN PRACTICING properly be called an art? Butter‑Lyon contends, "Art always employs method for the symmetrical foundation of beauty; a science employs it for the logical exposition of truth." Practice is the methodical development of system and interpretative ideas. If, therefore, the playing of a difficult concert program or even of a single complicated piece is considered a work of art, requiring not only natural gifts, skilled hands, poetic feeling, and personality, and in addition, a background of a number of years of persevering home study, I think we might call the method by which this knowledge itself is achieved a true art. The way in which even the very gifted student must proceed in realizing their dream at the piano is through that slow process of perfection which real musicianship is inconceivable without. It certainly deserves the name, "practice." It is both an art and a science. How to practice—for many teachers the only true art—has never ceased to puzzle teachers and pupils. Every pupil has asked such a question and will tell you what kind of practice he follows. A great piano pedagogue was once asked what he considered the real goal of a piano instruction.

"To teach the pupil how to practice," was his answer.

Certainly practicing never is easy. It becomes difficult when we have achieved a certain degree of self-control and self-discipline; when the mind wishes to throw off the old habits and to develop new interpretations and new tonal beauty. It is easier in the beginning when the pupil's first love for the piano is still fresh, to spend hour after hour in delight over new discoveries. As time goes on and the difficulties increase the original enthusiasm is likely to wane. The teacher's chief task is to sustain that enthusiasm and to show the pupil that daily work brings results and joy. It is necessary to teach from the very beginning the formation of correct habits. Young pupils must be impressed with the need of rhythmical exactness, with the accent, with the shaping of phrases, with the touch and the use of pedaling. Too often the student's practice is a mere mechanical exercise of the fingers, devoid of the purpose which transforms technique into musical expression. A good method of practice should always be the servant of the musical idea and not an end in itself.

No one method suits everybody. The teacher must select the method best adapted to the pupil's temperament and ability. Some students prefer short, frequent sessions; others progress better by longer, concentrated periods. In the selection of pieces and in the division of the daily work, the teacher must consider not only the result wanted in the near future but also the development of a lasting style and taste. One should also beware of "playing through" as the chief means of practice. I know hardly any pupil who has advanced satisfactorily by merely playing his pieces from beginning to end, repeatedly. Indeed, the habit of "playing through" is usually a sign of ignorance of how to attack the difficulties of a composition. The practice must be analytic. One must isolate hard passages, slow them down, study them thoroughly and then join them gradually to the whole.

Some pupils are satisfied when they can render a passage technically correct. But the art of practicing has also to do with the temperament: the tone, the phrasing, the dynamic subtleties. To obtain these the mere slow repetition is often not enough. We must learn the logic of the phrase, the architecture of the piece, and use imaginative concentration. A pupil should study not only the notes but the reasons for them. We have to recognize the composer's thought and to let it guide our technique. If we can hear the phrase in our inner ear while practicing, we will not need to play it ten times without result. On the other hand, superficial impressions of a teacher's demonstration, or of a brilliant concert performance, may be misleading. One must never try to imitate blindly the mannerisms of another.

Take the case of a student who feels that his scales are uneven because his fourth finger is weak. He should devote some special exercises to the weak fourth finger and proceed with this at intervals for a few weeks. Some months later he may be studying a new Mozart sonata. He will then certainly be rewarded by the fact that his scales have improved immensely. He no longer has to worry about the delicate passages and, as a result, he is able to learn the sonata in much less time than it would have taken had he not done the exercises.

To simplify difficult passages is another very important office of practicing. Its object is to make "child's play" of a passage so that at first glance might seem unplayable. Many students are astonished by the ease and smoothness with which a great virtuoso plays a difficult and complicated piano composition. In apparent effortlessness, there are, for instance, Hannon, Czerny, Bach, Heller, and the preparatory studies of classical composers. Of course the answer is practice, but that is not enough; it must be the right kind of practice.

Ease While Practicing

Students who practice along fallacious lines can never possibly acquire the sympathetic spontaneity and dash which fine piano playing demands. For instance, if a student could see his face in a mirror while practicing, he would observe at once that he was under some nervous tension. When a difficult run or a troublesome passage comes along, he somehow manages to play it by a kind of nervous spasm, and then he continues to repeat this passage in the same ridiculous manner, under the delusion that he is practicing. Look out for such spasms. They are practice evils which have marred many a career. They are overcome by selecting shorter practice units and practicing slower in a relaxed condition until selections can be mastered with ease. "How relieved you are," asked a pupil of his teacher, "How can I tell when I am relaxed?" Well, this is one way. Sit normally at the piano, relax whether your back muscles, your neck muscles, or your shoulders. Breathe and if you are tense or strained, try to relieve the tension. If you want to play a passage which shows fretful and jerky motions, take it slowly, think of the phrase and the tonal effects you wish to obtain, and keep your wrists, arms and shoulders free of all stiffness. In slower tempo, the short sections are perfected and then joined together. It is in this way that the whole is finally produced with evenness and fluency. Once we have acquired the habit of correct, slow practice, we shall find that the result will remain after the speed is increased.

False Impressions

Interminable damage has been done to piano pupils who are under the impression that they must learn a new opus at every lesson. They have been built up to practice while they were reading a book. This may have been the case, but we can hardly make many rules. There is certainly no reason for encouraging the desire to learn as many things as possible at any cost. The want of concentration is the chief enemy of steady advancement. It is far better to know thoroughly a smaller repertoire than to have a superficial acquaintance with a large number of pieces. Good practice means concentrated effort. A few measures thoroughly studied will often give better results than hours of indifferent labor. Practice with attention, intelligence and musical imagination. Avoid mechanical monotony. If the pupil has a definite aim, he will not be misled by fancy.

Do not be discouraged at slow progress. In almost every career of musical development there are slow periods which must be patiently endured. Believe the teacher and trust the method. Keep always in mind the end to be achieved and do not be satisfied with second best. It is a thousand times more profitable to practice a few correct bars well than to spend hours in superficial playing. What we require is not only a fleet and light technic but also a cultivated ear and a well balanced musical judgment. It is the union of technique and art which constitutes the difference between a pianist and a musician.

A severe nervous spasm, or a pronounced emotional disturbance, will of course impede progress for some time, and the remedy is often rest. But in many cases the difficulty is more apparent than real. The "weak finger" is often only an excuse and the real cause lies in faulty method or in lack of concentration. The teacher should be merciless about bad habits and insist upon a careful and methodic procedure. He should divide the work into small sections and require the pupil to repeat these sections in many ways—slower, louder, softer, with different accents—until the effort becomes natural and easy.

We shall now take up several exercises which will be useful in practice: scales, arpeggios, trills and staccato touches. Each of these has a function and a right manner of execution. Scales are the foundation of finger independence; arpeggios develop the hand's flexibility; trills and staccato touches cultivate the subtle articulations. But beware of the "mechanical" scale player who repeats routine forms without any musical or tonal purpose.

Disregard of details, careless pedaling, and neglect of phrasing are often the reason for disappointing results. The pupil must be taught to listen critically at every moment. He must train his ear to hear intonation, balance and the interplay between melody and accompaniment. It is useless to work at the fingers if the ear does not guide them. In short, make your practicing musical. Practice as if every repetition were a concert performance. Never allow yourself the habit of indifferent playing. Your first aim is to acquire a luminous tone, clear phrasing, and a steady rhythm. With these essentials, speed and facility will follow naturally.

May I conclude with a simple practical example. Take the case of a student who has to learn a certain passage of staccato and legato mixed. First play it exceedingly slowly, under control, with attention to each note's value and connection. Then increase the tempo in small degrees, once the execution is secure at each stage. If some measure gives trouble, separate it and practice it in contrary motion, hands separately, with accent on different beats, always with a calm and dominantly musical idea. When in doubt study the score, try to imagine the sound, and be patient. Time and patient work will yield the desired end.

We must not lose faith when results come slowly. The development of a musician is a patient and often painful process. But the final triumph brings an enjoyment which is worth all sacrifices.

Repeated Chords with Arpeggios
(To Be Used with Czerny, Opus 335, No. 40)

SOMETIMES CZERNY makes the mistake of introducing too many complications in an otherwise useful study. Such, I think, was the case here; so I have taken the liberty of cutting out eleven measures, reducing the technical difficulties to four points: 1. speed and endurance in right hand repeated triple chords; 2. brilliant left hand arpeggios; 3. double octave passages; 4. right hand repeated triplets with melody (Measures 12 to 17). Enough problems for one short study!

If your hands are small, or too easily, you may omit the low octave notes in right hand chords, Measures 1 to 8.

Ex. 1

Other hands may also practice it this way, but with these the regular

fingerings may be used, which will help to solidify the weaker side of the hand. For the repeated right hand chords with or without thumb, use as little forearm or wrist movement as possible (try not to "pump" at all), with fingers in constant key contact.

A good way to develop this technique, repetition "felt" is to practice the chords at first without inner tones; playing them as finger octaves; that is, with sharp articulation from the thumb and third finger knuckle joint. Try it and see how hard it is to get solidity and speed this way. Be sure not to move your wrist or arm. Terrific, isn't it? But it develops one's octave technique marvelously. Be careful, however, not to do it to excess.

Then add the right hand chords to the impulse groups. Be sure to rest each accent. Also work out Measures 12 to 17 in similar two-beat impulses—single and hands together.

Small hands may have to play the octave passage (Measures 9 to 10) with all 1-5; but note that Czerny wants it legato, which is impossible without using the fourth finger on black keys.

Practice the chromatic passage in "finger" octaves to eliminate lost motion. Don't "pump!" Also work a bit in broken octaves, rotating sharply toward the thumbs

It is perhaps unnecessary to mention that modulation may be effected from a major to a minor key, a major to a major key, a minor to a minor or a minor key to a major key. Thus we have the four interesting combinations.

To sum up the two important factors, Transition and Modulation, let us examine the following excerpt from "Sonata, Op. 2, No. 1," by Beethoven which is doubtless universally familiar:

The foregoing excerpt illustrates merely a transition borrowing the dominant key momentarily.

The following illustration, taken from the same composition, illustrates a modulation which employs a pivot chord:

[Musical example — small musical staves and notation]

Ex. 7

The German 6th chord of B-flat minor is the pivot chord used, by means of which the modulation takes place. In other words, A-flat major is moving into B-flat minor, a key of first relationship, and the German 6th — a subdominant quality chord of the new key — employed as a vehicle of modulation. The appearance of A-natural and G-flat affords the listener the clue to the fact that A-flat major is being definitely relinquished in favor of a key to which A-natural and G-flat belong. Naturally the ear perceives the foreign touch caused by the introduction of these two new accidentals.

The question now arises from the analyst as to what determines the feeling of transition and what proves the advent of modulation. The answer is that the length of time spent in the new key gives the proof as to which device is being employed. If the new key is barely touched upon or borrowed for only a few chords, immediately returning to the original key or plunging into another borrowed key, a transitional color takes place. If, however, the new tonality which is introduced persists long enough to make a lasting and definite impression upon the listener, there is no mistake as to its identity as a modulation.

One senses rather than reasons these two important elements of composition when casually listening to music, but everyone should be able to analyze the music that he plays from a reasoning standpoint as well as from a sensory one. In this way it will be discovered that all music, no matter how modern or how intricate in design, may be judiciously memorized.

The Fascinating Art of Practicing

To reiterate, we hear a great deal about the part played by the muscles and the mind in piano playing but very little about the marvelous human nerve system. Every note that strikes upon the keyboard is the result of a thought from the brain, communicated to the shoulders, arms, and fingers by the nerve system. Close inspection of many pupils while practicing reveals that in endeavoring to get a result, they arouse a kind of nervous excitement, which is akin to extreme tension, especially when striving to play a difficult passage. The stimulus which produces a good result often brings with it an accompanying tension that is injurious. In rapid and spirited passages the virtuoso pianist may seem to be under a nervous strain, but you may be assured that the performance is wholly controlled and not a series of spasms or nervous explosions. For this reason, if you are practicing, it is always a good idea to note whether there is an undue nervous tension at your shoulders or at your elbows. If there is, let your elbows float out laterally from the side of your body until the tension is eased and the hands seem to float. Then try the passage again, perhaps a little slower, and work up to the proper tempo without tension. If you do not do this, you may work for weeks and never advance.

I vividly recall that as a child of thirteen I had my first opportunity to hear the great pianist, Josef Hofmann. He played among other compositions Liszt's "Sonata in B minor." I was amazed. How much greater my wonderment when I saw the critique in the next day's paper. "What a great master is Hofmann in making child's play of this terribly difficult sonata of Liszt!" When, years later, I began to study this wonderful piece, I remembered the words of the critic and did not care to play the sonata in public before I had learned it. "This is really an easy piece!" This can be achieved only by methodical practice and ingenious practicing.

There are several ways to practice. We certainly would not practice a sonata of Beethoven in the same manner in which we would practice an exercise of broken chords. There must be a difference between the methods of practice of an etude by Clementi and a prelude and fugue of Bach. But there should be one thing in common. We should always practice with the same devotion and concentration that we would use if we were sitting in Town Hall and giving a recital to a selected audience of musicians. It might strike you as funny to imagine that you are playing a concert when you are really practicing scales with separate hands, but everything depends on the imagination. If you just try to feel that way, you will soon find out how exacting it is to play exercises and etudes as concert pieces and practice them as though there were nothing more beautiful than scales and arpeggios. Singing with the Old-Fashioned Three Register System

Dear Mr. Editor: I am twenty-three years old, and have no exposure factor with a range from B-flat, F-sharp to middle C. My problem is to know whether or not I can use the old method of registers or the newer system. My singing has improved moderately in the last six months. I have a very full mezzo-voice. I sometimes feel that I am not strong enough to sing modern roles which demand an even tone throughout the range. For instance, a number of my friends say that I do not have enough "head voice" to sing above middle C. Would you kindly advise me whether I should follow the old method of registers or not?

Dr. D.: The tendency to imagine three separate registers — chest, middle and head — is an old-fashioned view. The modern pedagogy emphasizes the even production of tone by means of proper breath control and laryngeal support so that what used to be called head register becomes simply the upper extension of the same voice. If you have a full mezzo and lack an easy high C, the trouble is usually technical rather than physiological. Practice exercises designed to lighten the laryngeal mechanism in the upper range and work under competent pedagogical direction. Do not become discouraged. Much can be accomplished in a year with steady, intelligent work.

Q. Is the October, 1903, remarkable article by M. B. [name partially illegible] true that [illegible] used to sing the part of Rigoletto? I have always thought that it was impossible for a tenor to sing the baritone roles. Would you explain?

A. Voices are determined by their natural tessitura and timbre. There have always been singers who attempted roles outside their normal classification, but such cases are exceptions and often damaging to the voice. Singers should choose repertoire which sits naturally in the voice.

A Hunger for Music

But Manager Miller was wrong. Duluth did want music. It wanted whether it was free or not. Members of the orchestra went to Minneapolis and called on Paul Lemay, principal viola player of the Minneapolis orchestra and assistant to Conductor Eugene Ormandy. They told him that they had a pretty good orchestra in Duluth and they wanted him to come and lead them in the first of a series of concerts.

Lemay agreed, but his first meeting with the fledgling orchestra must have been a disappointment. The assistant manager had failed to inform the manager to inform the janitor of the Shrine temple that a rehearsal had been scheduled, and when Lemay walked into the hall, he found his foster musicians fiddling away—still practicing in overcoats and hats.

It was a healthy job Lemay took on when he told the Duluthians that he would lead their orchestra. Morning—he rehearsed with the Minneapolis Orchestra. At noon he took a hurried lunch and caught the 12:00 for Duluth, a local that paused at every rural railroad shed on its fivehour journey through Minnesota's farming country. On the way up, he studied the music to be rehearsed that night. From six to eight, he held a class for viola players. At eight he entered the rehearsal hall and started the evening's work. Three hours of rehearsal, and he was back on the milk train, staying up half the night reading scores which he would have to conduct for Ormandy the morning. Life hadn't been so exciting for him since the days he flew over the western front as a member of the Royal Air Corps.

Lemay left the Minneapolis Orchestra, in 1934, to take over in Duluth. He already had sold the idea of a symphony orchestra to Duluthians, not only to the ladies of the afternoon musicale, and to the music teachers, but to the businessmen of the city. He had spoken before the Rotarians and the Kiwanis, before church societies and community clubs. He had conducted concerts on a cooperative basis to show Duluthians what could be done in a midwestern city where amateur and professional could meet on a common musical ground. He had an idea. It was to build from within. And he sold his idea as a salesman would go out and sell his wares.

"We don't want the biggest and best symphony orchestra in the country. We don't want imported concert masters. We don't want big budgets to pay for music. It will be much more fun to build out of what we already have here."

These words he preached week in and week out. He held classes for the amateurs. He went into the schools and invited junior and senior high school students to take up the bassoon, the oboe, French horn and the harp. For their efforts he promised them an eventual opportunity of playing in the orchestra.

On a Firmer Basis

Then he sold the city a design for music which called for a communitywide association for financing of rehearsals and concerts.

Businessmen, professional men, housewives and others appeal. A Symphony Association was formed, the first drive for funds was held, and when the committee counted up the returns, Duluthians had contributed five thousand dollars. For their money, the subscribers got no tickets, not even a chance at the choice seats.

"How do you do it?" managers of a dozen community symphony orchestras have asked Duluth. "How do you get people to subscribe to an orchestra fund without giving them tickets in return?"

"It is simple," answers Lemay. "Sell it to them as a business proposition. Tell them of the publicity it will give the city. Show them how the concerts will bring hundreds of visitors to the city. Then sell your programs like a department store sells its merchandise. Glamorize your product. Dare the businessman to attend a concert. Once he comes, you'll find he returns again and again. Our Duluth businessmen no longer are frightened by an announcement that Heifetz will play a Beethoven concerto or Flagstad will sing a program of arias from 'Der Ring des Nibelungen.' "

The plan of making its orchestra a neighborly affair has worked. To-day a tenth of the population subscribes to the orchestra's maintenance fund, with subscriptions ranging from fifty cents to one dollar. Housewives ring door bells. Businessmen tour the industrial areas. When the annual orchestra drive is on, community groups vie for the honor of topping their quotas.

The orchestra, now with eighty-eight members, operates on a budget of thirty thousand dollars a season, of which amount half is raised through subscription and the rest by box office receipts.

Each season it plays six evening concerts with noted soloists as guests, two programs for school children, a trio of "Pop" concerts; and, in addition, makes a tour of nearby communities. Last season the orchestra played a series of thirteen weekly concerts over the Mutual network.

To assure the orchestra of a continuous source of material, Lemay has established a junior symphony, and once a week he rehearses the teen-age musicians, many of whom he had dozen years ago needed his plea that they start studying the bassoon, oboe, viola and English horn. Every rehearsal is a course in music. Members of the senior orchestra attend to sit alongside the youngsters to help them in their work. The town was eager to hear youngsters on a recent Saturday afternoon when the sixteenth of them turned in a rousing premiere concert, and then broadcast their music over a nationwide chain of one hundred and thirty stations.

"Conducting an orchestra of tradesmen and professional men sometimes has its compensations," Lemay says. "Our soloist broke the heel of her shoe one night and there was shoemaker Angvik, our bass player, ready to fix it. When Heifetz was nearly asphyxiated by a gas heater in his dressing room, he had a doctor. No one ever stays away from a rehearsal because of a toothache. Dr. Will Benson is always on the job."

"But who," asked dentist Benson, "is there to take care of me? After I got through drumming out Ravel's Bolero I had to put up my 'Doctor Out' sign for two whole days."

Amount of Finger Pressure

J. A.—I.—Any good violin teacher can teach you how to tone your violin correctly. This is of the greatest importance to the young pupil. There is one practice to get good results, paying no attention to the desire to produce an enormous tone. At the very beginning do not attempt to force the tone; you will have to judge this by the ear and by experience. Your teacher can tell by the way you hold the bow as to the proper pressure. You should place the thumb so that it comes in contact with the bow and then judge by the sound. The right balance of the bow will be found by using moderate pressure, and you will be told to use more pressure as you grow older. Try to be sure of your teacher's advice. You should not attempt to make 'large' tone at first but to get a round tone by being economical with the pressure and bow.

"The greatest value of music,
though, is the richness it brings
to ordinary, everyday living. Whenever
I hear people complain of
restlessness or a lack of something
to do, I recommend the music hobby,
not because it happens to be my own
hobby, but because, in its very nature,
it seems best calculated to bring
release and refreshment to many
varied temperaments. Music, after
all, is so infinitely adaptable to the
being molded to every definite need.
Does that sound contradictory? Actually,
it is not. Music does not deal
with facts and statements; it mirrors
emotions, and mirrors them more
clearly than do any other art. The
words that build a book or a play
may be found in a dictionary, with
the same meaning or other; the
impact of a certain tone or a statue is
presented by forms and objects that
everyone recognizes (unless, of course,
the stance is such that the subject is
not appreciated before the surrealistes!).
But music deals with nothing more
definite than feeling. No one can say
for a certainty exactly what thoughts
were in Beethoven's mind when he
sat down at the piano and wrote the
Symphony, or those notes mean some-
thing different, yet something
nearly real and personal to everyone
who hears them. And that, precisely,
explains the worth of music. Music
is valuable to that the special personal
voice that is so vital to spiritual re-
freshment. One needs a knowledge of
English words and instruments and
structures to appreciate Shakespeare; but
the trained ear among people who listen to Beethoven can
draw spirit from refined human thought,
knowing something more definite than
that he loves to listen! However, one
must be careful not to ascribe thoughts
to Beethoven that cannot be proven
to have been his own. Nothing is more
annoying than to find some self-
appointed critic of the music, the
singer called critic of the values of
an aria who was not the general
really 'meant.' How can we say?
Certainly, there are a large number
of works whose very titles indicate
that the intention of the composer. But,
for the most part, music concerns
itself with that peculiarly personal
emotion—which each listener can
interpret to suit his own need. That
is why the spiritual charm of music
is nearer-reaching than that of any
other art.

"Music teaching has made remark-
able progress since my own study
days. For example, my daughter has
just been required to spend an entire

year working at Bach and nothing
else. That was unheard-of in my girl-
hood—yet it is the best possible study
that could be provided for the forma-
tive, impressionable years. The
serenity, the simplicity and the beau-
tiful orderliness that emanate from
Bach are invaluable not only for
further musical study, but for the
whole enslavement of life. My
daughter was frankly a bit staggered
when first she was assigned a full
year of Bach study; but now that it
is behind her, she goes back to Bach
of her own accord before beginning
work at the opera, in which field
she seems to make her career. I hope
she does, although the opera is not
my favorite form of music. The
very elements which make it 'glam-
orous' seem to draw attention away
from its more purely musical values. Per-
haps I feel this because, as a dramatic
actress, I find the dramatics
I know the reason for this, of course:
I know the singing actor must think
first of his vocal projection, of atti-
tudes and gestures which will not
hamper it. One of the important fac-
tors in the art of "knowing" does
not alter the fact that operatic acting
is standardized. Only the
exceptional operatic actor—only a
Flagstad, for example—has such con-
trol over the several important com-
ponent parts of her art that the audience

"My own vocal lessons progressed
as far as the coaching of seven full
operatic roles, of the lyric soprano
repertoire, among them Marguerite,
Juliette, Chimène. I am proud to re-
port that I was very good at my roles,
but I never mastered up sufficient
courage to sing in public! Apart
from the sheer pleasure of singing,
I derived great benefit from my vocal
lessons. I learned to breathe corre-
ctly, to support breath, to husband
its emission through long phrases, to
'place' my voice, to focus tone. That,
of course, is invaluable in my stage
work. I am able to manage the
'worked' species without difficulty.
Vocal study is also excellent for em-
phasizing enunciation values. I count
the small changes to be some of the
greatest in years in the mastery of
vocal art.

"But I do not like to think of music
solely in terms of practical ad-
vantages; it can provide an approach
into deeper branches of artistic ac-
tivity. Its ethical value lies solely in
itself—in the glimpses of truth and
beauty it affords us, in its power to
transform, to refine, to uplift. Music
is, after all, a kind of miracle, a
healer rather than a teacher, but much less

K. S. W. This teacher was not young.
He had been trained in Germany by
some of the best masters and had
also studied in France with one of
the greatest composers. As a young
man he had taught but had given up
teaching to become an energetic con-
ductor. He established himself in an
extensive studio, issued an elaborate
circular in which three pages of fine
type were required to tell of his tri-
umphs. Then he sat down to wait for
the pupils—who, alas, did not come.
Ten months later, his funds depleted,
he came to us for advice. One look
at his circular made clear the reason
for his failure. His thought was fo-
cused upon himself and his glorious
past, rather than upon what he was
going to give his pupils. A new cir-
cular was prepared. It was adjusted
to the needs of his probable pupils.
He gave a few recitals and the "as in"
his studio—with some of his famous
pupils. Colleagues were persuaded to
appear. Soon the pupils commenced
to "trick on in" and before a year he
became a very valuable artistic mem-
ber of the community.

Moral: Forget yourself and work
for your pupils.

Note that, in the cases we have
presented, nothing has been said
about the pedagogical or musical
skill of the teacher. That was taken
for granted. We have discussed sim-
ple common sense business matters.
It has not been possible to help all
who have come to us. Some have been
in such obvious ill-health, or states
of mental depression, that success
was unthinkable. Others have been
inadequately prepared professionally,
so that there could not possibly be
compensation. The Etude's position
upon these matters is well known.
For half a century it has stood for
the highest standards, but it has
never been without its own obstacles.
The only way to secure these
standards was by attending cele-
brated institutions, or by passing
"first" examinations.

The great teacher is first of all a
genius. His greatest college is vast
experience in trying out the problems
in his own soul and brain. We know
one teacher who has been through
the musical courses at three of the
most famous music schools of the world.
His pupils do not compare with those
of another teacher who was largely
self-taught. Some of the best vocal
teachers we have ever known were
doing a year where devoted part of his
time to a prosperous banking business.

Make to-day the resolution that
will lead you to success in your
tomorrow.

Orient yourself to the community
in which you are teaching. The
private teacher is denied the coopera-
tion of a Board of Directors and
the association of other faculty mem-
bers. He or she must "build" alone
and sometimes it is not easy.

The private teacher in the smaller
town will find a need for training
more advanced pupils for playing
as church service in a credible
manner, as there seems to be a
scarcity of pianists or organists who
can play a simple church service.
Consequently it is most difficult to
find substitutes.

For the inexperienced player it may
be of assistance to get from one of the
local churches the list of an experienced
organist who will permit the beginner
to practice the hymn playing and
playing in the choir loft.

Younger children should be en-
couraged to play occasionally in Sun-
day School. This is excellent training
and for the older children suitable
pieces for the Processional, Offertory
and Recessional should be included.
If some simple duets can be incorpo-
rated in the program so much the
better. The more pupils are trained
to play, the more musical activity there
will be in the community and the
teacher will find more work. Recess-
ional and Processional will no doubt be
glad to advertise this.

Positions for community singing are
constantly in demand. It is well that
the private teacher have some small
groups and a class in harmony if pos-
sible. The school is your "survival"
system. The best results come from
establishing classes for sight reading,
song leading and the like. Remem-
ber, too, that most of the church work
calls for modest repertoire, such as

Perhaps the first appearance of

in Paris in the year 1844, in the pro-

Vincent D'Indy in his "Legende

saxophone in his "Freval" for

through the present, Thomas, Kastner,
Villa Lobos, Walton, Copland, Pro-

Dallapiccola, Hofobach and many
others have called for one or more
saxophones in their orchestral works
or operas. Solo literature for the in-
strument is of a very modest large, but
is growing steadily. D'Indy wrote a
"Choral Varie" with orchestra, Florent
Schmitt a "Legende," and Claude
Debussy a "Rhapsodie." The Debussy
solo has a rather remarkable history,
and the story of its composition goes
back to the turn of the century when
Mrs. Paul Hall was honorary Presi-
dent of the Boston Orchestra Club.
She played the saxophone for the
sake of her health and was naturally
eager to have solo pieces for women
at various functions.

Mrs. Hall, therefore, commissioned
Debussy among others, to write some
thing for her instrument with orchestral
accompaniment. Debussy at-
tended her performance of D'Indy's
"Choral Varie," and his reaction was
very unfavorable. Presumably he had
not before heard the saxophone
played by a really outstanding artist
in the instrument. He did not like it,
and he thought it ridiculous to see
a lady in a pink frock playing such a
"luxurious instrument"; he was not
at all anxious that his work should
provide a similar spectacle. He never
finished the "Rhapsodie," but many
years later sent only a pencil sketch
to Mrs. Hall, which she could not
perform. From this sketch, however,
Roger Ducasse wrote a score in 1919.
At this point the year 1893 that the
"Rhapsodie" was given its first per-
formance in its original form—that
is, on the saxophone—when the writer
played it with the Boston
Symphony Orchestra.

During the last decade European
composers of almost every nation
have contributed to the solo repert-
oire of the saxophone. Concertos
have been written by Glazunoff
(Russia); Dressel and Bonet (Ger-
many); Ibert and Vellones (France);
Bosan (Italy); Coates and Demuth
(England); Tarp and Benison (Den-
mark); Larson (Sweden); Pfleuster
(Poland); and Martin-Jeanmain
(Switzerland). Chamber music has
been written for it with piano and
other instruments by Swind of Eng-
land; Hyman of Holland; Knorr,
Jacob, Brehme, Burnek of Ger-
many; Hindemith of the United
States; Oster of Yugoslavia; Reiner
of Czechoslovakia; Pierre of France,
and Paz of Argentina. We can in-
clude sonatas, concertos, quintets,
and other works by such American
composers as Cranston, Brant, Clem
Haddon, McKaye, and others. The
"Quartour de Paris" have played tran-
scriptions of Haydn and Beethoven
quartets as well as original com-,
positions, and their performances were of
high interest. It is said that the
Brown brothers achieved remarkable
results in tone quality and were
successful in combining saxophones
of different pitches.

The list of works for saxophone is
by no means small any longer. Most
of the compositions require a range
exceeding the traditional two and
one-half octaves, but Henry Brant
has asked for four full octaves on
saxophone in his concerto, which can
be played by three artists on the in-
strument. The repertoire for this in-
strument is constantly being en-
larged, and harmonies are unlimited.
The saxophone calls for as great a
study and as close an application as
any other instrument. The saxophon-
ist who wants to master the instru-
ment must train fingers, tongue, lips,
jaw muscles, lungs, and diaphragm
fully in accordance with the require-
ments of the instrument. But the must
mentally go beyond these mechanical
perfections in making the playing of
the instrument a matter of musical
beauty. He needs the ability of inner
tone-imagination to a colorful, vivid
degree. Coupled with the convincing
power that characterizes the artist
who performs on any of the accepted
instruments must be a broad under-
standing, and respect for the instru-
ment. The performer needs high as-
piration, a desire for truly beautiful
expression, to avoid the tincture of
vulgarity and clownishness which
seems to have become the lot of the
saxophone as an instrument. The
saxophone is at a truly admirable in-
strument in the hands of a cultured
musician who approaches its per-
formance with the attitude as well
as skill which will give it a place in
our contemporary musical culture.
Without that attitude, the saxophone
must fight many more decades for
recognition.

The Piano Accordion

Advice on Various Problems
By Pietro Deiro

As Told to Elvera Collins

ONE OF THE REASONS why we
enjoy writing for this Depart-
ment is because it enables us
to keep in touch with accordionists in
all the highways and by-ways of the
country. Nothing can surpass the
pleasure we feel when we receive a
letter, thanking us for some bit of
advice which has been helpful, or
asking us to solve some problem
which is proving a stumbling block.
A few of these recent letters touch
subjects which we believe will be of
universal interest to accordionists.

A teacher asks our advice concern-
ing a girl pupil, ten years of age, who
has a twelve bass accordion. The
child has advanced so rapidly that
the limited music, possible on the
small instrument, is retarding her
progress, yet she is not large enough
nor strong enough to play a full-sized
heavy instrument.

We believe that one of the modern
light bass instruments would be
quite satisfactory. Its range would be
light enough in weight and small
enough in dimensions for this little
girl. These instruments are now
streamlined so that all unnecessary
weight has been cut down, and yet
the quality and volume of tone com-
pare favorably with the larger models.
The range of both the piano keyboard
and bass section makes it possible to
play the same music as that used for
a full-sized instrument. The bass sec-
tion is arranged so that all the prin-
cipal chords may be played, such as
major, minor, dominant seventh and
diminished. No row of buttons has
been provided for the latter chord but
a special combination of buttons
makes this chord possible.

Solving the Difficulty

We suggest that the teacher or par-
ents of the child consult with their
local music stores or write to various
accordion manufacturers and secure
illustrated catalogs with price lists
for comparative values. Even if it is
necessary to have a special instru-
ment made, we would recommend this
rather than have the child lose sev-
eral years of valuable practice time
if she keeps the twelve bass instru-
ment, or injure her health if she
tries to play a heavy full-sized in-
strument. True enough, there are
many children of this age who have
no difficulty handling the large in-
struments, but we must remember
that there is a great variance in
physical development of this age. The
little girl in question has already

shown sufficient talent to warrant in-
vestment in one of the new models.
The twelve bass instrument may be
traded in as part payment on a new
one or sold direct to some one else.

Another interesting letter comes
from a young man who has become
so discouraged that he wonders if he
had not better forget all about accor-
dion playing and turn his attention
to something else. This letter carries
a certain pathos, for the accordionist
in question has been a fine student
and has already mastered an extre-
mely wide repertorie of accordion music.
The discouragement comes from the
fact that, during the past year, the
tone and volume of his instrument
have seemed to deteriorate. He has
become so discouraged that he no
longer feels that he will be of any use
to the profession. How he finds that it
is impossible for him to do so, and he
is a slave to the printed notes. You
see, he has built his mental musical
home without a complete foundation,
and now it is not balanced and, to-
tally, as would any building where the
support under one of the corners had
been omitted.

Belated Memorizing

He further states that he has al-
ready tried all the generally recom-
mended systems of memorizing but
which much has been written, but all
have failed. In other words, the cer-
tain section of the brain which has
to do with memorizing has never been
used and naturally is so rusty that it
cannot be brought into action. A
problem of this kind should certainly
open the eyes of many of our ac-
cordionists who keep postponing at-
tention to memorizing.

Well, as long as all the popular,
recommended systems of memorizing
have failed, we can only recommend
the following which many sound like
a penalty for past neglect. It may or
may not bring results immediately
worth trying when such a serious de-

hanging in the balance. We suggest
that this young man discontinue all
other forms of accordion practice and
give his undivided attention and con-

times when it pays to have a one
track mind. With the exception of a

What the Great Masters Thought of the Mandolin and Guitar
By George C. Krick

IT IS NOT THE PURPOSE of this discussion to present a detailed account of the lives of some of the immortal giants in the history of music, but to mention only their association with the mandolin and the guitar. Players of these instruments should feel proud of the fact that many of the master composers showed sufficient interest in the mandolin and guitar to devote time to them and to compose original music for them.

Ludwig van Beethoven needs no introduction to the musical public. When between twenty and thirty years of age, Beethoven met Wenzel Krumpholz, one of the first violinists of the Court Opera in Vienna, who had become well known as a mandolin virtuoso. The two men met often and had many musical discussions. Beethoven often wrote for the violin and the mandolin while associating with Krumpholz. The result of this acquaintance is shown in the Sonata for mandolin and piano and an Adagio for the same instruments which Beethoven composed and dedicated to the Countess. The original autograph copy of the Sonata can be found in the British Museum in London, and that of the Adagio is in the Royal Library in Berlin. These compositions show clearly that Beethoven was well aware of the characteristics and artistic possibilities of the mandolin and that he had a thorough knowledge of the fingerboard of the instrument and the mechanism of the plectrum.

In 1796, the master visited Prague and there was introduced to Count Clam Gallas whose wife was an amateur musician, being quite a skillful performer on the mandolin. This lady was a pupil of Krumpholz. The director of the Italian Opera in Prague and also a fine mandolinist, Durante his period, Beethoven wrote a number of pieces for mandolin and piano and dedicated them to the Countess. Most of these were preserved in manuscript. Beethoven himself was the possessor of a mandolin, and a photograph of this instrument suspended by a ribbon on the wall near his last grand piano was published some years ago in Bonn, his native city.

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, the immortal genius, evidently became fascinated with the mandolin while traveling through Italy when about fourteen years of age. It was in 1780, when living in Salzburg, that he composed the song, Come Dearest Mandolin, and somewhat later the song, Contention, both of these with mandolin accompaniment. On October 29, 1787, was the first performance of his opera, "Don Giovanni," and on that evening Mozart wrote for the mandolin the accompaniment to the famous serenade "Deh Vieni alla Finestra." That performance led Italian mandolinists to take note of his music and to arrange it for their instrument.

Hector Berlioz, one of the most remarkable musicians of the nineteenth century, was an admirer of the guitar. In his early days in Paris he was greatly interested in the guitar and used the instrument in small concert settings. Berlioz wrote enthusiastically of the possibilities of the guitar and its sympathetic tone for accompanying songs and illustrative music.

On Self-Study

A lady has written to ask advice about attempting to study without a teacher. Although in general, we are firm believers in personal instruction under a capable teacher whenever it is possible, the lady in personal home responsibilities at present which make it impossible for her to begin a definite lesson schedule with an instructor. It is a case of either self-instruction or no instruction at all. She has had some training on the piano.

What she really wants to know is whether any one can advance when studying alone, and whether all the things written in favor of self-instruction methods and correspondence courses are facts, or merely assertions, or whether much is true. We can truthfully say that these methods are valuable and are of real service.

We consider it part of our duty to keep informed on all new accordion publications and peruse practically everything as soon as it is published. We are, therefore, particularly happy that in our opinion the majority of accordion literature which is being put on the market today is of good quality. Yes, we admit that very few of these publications are really superb, but nearly all are valuable works which might be used. The trouble with many is that they are poorly edited, and the musical taste of the editors is at fault, however, lies with them and not with the material. If one purchases a correspondence course or method and merely looks through it and picks out a few tunes which happen to appeal to him, he certainly will never learn to play. The idea may be compared to a sick person who calls a physician, has the prescriptions given him filled at the drug store, and yet never takes the medicine. Can the physician be blamed if the patient does not improve?

Merely hitting the high spots of a method for self-instruction or a correspondence course is not enough. If results are to be obtained, the student must go about his learning systematically. A daily practice period should be adhered to and combined with a weekly review. Every line of every piece in a course should be studied. The author must have known that instruction was needed, or space would not be devoted to it. All instruction should be followed to the smallest detail. The accordion and music should be kept in a convenient place, where daily fifteen minute intervals which otherwise might be wasted, may be used to advantage.

Home study students are inclined to postpone practice and find excuses. This should be avoided, and we suggest a small ledger for an accurate accounting of all practice hours during the week. Each successive week should show an improvement on the record of the previous week.

We recommend the investment in a good playing machine and a library of records of the best accordion artists. Much can be learned by concentrated listening to these records. We further recommend occasional check-up lessons with capable teachers, and that a part of each summer vacation be devoted to a short special summer course at any of the large number of schools in the country. At all of these schools the regular staff of teachers are well trained and serious students will obtain useful and accurate instruction.

Music in Washington’s Day
By Paul Fouquet

Bobby and his Uncle John had been discussing George Washington, whose birthday was only a few days away. Bobby’s inquisitive mind always turned to music, and Uncle John was the one to answer questions of a musical nature. So Bobby asked, “Uncle John, will you please tell me something about music in America during George Washington’s time?”

Uncle John laughed. “Bobby, you always ask questions that take quite a while to answer. But I’ll be as brief as I can.”

“When the early settlers arrived in America, musical instruments were rather scarce among them. The tiny boats, which they sailed in, were often so crowded it was necessary for the people to leave behind some of their most cherished possessions, and these, of course, included musical instruments.

“As you know from history, the Puritans were very strict and looked upon music as something that would distract the people from their work and make them dance and play; so were forbidden.”

“But,” said Bobby, “the Puritans were only in New England, Uncle John.”

“That’s true, Bobby. In other parts of the colonies there were no such strict rulings, and the people made good use of music to help them relax after their days of arduous labor. Their music was confined to dancing and singing, and their musical instruments were simple and rude. Many people used the flute and the fiddle; the drum and the fife were also common. There were people who could sing as well as play and many of these people were the backbone of local music-making.”

A writer once said “great things are done by a number of small things done well.” That is a delightful thought because it makes us feel that we are doing great things, and the feeling that we can do something is the surest way to do it.

THE COVER FOR THIS MONTH—The cover for this month is an interesting portrait of Kirsten Flagstad in the role of Gluck's Orfeo, in which she scored a great success with the Metropolitan Opera Company. It will be noted that the lyre used in the characterization of Orfeo is made true to the traditional lyre, the sounding box of which was made of a tortoise shell, the open side of which is worked with parchment, the supports for the strings cross-bar being the horns of an animal.

This famous contralto was born in Sweden, and after making her debut with the Stockholm Royal Opera and being a member of the company for some years, appeared in opera houses in Prague, Berlin, Vienna, Buenos Aires, and Covent Garden before coming to the United States in the latter part of 1936.
