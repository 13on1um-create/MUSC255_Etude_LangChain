SOME WHOLESOME TRUTHS FOR TEACHERS.

First, a teacher is overworked; this is the general condition; either overworked in the profession or out of it, as will be seen. Secondly, the teacher is pursuing the benefit of personal improvement, exhausting himself up in handling that class, and teaching the children, hard to pay expenses.

First and foremost one should aim to be a best pianist, not simply a brilliant technician, and certainly not a mere entertainer, but one grounded and well cultivated in all schools of piano playing, and with a rich comprehension of playing of moderate and advanced musical realization. The singer, for example, must be an able interpreter. It is impossible to say that either element should take precedence, for all should be developed together.

The importance of thorough technical skill, flexibility of fingers and strength of muscles cannot be understated, but these journeys are better understood and better thought of, primarily, for their workings, techniques, and habits, and no other considerations must accompany them into the studies. 

One should rather compile, in connection with the technical development, methods of teaching. One must surely understand the various exercises and schools, but one should keep all the particular development of techniques to a minimum.

“COUNTERPOINT AND CANON.”
BY H. N. ATTEN.

In the preparation of this work, the author has kept constantly in mind the following important conditions: 

1. SIMPLICITY.
It is well known that many of our most valuable works on musical theory are rendered almost useless to the average student, being practically incomprehensible, by reason of their long and involved sentences, ambiguous terms which confuse, and sometimes entire periods that express nothing whatever. The simplest principles are sometimes enunciated in the most elaborate sentences, as if it were necessary for the student to depend on his learning one English paragraph in order to learn (with his teacher’s help) some simple fact. In some of the books on the rudiments of harmony, there are simple and clear enough, while the very order and method of the principles and theories makes it tedious to the student’s efforts at constructive work. This is true of several works on counterpoint. 

2. THE ELIMINATION OF IRRELEVANT MATTERS. 
A careful analysis of the fundamental principles of counterpoint has also been presented with the view of emphasizing those things which are most important, thus bringing lucidity to the systematic teaching.

Distinctions are made between the established rules for good counterpoint in a contrapuntal style, and that of modern composition, as daily ruled by Bach, Handel and Mozart. He claims that these rules must be thoroughly mastered before the student will be prepared to initiate, successfully, the innovations of the more modern composers. The crying evil of the day in the musical world is the mistaken notion that a new theory of composition may be deduced from the works of Wagner and his contemporaries, and the world of imitation students who can only glean from the position such rules as they can gather from those of the famous and truly great school masters. 

But there is no more theory of composition. The old field of counterpoint has lost some of the force, and there are no new rules. Great and noble masters have ever followed old rules, but they were substituted for new ones. But no one has really disregarded these rules. Only those who are most familiar with the details of each rule have known how great importance should be attached to their foundation by studying Handel and Bach as their model. 

Mrs. HAYDEN EXAMINES THE WORK. 
Every teacher knows that pupils will ordinarily work with more enthusiasm and interest when they have definite tasks to perform than when their work is vague. Therefore, the work presents to each student a distinct aim, in which he is enabled to do his own work intelligently. It clearly thinks of all the student’s work for him, and places him in the standing position necessary for practice. The teacher should say the kind of practice the student may take.
