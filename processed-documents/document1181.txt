AS WE BEGAN this editorial
a performance of Ralph
Vaughan-Williams' impos-
ing and engrossing London Sym-
phony coincidentally came through
"on the air" in our study. Let us
imagine a man who never had
been to London and was un-
familiar with the tremendous
history of the English metropolis. Is there anything in this lovely
musical work which would make pictures in his mind which
would resemble that wonderful world center? The melodic bits
employed here and there, the jolly folk dance, the warp and woof
of the material are English only in the imagination of one who
has become familiar with these elements.

Music is still as abstract as it always has been. It is only by
association that we attribute to it the power of creating pictures
in our minds. However, one does not need to have had an exhaus-
tive experience in music to realize that the art has a great effect up-
on our moods. Listen! There came
on the radio just now the majes-
tic clash of a cymbal, which can
only mean the pomp and circum-
stance of an unforgettable Brit-
ish historical event thus portrayed
in Williams' score. Our hypo-
thetical ignoramus may never
have heard of Napoleon, but when
he hears the bells in Tchaikov-
sky's "1812" Overture he knows
that they must be connected with
some powerful moment in his-
tory. Thus, in a general way, by
association, music molds our
moods.

Even without direct associa-
tion, music may have a powerful
effect upon all of us. For years,
your Editor has always endeavored
to write these editorials when it
was possible to hear, at the same
time, a fine musical program "on
the air." He has invariably found
that writing became more facile
during a soft musical background; thoughts seemed to flow easier,
and the editorials written under such conditions apparently in-
spire a larger response from the reading public. Why? That is a
problem for the psychologists.

At the 1946 convention of the Music Teachers National Associa-
tion, Dr. Walter H. Rubsamener of the University of California,
read an impressive paper, "Descriptive Music for Stage and
Screen," indicating an extensive and brilliant survey of the basic
principles by which music becomes illustrative. The examples he
adduced and which may be purloined from Dr. Rubsamener's ob-
servations, readers of THE ETUDE often must have wondered, while
attending the moving pictures, what processes the composers
employed in providing the great variety of scores. Many of the
scores have been so masterly that we often have deplored the fact
that they are so temporary. They deserve a much juster fate than
that.

Some of the finest musical talents in the world have devoted
part of their time to motion picture music. It is interesting to in-
quire whether they are making a permanent contribution to the
art by their work in this exciting field. Composers have
written symphonies, oratorios, and operas which are far more a part
of the present day repertory than when they were first heard over
a century ago. What is to become of the vast genius and labor that has been put into a
great motion picture? The good picture is the closest, let us say, to
the novel; the best and most lasting are canned and stored in
the vaults in Hollywood. Will they be opened for future genera-
tions? Will the scores be turned into concert arrangements,
to be performed by living musicians?

Or will they be forgotten, and
disappear like fallen rose petals?

Much pictorial music depends
upon line and volume. That is,
the melodic line goes up or down, as
the fortunes of the players ascend
or descend. The heroine trips up
stairs and the small boy slides
down the bannister. What could be
more suggestive than the ascend-
ing major scale and the descend-
ing chromatic scale? What are
scales anyhow? Nothing but musi-
cal stairs. Dr. Rubsamener writes:
"The visual movement most
frequently reflected in music is
that of an ascending or descend-
ing line. One of the cliches of
cinema music, for example, is the
use of a fast arpeggio in a de-
scending scale, staccato, to ac-
company someone running up or
down stairs. The rise or drop in
pitch is obviously a transfer from
the visual field, while the staccato
imitates the short noises made by
hurrying feet on the steps. The
particular transfer, which musi-
cally illustrates many often ways
of going up or down, can
usually be traced back to medieval processions who were finding rising and fall-
ing melodic lines for the words ascendit and descendit of the
Mass. The visual impression of a storm at sea, for instance, can
be effectively reinforced by a melodic line that gradually rises
and falls to accompany the surge of the waves."

In the film "Rebecca," Franz Waxman depicts the collapse of a burning
house by means of crashes and downward glissandi in the entire
orchestra. An audience will accept this transfer of a downward
movement to a descending musical line, even though the
noises made by a collapsing structure certainly do not descend in
pitch. On the other hand, a rising and falling movement is some-
times interpreted musically by a gradual increase and decrease
in dynamics. Thus, in Waxman's music for the film "Suspicion",
a series of alternating crescendo and decrescendo accompany waves
that rise and break against the base of a cliff.

the irreproachable music of Brahms because it was "pure," "ab-
solute," or "abstract." Brahms must have felt that he was not pic-

They Hail From the Red River Valley

The Amphion Chorus of Northern Minnesota and Dakota Reveals Success Formula for Male Choruses in the Average American City

by Grace V. Watkins

Cities small and large all over America are discovering the social and publicity value of a crack-jack male chorus, made up of prosperous men citizens. There probably is no quick means of presenting the civic standing, ideals, and quality that excel the concert of such a group, when it takes time to make tours to other parts.

How can a "top notch" choral group be developed in the average American city? How can such a group be drilled, without waste of precious time to the members, so that it will command the enthusiastic praise of qualified critics? What are the secrets of success for the everyday citizens with high choral ambitions?

The Amphion Chorus, of Fargo in the Red River Valley, North Dakota, and Moorhead, Minnesota, was, up on the borderline of Canada, knows these answers to these and similar questions. These vigorous "Men of the West on Wings of Song" have unparalleled claims to distinction. In fact, they believe Amphion unique in the field of American music to-day through its triple role of (1) being a top flight professional chorus, (2) sponsoring an annual artists course in the community and (3) financing its own organization of one hundred members without one dollar of aid from patrons or other benefactors. Not only has the group spent $70,000 on community betterments in the past eleven years, but the Amphion budget is balanced at the present writing, and there is a surplus in the treasury. Probably no other chorus has attained comparable distinction without the backing of some institution.

When the Amphion Chorus appeared in concert at Town Hall, Slug-hall? in the eastern ? of the state? the first remark one hears about them is: "They are the best little chorus in the world." Mere words will not tell the story, nor does the fact that their repertoire is extensive and of the highest quality of tone, diction, and precision ever tell all that the keenest critic could desire. "Then it was that this group of men shunned singers from Fargo, North Dakota, and Moorhead, Minnesota, were that artistically speaking, Amphion had arrived."

A Variety of Vocations

Although it is strictly a professional group, Amphion counts not one professional singer in its ranks. From the factory one member, from the farm and offices, from the school-room, and from the bank. This is a cross-section, a nurse, a teacher, a operator? librarian? doctor, insurance man, creamery worker, hotel man, merchants, a printer, a grain commission? Twenty-five businesses, professions, arts, and crafts are represented in the membership.

Requisites for admission are a love of music, a reasonably good voice and some experience in choir or chorus work; the desire to sing, to cooperate and share. Thus in the Red River Valley of the North, formed the world over for the production of No. 1 Hard Wheat, these men have been drawn to a period of fifteen years developed into a powerful force in the cultural life of the Middle West. Under the able administration of Dr. E. Preston, director, they have inspired the organization of many other male choruses.

The Amphion was chosen from the mythological story of the lyre player Amphion, whose music caused the stones to move into place of their own accord when fortifications were being built in thebes. The repertory of Amphion is drawn from every reputable source. It will be found works ranging from the stately dignity of Palestrina to the satirizing madness of modern arrangements of old Russian folk songs. It has required years of careful study and diligent work to perfect the organiz? and the bases which Robert A. Simon said "compare favorably with the best of the Russian massed"—the lyric contours of the high tenor, the impeccable ensemble work of all the choirs.

Some Notable Activities

The annual Amphion Artists' Course is top news for music lovers throughout the Red River Valley. In cooperation with Moorhead State Teachers College and Concordia College, the Chorus has in the past eleven years brought forty-five concerts and thirteen producers to the community. It is only in recent years that musicians have begun to prepare themselves exclusively for a career in conducting. In former years, the conductor usually grew to the position after he had played in an orchestra, or had been a vocal coach. I believe that the young musician should know what he wants to do, and build for it from the beginning. My own career was built in that way. I began with the idea of conducting, and did not vacillate. I became familiar with all of the instruments in the orchestra, and studied the cello, the clarinet, and the French horn, in addition to the piano. In general, the conductor must have: (1) a broad working knowledge of the piano; (2) the ability to sight read orchestral scores, and to score work, and (3) to have a working knowledge of one woodwind instrument, one brass instrument, and understand the percussion instruments.

It is not possible to become a sound orchestral conductor, or to convey the correct interpretation of a score, or to demand certain phrasing from musicians unless one has a knowledge of the instruments in the orchestra. The piano is the fundamental instrument for the conductor. It is the most comprehensive, and has the finest limitations. Without a knowledge of this instrument, the conductor has set a tremendous handicap for himself. Being able to play orchestral scores at the piano is part of the conductor's background. He will not become a patient listener to music unless he has acquired a firm piano technique, and a superior knowledge of its dynamics.

The conductor also must have a cultural background. Good taste is his stock in trade. He should have a well rounded education with special emphasis on the humanities. He should know Greek and Latin, and should have a knowledge of the modern languages, German, Italian, and French. It would be difficult for a conductor to study the classic songs, literature, or opera scores unless he knew foreign languages. He could not expect to have an intimate knowledge of folk songs and folk-singing unless he had a thorough knowledge of the language of the country where he plans to carry on his work. As to conducting, there are opportunities all over the world.

Breaking Into Radio Conducting

It is difficult to say just how the young conductor can break into radio, and begin to conduct, because so much depends on his background, and his versatility. There is no school for the conductor that will turn out the real musician. To conduct any kind of an orchestra, a vocal ensemble, where one can have orchestra practice, radio-wise, the conductor must have experience. Opera conducting is the finest training ground for a conductor. I know because my own career was built in that way. The opera conductor learns to face the stage full of problems. Here one must be on time in order to make the dramatic impression. A conductor who has the opera background will not be frightened at sudden occurrences in radio. He will be able to keep his head under pressure.

In symphonic conducting, the young leader is apt to deal with better musicians, but be not likely to get as much operatic experience, in a brief period of time. To become a radio conductor one must have an extensive knowledge of operatic, symphonic music, concert music, operetta, and swing music.

I would advise the young aspirant to finish his formal schooling, and his musical education, and then try to become connected with an opera company. A young conductor sometimes is invited to be temporary conductor of the morning rehearsal of a radio station. I should say that many of the better trained people employed in radio have gone as concert conductors, and some have come from the opera. I believe that it is the best training to have opera, symphonic and choral experience; and there still is more opportunity for the conductor to get a job in radio since the repertoire in radio is so wide. Many younger conductors are fortunate in getting a job in radio if they have the technique and the stamina. They must be able to stand the pressure and take the tremendous hours of work.

Technique of the Radio Conductor

"March 26, 1918: The great offensive is on at last, and we are breathless with excitement. The thunder men who arrived last night on leave were called back to the line against day, poor fellows! It looks serious. ... For two days now Paris has been bombarded by a gun, 75 miles away. During fifteen minutes intervals from dawn to dusk the Chermans have been terrorizing the inhabitants of the city. . . ."

When those lines were written in my World War I diary there was no time to record that Claude Debussy had died that day in nearby Paris. Three days later, when his body was borne through the city to be buried in Pere la Chaise, the entry read:

The frightful battle is still raging. The Germans have gained some ground, but the Allies are holding like bulldogs. . . . Debussy was buried today. What a tragic moment for the world to lose such a man!

With the Germans hammering at the gates of Paris, this was scarcely the moment to ponder on the passing of one shy, sensitive spirit who had long been ill and exhausted, and whose creative output, according to the critics of his day, had slumped in quantity and slumped in quality.

Debussy's Rating Today

But now, almost thirty years later, let us look at the score. Who are the most beloved French composers? Bizet, Gounod, Ravel, Debussy, with the timid Claude Achille gaining each year. He has not retained the same popularity, he has lapsed ahead. For a long time the Clair de lune has been selling to the tune of thousands of copies per week in this country alone. Many of his other compositions are not far behind in popularity. Almost everybody knows the Arabesques, the Minstrels, the Sundermered (Schneider?), Children, the Mad Woman in the Attic, the Reveries and the Afternoon of a Faun. Debussy's high popularity ranges from the music-loving public to the sentimentalist of the concert platform. The music of Debussy is loved for its lovely color, its exquisite sound, its fragility that was so often mistaken for being slight, for being merely "impressionistic." No composer since Chopin has so developed tone color as an integral element of his keyboard writing. He was the poet of pianistic tone as Chopin was the poet of cantabile.

Debussy's Last Period

And what of those last painful years of Debussy's life, the period during which the critics pontificated that his creative capacity had deteriorated? We know now that these were years of exploring in fruitful fields and of experimentation along new paths, with results that are by no means meagre.

To be sure, the "Twelve Etudes for Piano," and the "Blanche et Noir Suite" for two pianos of his last period are not understood by the public, or in fact by most pianists. But give them time! . . . The next years will see these highly original masterpieces fully explored and appreciated, especially now in the face of the prevailing sterility of the pianistic produce ordered as it is in this generation. These "Twelve Etudes" and the other pieces of the "White and Black Suite" are astonishing examples of whimsical, almost technical fecundity. Their strength lies in their sparse, economical, suggestive counterpoint and content. There is hardly a note of padding in the pieces which surprise and delight at every turn. They are quite unlike the piano compositions of Debussy's earlier period; and as for "modern idiom" one of them, the out-Schoenberg's Schoenberg as a pioneer seems in the so-called twelve tone scale. (See The Ruler Pour Les Notes Repetes.)

While you are taking a summer off to get acquainted with his later Debussy works, study also the charming set of short "Eastern Impressions," composed in 1915. These were written for piano, duet, and sometimes for two pianos. Their titles match the super-sensitive music: To Evoke Pan, God of the Summer Winds; For a Remembered Dance; The Nine Children's Murmur; For a Voix; To Think Back on the Morning, and so forth.

Neglected and Brilliant Pieces

Pianists neglect some of Debussy's most rewarding music. At least half a dozen of the second book of "Preludes" are unaccountably passed by; the longer delightful ballet written (about 1910) for piano, La Boite a Joujoux--The Romance of the Toy Chest--is almost unknown. And what has happened to the exquisite "Petite Suite" of Debussy's first period? Its four short movements written originally for piano duets (like the "Papillons," all in four hands) sound enchantingly so in the two-piano arrangement. Many present-day programs in Paris would be considered as perfunctory indeed if they contained a single movement of the "Petite Suite."

Few students ever learn the glorious "Gollonie" (?), Debussy's most brilliant piano piece, which makes a great final number for any piano program. Among the charming items are the Toccata in the French manner, and the broader, the most popular, for a brilliant piece for one hand, the "Tule" and the "A Mirage from the 'Pour le Piano Suite.'"

Bubbles

May I pick a couple of popular Debussy bubbles? "Puff" goes the "Impressiontistic" bubble. This item applied to Debussy is vague and misleading. Debussy was a master of light form and spare content. Like Mozart's music for children, clear in concision, concentrate essence. Debussy is an about or approximate about themes. Debussy's music is cleanly cut; he creates a Mozartean clarity and delicateness, which can be produced only by a perfectly controlled technique.

The one-half, one-quarter, or long sustained damper pedal, which he requires, serve to bring into clearer focus the dazzling superstructural design which he builds over the living, throbbing foundation. Witness for example his magical use everywhere of consecutive seventh and ninth chord "bottoms" for the sharp projection of the higher overtone textures.

Proof! goes also the "whole tone scale" label. Comparatively seldom in his ripe periods does Debussy employ long wholes tones in pentatonic succession, since he knew, perhaps better than we, the poverty of such tonalities. He so-called exotic effects are more often produced by composing whole pieces in the old Greek modes. He uses these modes lavishly, especially in his later periods. A good example is the "Toccata" ("Pour le Piano Suite") composed in the Aeolian or sixth scale-step mode.

Debussy's Directions

If you would become a good Debussy interpreter follow the precise directions which he gives in all his pieces. When you can give a convincing reason for momentarily disregarding the composer's directions (for some disputed spot--as I shall try to do later in the Clair de lune)--then you may momentarily stray. Otherwise, I advise the most accurate and literate reading of his explicit markings.

The Clair de lune

If you will think of the Clair de lune as a striking search of tone which reaches the apex of its curve in the measure 41, from there descending gradually to the soft dark shimmer of the final Measures, 66-72, you will, understand why the piece is in an almost perfect example of formal design. How Businesslike Are You?

by Ruth Teeple Reid

[portrait photograph of Ruth Teeple Reid with caption RUTH TEEPLE REID]

MANY TEACHERS contend that the business side of their profession should never be mentioned, as it interferes with art. It does—but definitely—not, however, for most of the reasons given by the majority of teachers.

Music teaching has been a haphazard business ever since the first music teacher hung out a shingle. Pupils and their parents, usually the mothers, request lessons, and you, as the teacher, go home to the studio, and the ragged little pupils come. The mother waits in the parlor, and sometimes becomes very chatty, asks questions, 'How much do you charge?' and so on. If you are a little worried about your efficiency, you are likely to apologize for business; you will talk about "art" and have many little excuses. But the problem still remains—the teacher is expected to have business sense as well as musical sense.

Some teachers, who charge large fees, have very little business sense. They fail to keep correct records, they cannot plan lessons so that pupils progress, and they neglect to collect fees. Other teachers, who charge less, have splendid business methods, prepare and send out blanks, call for payment, and have the studio in good order. Pupils come steadily, are punctual, and make progress. The moral is clear: businesslike teachers have less worry and more success.

My own mistake in teaching was that I left the business end to chance. I used to tell parents that the boy or girl might have one lesson free, or that I would "squeeze them in." I could never refuse a "good" pupil and so I kept doing things for many whose payments were irregular. One day I realized that I had to be businesslike, or I must retire.

A New Method is Devised

Music Teachers! Has that happened to you too? Of course it has. In taking stock of my pupils and their parents, I found them to be, reasonable people. The fathers were executive-administrators, professional men, and top-flight executives, with substantial incomes—men who run their businesses and professions with clock-like regularity. I took my problem to the fathers of three pupils. They suggested one way of dealing with the money question which to my happy surprise, and to theirs, gave the best results. They asked me to see that the money was paid. The mothers, in many cases, agreed to have the teacher call. For some mothers it was a great relief. Some would pay more readily and punctually if the teacher took the responsibility.

You Don't "Decide" to Be a Singer!

A Conference with

Thomas L. Thomas

Distinguished Baritone

SECURED EXPRESSLY FOR THE ETUDE BY STEPHEN WEST

[Photograph: Portrait of THOMAS L. THOMAS — a head-and-shoulders black-and-white photo of a young man in a dark suit and tie, looking slightly to his left.]

"THE PROFESSIONAL singer is often asked a
question which it is not easy to answer. Aspiring
students want to know exactly how a successful
career may be built. There is but one answer: learn
the vocal craft and then put it into active use! It is
deceiving, a satisfying reply, however; many young people
seem to feel that there are special and mysterious
"secrets" which, once mastered, can unlock the door of
successful singing. "How do you breathe?" they ask,
"what do you do with your chest, your diaphragm, your
throat, your back?" And at that point, I am properly
stumped—possibly because I have my own theories
about singing. I believe that singing must be entirely
natural, entirely simplicity—infinitely unhampered by me-
chanical restrictions of "method" or "school."

The Importance of Musicianship

"How do I breathe? Truly, I haven't the least idea. I
breathe naturally. The purpose of breathing is, not
to sing, but to keep alive; if a natural breath—the
kind that you draw as a baby—is good enough to keep
you alive, it's good enough to let you sing. It seems to
me that the chief function of the good vocal teacher
is simply to keep you doing natural things in a natural
way. Of course, if there are muscular difficulties, there
must be overcome—but such difficulties are not natural.
Where the vocal tract is normal and sound, the only
problems that can possibly arise, in the entirety of nat-
ural act of singing, are due to acquired bad habits. And,
alas, we have many of them! What the good teacher
must do, then, is to guide his students so that bad
habits do not arise. After that, the widest thing is to
leave nature alone. Either you do breathe naturally,
or you don't. If you do, keep on doing so and forget
about it—don't experiment and doubt and examine your-
self! If you do not breathe naturally, your teacher will
help you overcome whatever habit is holding you
back. And once this habit has been overcome, for-
get about it!

"The technical aspects of voice production have,
comparatively speaking, but little to do with the all-
around picture of a vocal career! Naturally, a singer's
musical life is a fine singing voice and the correct use
of breath, his voice is only one of the requisites. Natu-
rate voice nor method can be mastered by studying a book
of rules. Any good trained voice may be preserved, the
problem is of channeling them into a career still remains
to be settled!

"My own experience inclines me to tackle the ques-
tion from a very different angle. Having had the ad-
vantage of a childhood in Wales, I believe that the
first required for the serious musician is, quite simply,
musicianship. Where I grew up, music was in my home,
as a popular diversion, quite as baseball is in the air
here. How does the average American boy of six or

seven 'learn' to throw a ball—to understand the differ-
ence between a catcher and an outfielder? Well, he
doesn't learn it—he absorbs it as part of his very air
in the practices. That's the way music ranks in Wales, and
it is a splendid start! The Welsh people have a natural
feeling for vocal music. The smallest town has at least
one or two soloists, and everyone is part of it. It well
remembers seeing the men of our town come home from
a day's hard work in the mines, eat their supper, rejoin
their members up, and then set out to walk five or
six miles to rehearse. At evening, they would polish
up the "Elijah" or the "Messiah," working at phrasing,
articulation, sending out naturally pure tones—every-
thing themselves! Then they'd walk back home, around
one or two in the morning, and be up and ready for the next
day's work in the pits at six. The whole family learned
the oratorios—the most minute details of every phrase,
—and when the great day of the performance arrived,
and one heard those glorious works sung by a massed
chorus of 1,000 (or one's own townspeople, one felt that
he had a proper share in it all.

Singers are Born

"Now, the mere study of rules out of a text-book
doesn't give that approach. It surprises me, sometimes,
to hear a fine lad say that he learned Offenbach arias
and put such applause when he sang it, at a party,
that he has now decided to become a singer. You don't
decide to become a singer—either you are born a singer,
or you are not. The one who is born a singer will have
a voice and a sense of judging when he is using it
correctly. He will know by the feeling of ease and
freedom that permeates his entire being when he sings,
His teacher will show him what to do, of course, but
no teacher can do it for him—neither can an artist or

stand beside him all his life, making signs as to what
is and what he is not; people do not listen
to guide him, but urge him in his own sensations. And
that requires intelligence. The born singer doesn't
get discouraged easily—he can't—he simply has got
to sing.

Value of Practical Experience

"Intelligence and persistence, then, count for far
more in the long run than rules out of the book. Rec-
ently, I listened to a young chap with a phenomenal
voice. He sang a sort of Joan of Arc? I recognized its
melody—but the words? I asked him in which language
he had been singing, and he answered, 'in German!'
Now, I started out by saying that this fellow had a fine
voice. Actually, though, I am not too confident about
his success—because he lacked the intelligence to per-
fect his language work before setting out on auditions.
Evidently, he hasn't realized the importance of language
teacher, is going to take the same ten minutes to teach him
the great to learn. And if he doesn't realize that at the
beginning, his own manner is going to ruin his whole
career. To become a singer necessitates taste, sound feel-
ing, a knowledge of languages and a fine musical
craftsmanship. Many of the great singers of the past are
now, perhaps, less than they might have been. There
are many marketable voices now that lack the back-
ground of interpretative knowledge. on the other hand,
there are great artists.

"Whenever I begin to analyze, sing, or indetify with
a singing problem, I find that the great mass of pupils
are temperamentally not fitted to be a singer. They
get away with a 'hit-or-miss' method. The only success
that they have is from constant hard work. If a singer
is not a 'born' one, he will be condemned to a life of
struggle. And often he must learn to be a teacher or
a coach in order to make his living. You can make
yourself into a musician, but you cannot make your-
self into a winner if you aren't born with the essential
inclinations and the tonal quality. This is not to be
discouraging; rather it is a sober truth. The happy
thing is that the majority of people who become suc-
cessful singers are deeply musical and have fine asso-
ciations and family background. They sing because
they love it and because it is part of their nature."

Of a Famous Music School

Some of the Principles Indicating Why the Paris Conservatoire Has Been Historically Successful

by Maurice Dumesnil
Member of the Juries and Examination Committees for Many Years

TOWARD the end of the eighteenth century a period of great musical activity manifested itself in Paris. The turmoil of the French Revolution had had a widespread effect on the Arts; the poets wrote madman verses and Odes to Liberty while the musicians, their inspiration kindled by patriotic enthusiasm, exalted the new order in compositions often combining sincerity with grandiloquence and bombast. Previously in 1784 King Louis XVI had signed a decree establishing a music school in which Chosselot acted as director, and Piccini taught voice. Out of this embryonic organization the Conservatoire was born in 1795, when Bernard Sarrette took over the directorship and launched a new and more ambitious program.

Sarrette was well fitted for this position, as he combined the mechanical skill of an excellent band leader with the efficiency of a forceful promoter. Under his guidance the scope of the school was broadened until the curriculum included more or less what it does nowadays: piano, voice, all branches of string and woodwind instruments, organ, theory, harmony, counterpoint, composition, chamber music, orchestra, and dramatic arts. Those early years were very fertile, and on the staff one saw the names of Grétry, Méhul, and Gossec among them. Cherubini took the helm in 1822; a still greater period began. Soon the institution grew considerably, both artistically and materially. Apart from his musical eminence, Cherubini's firmness of character and somewhat disciplinarian make-up imposed respect. At his death in 1842 Auber succeeded him. The author of "Fra Diavolo," "La Muette de Portici," "Le Domino noir" and other operatic favorites of the past was more brisk and Fatherly, a man of fixed habits for whom precision and order always came first. His period was one of tranquility and prosperity—for the school, and he occupied his post for almost thirty years until death took him in 1871 during the Franco-Prussian war, at the ripe old age of ninety. Then Ambroise Thomas was called, and the famous author of "Mignon" continued the splendid works of his predecessors. He, too, served for a long period of twenty-five years, until he passed away in 1896. Théodore Dubois occupied the chair until his resignation in 1905. And next in the line came the exquisite and distinguished composer, Gabriel Fauré, whose activities extended until 1920 when a growing decadence obliged him to cede the directorship to Henri Rabaud. There are ones that during the long term of one hundred and forty years only seven men assumed that directorship! This is an amazing fact, eloquent in itself, and, at once it comes to mind when the question is asked: what is it that makes the Conservatoire de Paris such a long and rankling institution, and probably the first all-around music school in the world?

The answer can be summed up in three words: tradition, formality, and discipline.

THERE are more and more requests for information regarding the use of the organ with other instruments. There are questions as to repertoire, and there are questions as to the effectiveness of other instruments with the organ. Also questions are asked regarding the proper technique in registration so that the particular instruments will go well together. In future articles we shall discuss the various combinations. In this article we shall deal with the organ and piano, which perhaps is the most widely used combination and one for which the most information is requested. Later, I shall write on the organ and violin, organ and harp, organ and 'cello, organ and trumpet, and organ and ensembles of strings and woodwinds.

To say that there is a wealth of material written for the piano and organ is to make a decided understatement. There is little written that is good, little that can be even made effective. Some of it is so bad that it is ludicrous. Some of the things, however, that have been written, for example, by Clifford Demarest are effective; and here and there we find arrangements which are effective. However, our aim, of necessity, is to speak, as is found in the things that we can make available and to analyze ourselves. In making these arrangements we can be constructive by listing, first, the use of the organ as the solo instrument, using the piano as the accompaniment; second, the use of the piano as the solo instrument with the organ as accompaniment.

The Organ as the Solo Instrument

We can begin, of course, with the Concerti by George Frideric Handel of which a number of editions are available. They are arranged, for example, in the Biretalepol and Hareet editions so that one organ and the piano or ensemble part are printed on the same page. They are most effective with a small chamber orchestra, but they can be done very well with the use of the piano only. The organ parts are not easily done but are worth working on and preparing well. The possibilities for ensemble work even with the piano give opportunity for some thoughtful preparation. The most useful ones to begin with are Numbers Two, Five, and Ten. Number Five in F major gives the organ an excellent opportunity. The possibilities for inventive registration are endless. The flute passages are so well written that one can use a part of the flute organ and still have it heard as though much more organ were being used. The lilting passages can be done on the brightest quality of tone with all sorts of off pitch stops and light mixtures. The slow movement, one of the most lovely passages ever written, can be played in the mannerکهت kind of combinations with real effect. There are also opportunities for some exquisite solo stops in this slow movement. It is possible to use all of these (concerning) with telling effect; I simply take this one for example. The parts where the piano plays are not virtuosic types though, and any good pianist can enjoy playing them with real pleasure. There are opportunities for producing beautiful tone and for the weaving of the tone from the piano to the organ. With careful thought out attention to detail, the organist can take his tonal build-up simply melt into the piano.

The concerto by Pietro Yon for the organ are certainly worth serious consideration. Here again are pieces which were originally written for two or three orchestral parts. The pianist must consider his responsibility and be ready to support the organ. There is a great deal of balance to be observed, especially for the softer organ registration and for the lighter piano tone. They demand very careful attention in both rehearsal and performance.

Other numbers which may be considered in this category are numbers by Handel, Vivaldi, and others, including Mozart's pieces arranged for the organ and piano. They are very effective and have been used in many recitals. The arrangement often demands that the pianist keep a clear rhythmic support while the organ carries the main thematic material. One must be careful to watch tempos and breathe as if one were an orchestral player.

We often find easy adaptations of orchestral works for the organ with piano. It is seldom necessary to read every bit of an orchestral score; often an intelligent reduction will suffice and, when carefully chosen, can make a good recital feature. The organist will need to exercise restraint in registration; a tone too big will destroy the delicate balance.

The Schumann and Brahms numbers are often done effectively with the organ taking the orchestral lines and the piano carrying many of the solo instrumental parts. For example, the slow movement of the Mendelssohn Concerto is wonderfully adapted to organ and piano. There must be very careful rehearsal in order to obtain a blend and a sense of orchestral unity in the performance.

Some of the late French composers have written very successfully for the combination of organ and piano, and these works are effective if carefully prepared. The pairing of the two instruments requires a true ensemble spirit, and both players must listen and blend.

There are other numbers which may be considered, for example, the grander operatic numbers, where the organ can supply the choruses and the piano the recitatives or the more intimate solo parts. These combinations can be very dramatic if handled with taste.

Developments in chamber music have made the use of organ more frequent in smaller ensembles. The organ's sustaining power and unique tone color add a special character. The use of the piano as an accompanying instrument gives the necessary definition and rhythmic support.

It is obvious that the repertoire is wide and the undertaking of arranging requires discrimination. Not all works are successful; the choice must be made with thought and with an eye to the instruments' capabilities.

The organist should not attempt to cover the entire orchestral spectrum as the organ is unique and should be used for its own qualities. The piano may be treated as an ensemble instrument which can adapt to many roles.

Registration is, of course, at the very heart of the matter. The registration must never be ostentatious; it must always hold the balance of the piano and organ. The organistic effect must complement the piano and vice versa. The organ should always be considered as an orchestral partner to the piano in these combinations.

There are many schools of thought on this subject. Some organists like the more romantic, orchestral registration, others prefer cleaner, more defined registrations which make for clearer ensemble playing with the piano.

One key is communication. The organist and pianist must talk, discuss registration, tempos, dynamics, and phrasing. The rehearsal is where the most important adjustments are made. The players must be careful and patient.

As to the technique of combining the pedal work with the piano part, it must be practiced slowly and with meticulous attention to detail. The precision in entrances and cut-offs is necessary in order to maintain rhythmic accuracy.

A final point: in education of organists, instruction in ensemble playing should be included. The organist must learn to play with other instruments and with the piano; this will broaden his musicianship and his ability to serve in various musical contexts.

The first step in attacking the problem must of course be the indirect means of providing oneself with a basic knowledge of the instrumental technique involved in playing the clarinet. A basic study must be made of the problems of the tone, technique, and tongue, for which suggestions the writer again refers to the previous articles in this column. In general, beginning students must be taught to maintain a tone of even quality and constant pitch. With a good embouchure and normal breath-control this should not be difficult. Matters such as the selection of a good reed, the instrument, and the use of a good reed, etc., are obvious, and adequate information can be obtained on these matters from many sources. The development of moderate technical proficiency can be secured by the use of such excellent material for study which is readily available and again we do not feel that this is a place for a discussion of the matter.

However, after adequate technique, tone, and general playing ability have been developed, the problem of intonation will begin to be more and more troublesome. The student will find that intonation is rapidly becoming his greatest problem, his most severely criticised weakness and his saddest of musical experiences. It is to the student in that position and to those who are trying to help him that the remainder of this article is devoted.

Remembering first of all that pitch is wholly relative we must proceed to make a comparison of any pitch desired with any pitch produced. We must be able to distinguish the smallest possible variation between what we play and what we desire to play. By close listening we can develop our sensitivity of pitch to a degree which can be characterized by the word "feeling." One can "feel" that he is in or out of consonance even when an undue "beat" is in evidence; as for example when in a large ensemble the mass of sound destroys the individual hearing of an instrument but manifests a pitch recognition factor.

Control of Breath Pressure

We must also be able to alter the breath pressure by either increasing or diminishing it under all possible circumstances. We must be able to increase the speed of the breath in its passage through the instrument without increasing its pressure and vice versa. And we must be able to increase or diminish the pressure of the embouchure by infinitesimal degrees. The two variables involved, breath and embouchure are compensators and must be understood fully. An increase of breath pressure will cause a slight flattening of the pitch. A decrease of breath pressure will cause a sharping. An increase of embouchure intensity will cause a sharpening of pitch and a decrease of intensity will cause a flattening. It may readily be seen from this that a combination of these factors will cause a compensating action which must always be guided by the desire to maintain a constant tone quality.

The second type of alternate fingerings employ a different tone combination and may also effect the mechanism. Music for Wind Ensembles
The Extent and Value of the Literature
by Richard Franko Goldman

Richard Franko Goldman has been Associate Conductor of the Goldman Band since 1927, and has devoted much attention to various phases of band music, pioneering on behalf of both old and new works and exerting considerable influence on the enlargement of the band repertory. He is considered an authority on early wind-instrument music and on early American composed music, and has conducted many premieres of original band works. He is a member of the National Institute of Arts and Letters and President of the New York Composers' Club. He has been the conductor of the New York Festival Orchestra and of the Columbia University Orchestra. He was for many years conductor of the 7th Regiment Band, and is the author of numerous articles on band music. He has made many arrangements and transcriptions and his work in this respect has been extensive. Among his publications are "The March in American Life," "The Wind Band and Its Future" and "Band Music of the European Masters." He has served as editor of many volumes of band music and is at present serving on a biography of Niccolo Paganini who published in 1948. Mr. Goldman is a graduate of Columbia University and of the Juilliard School. He is now (1947) active as conductor and teacher and as Chairman of the Department of Music Education at Columbia.

(Editor's Note.)

IT IS IN THE FIELD of ensemble music that the wind instrument player can find his best opportunity for developing musicianship and acquiring knowledge of style. It is a lamentable fact, however, that the wealth of original and essentially varied literature in this field remains comparatively untouched. One cannot, of course, claim that this original literature even remotely approximates that which is available to string players. Nevertheless, its can form the basis of a repertory of greater musical interest and worth than that now in common use. Its values should be clearly evident, since they stand in striking contrast to the lack of educational or musical value of most of the material on which wind instrument players are now trained. Perhaps the most important value is that of the knowledge of style, an element in musicianship which is not always given its proper importance.

Most proficient players of stringed instruments, are, either through inclination or teaching, at some degree of acquaintance with the great heritage of original music which is theirs. Through the use of this heritage, opportunities are offered them for musical development along with the acquisition of technique. Study is at least to an extent based on a literature which has its effect upon individual appreciation. Piano students have the same advantage, being as a rule taught on their progress by way of Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin, and the other great composers for the keyboard. Perhaps even more important, the intelligent student of keyboard or stringed instruments is led to realize that vital treasures of music, written for his instrument, are there for him to explore.

Original Works Are Valuable

He learns that this is not only of solo literature, but also applies to the vast literature of chamber music and to larger works of original composition, and the study of such works forms the content of considerably more serious development. Among the several classes of original works for wind instruments we of course have the works of the early masters, those of the 17th and 18th centuries, and more recently the works of the 19th century and up to the present day. But in addition there are also contemporary composers who have written of high worth for winds.

Oboe and clarinet, and even horn and bassoon, can boast of a certain amount of original musical material, but this appears to be less widely performed. The brass, of course, have unfortunately little solo literature of musical value. To my knowledge, for example, the only instrumental solo written for trombone by an acknowledged master is the Recitative and Prayer from Hector Berlioz' "Grand" Symphony for Band. And this seems to prove my point, since this splendid solo has until the present remained almost unknown!

Solo material of this sort should certainly be used more widely, as should the ensemble material which forms its complement. The original literature for wind ensembles, both woodwind and brass, is considerably richer than most performers or teachers realize. There of course are great deals of transcribed material for solo wind instruments and for ensembles, forming today the greater part of the repertory in active use. They are of course quite well adapted to the interests and purposes of younger players and some of the transcribed material is genuinely miniature, since it is carefully and intelligently arranged for the individual instruments and for the conductor. But there are still possibilities for original works which are too little utilized.

It is in the field of ensemble music that the wind instrument player can find his best opportunity for developing musicianship and acquiring knowledge of style. It is a lamentable fact, however, that the wealth of original and essentially varied literature in this field remains comparatively untouched. One cannot, of course, claim that this original literature even remotely approximates that which is available to string players. Nevertheless, it can form the basis of a repertory of greater musical interest and worth than that now in common use. Its values should be clearly evident, since they stand in striking contrast to the lack of educational or musical value of most of the material on which wind instrument players are now trained. Perhaps the most important value is that of the knowledge of style, an element in musicianship which is not always given its proper importance.

Some of the transcribed material is musically acceptable, since it is carefully and intelligently arranged, but the great bulk of it is mechanically written and often performed without any regard to idiomatic quality. It is of course natural that transcriptions of orchestral works for wind ensemble shall be used, but to make these the central repertory is to miss the point. The preservation and the improvement of the wind ensemble as a distinct artistic medium depends upon a steady cultivation of original literature for winds. Such a literature exists and is growing; it is our business to seek it out and use it.

Now let us begin with our four fundamental bowings. The first one, the slow gliding stroke, is very well exemplified in the opening measures of the Bach Air or Gigue; the stroke should be long, smooth and steady; the weight distributed evenly and the bow traveling with a steady hand from frog to point and return. The second form, in contrast, is the quick, little strokes used for light passage-work such as trills and small scale passages; these require a freedom of the fingers with scarcely any pressure. The third kind is the short, accented stroke, produced by a decided firmness of the wrist, while the last one is the separated stroke which the Germans call "martelé," produced by a sharp accent to each note.

It is essential to learn to control the bow so that each of these strokes may be produced at will. The beginner should practise them slowly, measuring every movement of the arm and wrist, until the motions become natural and automatic.

Sometimes pupils make the mistake of relying entirely upon the fingers to move the bow. This yields a frail tone and narrow color. This rule of thumb method of transposition is equally applicable to all voices or parts with the result that the complete harmony of a piece may be transposed quite as simply as can the melody. One stage in the long development of keyboard instruments culminating in the modern piano was the Virginal.

The Virginal was a small rectangular spinet, without legs. It was held on the lap, or placed on a table. It was popular in Europe as a household instrument during the 16th and 17th Centuries. The Virginal passed out of the picture when it could not keep pace with the improvements in musical quality, playing ease and beauty of appearance of the Harpsichord and the Clavichord ... just as those instruments in turn were displaced by the Piano. Working Toward Tone Perfection

"When this preliminary groundwork has been carefully covered we test the boys for their natural registers of range, and assign parts. Then we do the range, exercises in parts (alto and soprano), with an interval of a third between. This has the dual advantage of familiarizing the boys with part singing, and of changing the exercises into interesting music rather than mere drills. The registers for the anthem may be used to usually extend from F below middle C to C above for second alto, and A below middle C to the second F above for first alto. Second sopranos usually sing from middle C to a little above the staff, and first sopranos from first line F to high B. There are, of course, exceptions in both directions.

"These exercises have been devised in order to protect and perfect the greatest beauty of the boys' choir—its tone. There seem to be two general 'schools' or theories of tone. The Austrian school tends toward great brilliancy—doubles because of the very narrowness of the German language. This is exciting to hear for a while, but ultimately becomes too brilliant. On the other hand, there is the English school, which—perhaps as a counter against over-brilliancy—becomes dull and can all-too-often degenerate into hoarseness. We believe that the best tone lies between the two, sharing the advantages of each, but avoiding the vices of the pitfalls. For most purposes, we seek a tone which is sustained toward the roof of the mouth about halfway between the soft-palate and the teeth, thus gaining brilliance from the nasal cavities and lower from the lips.

"The drills we have outlined will help produce this desired placement. There are, of course, further aids to securing and maintaining it. To help the boys understand, for instance, that tones originate not in the throat, but in the diaphragm, we tell them that a tone is like a bubble that rests on the breath, floats up into the head, and needs room to get out. Too much air forced into the toned bubble will explode or decay and so will constrictions.

"After these unison and set pieces, we begin sight-singing on the places symbols. Immediately after, we reread on too, lah, loo, and LEE before finally going to the words, which have in most cases been memorized previously. In working up repertory, we learn about thirty-six songs a year, so as to have three full programs ready. We use old liturgical music; d. Lasso, Haydn, Schubert, Mozart, Mendelssohn, old English madrigals, and some contemporary American works.

Selection of the Choir Boys

"The Apollo Choir consists of twenty-three boys, between the ages of nine and fourteen, all of them selected for special musical aptitude and for general intelligence. We try not to foster any professional attitude. Soemt of them will develop into fine singers, but their goal at present is to sing for the love of it, and to make the world better by their singing. For six weeks each year we go on extended concert tours and during the summers spend another six weeks in camp. The school season is devoted to regular academic studies, with the additional delved of correlating artistic awareness. For example, we take our boys to great art galleries to teach them the expression of rhythm through line and form; we read much literature to them calling their attention to the rhythmic flow even of prose; we insist that they attend fine concerts and endeavor to keep them ever alert to the beauties of nature.

"Once a year in an effort to secure the finest talent available, we send letters to 1000 supervisors of public schools as well as over the county announcing scholarship auditions for boarding students and asking them to choose from their best boy singers those who might wish to become members of the Apollo Choir. Then we go personally to some twelve or fifteen key cities to hear them. The best are invited to enter the Apollo Choir School at 6957 Lakeshore Drive in Dallas for further training. As soon as they have acquired the desired vocal habits and memorized the current repertoire, they become members of the concert choir and are entitled to the privileges pertaining thereto. The activities this season include participation in forty-two concerts in thirty-eight states, composed by several engagements with major symphony orchestras, three nationalwide broadcasts, and joint appearances with such eminent artists as Helen Traubel and Frances Yeend."

In addition there were several concerts open to the public during the year. Such activities are a wonderful drilling process for all concerned, as it prepares them for the great event toward which all efforts are directed: the final public contest for the awards. Objections have sometimes been raised against the practice of these contests. One argues that numerous [contests?] can cause participants to lose five or ten minutes the fruit of an entire year of hard work, and that has been suggested that a summing-up of the grades obtained at the examinations and exercises might be a fairer way to determine their real merits. But this has never gone through, because after all the Conservatoire is primarily a school of music, of operatic artists, and the degree of self-control which they demonstrate at the contests is one of the capital factors on which the decisions of the judges are based. American listeners who attend the "Concours du Conservatoire" are often puzzled by the atmosphere which prevails there. The juror's manner is packed with musicians, critics; from time to time every fifteen minutes or so a contestant is introduced, sometimes so scared that he looks like a convict going to his doom. The worst experience is the reading at sight of a manuscript written especially for the occasion by a prominent composer. When the whole list has played and read the jury retires for deliberation. At this stage the excitement of the audience reaches its peak. And loud chatter on the courtyard and [illegible] interest engages, representing the "voice of the people." If the decisions of the jury coincide with it, well and good; otherwise something of a riot is likely to develop among the audience, not always prevented by the [illegible]. A letter from Paris informs me that the 1946 violin contest brought another instance of these regrettable occurrences. However, they are the exception and more often than not the judges and the public pick out the same favorites; this in the contest closes harmoniously in a thunder of applause, with loud cheers, and fond embraces from exuberant relatives.

Let us examine now a few important points connected with the tuition itself: there are no individual lessons, but classes of twelve pupils during which each one receives instruction and at the same time benefits from the advice given to all the others. Originally, boys and girls were separated; but later on the co-ed system was adopted and it produced remarkable results because of additional emulation. As to the duration of the studies, it varies from one year to a maximum of five years. If by that time the student has failed to conquer the first prize, he must leave the school with only a minor award; and the same applies should two consecutive years pass without his taking any award at all.

Most notable is the complete freedom left to the teacher as to the choice and use of the method which they find convenient to adopt. In this there is absolutely no interference and the authorities never meddle. But they take note of the results and offer criticism when it is needed. The Conservatoire has always found this regime of freedom most profitable artistically because it allows a large share of initiative to each professor and creates a wholesome competitive spirit between the different classes. But here more, tradition reigns supreme and there is a general acceptance of certain recognized principles. The dictum of Hans von Bülow: "These things are necessary in order to play the piano: 1. When a student has been assigned to a class, he remains there as long as he is in the school, which is an excellent thing since it gives the teacher an opportunity to study his pupil in detail and at length, much in the same manner as a family doctor would do. Then the right method can be applied, one that will fit perfectly each individual case.

One never hears, at the Conservatoire, statements or questions such as these: "If I am going to 'take' from so-and-so ... or from someone ... (most very likely)." Then I will change schools and take from so-and-so ... (three weeks possibly)"; and so on. Or: "What's the use wasting so much time on those silly old-fashioned exercises?" That simply would not be tolerated. A serious conservatism is in order; and it includes the study of phrasing, tone production, dynamics, and last but not least: style. "What is style?" one may ask. Style is the right approach to the composers through an assimilation of their personalities, their distinctive characteristics, and the period in which they lived. Thus Conservatoire students do not play a Beethoven Sonata like a Chopin Ballad, a Bach Toccata like the Schumann Papillons, and Debussy's [illegible] in Chaconne as it was Liszt's Liebestraum. Chamber music training, too, is compulsory with regular meetings at which sonatas, trios, quartets, and quintets are studied and carefully rehearsed under the supervision of an expert. There are classes for orchestra and vocal ensemble as well, and all in all it combines to develop a high degree of musicianship among the participants. All these activities, however, are based on purely artistic grounds and taken as such, exclusive of "credit hours" which do not exist in the curriculum. Admission to the Conservatoire is through a contest, and the average number of piano applicants reaches about three hundred every year for no more than six or seven vacancies. This also gives an idea of the high level of such contests.

Fortunes in Movie Music

The editorial for this month, "Music and Morns," refers to The Warner H. Rosenbaum motion picture: "Sunrise" and the conspicuous effect it had upon the reputation of music in movies. Such films as "Sunrise," "The Hunchback of Notre Dame," "The Phantom of the Opera," "The Crowded Hour" and many others have contributed to making motion-picture music an important and valuable field. There is an increasing demand for trained composers, conductors and musicians who understand the needs of the film industry. The establishment of studios with orchestras and from the symphonic organizations has been a notable development.

TO THE EDITOR:
Your questions are not too difficult to answer, although I must hasten to remind you that I am expressing my personal views only. As to some of the statements in the editorial: It is true that the Script and the late Motion Picture producer have been more and more inclined to use good music in their pictures. The results of this change are seen in the increased demands upon composers who are able to write both for the films and for the concert hall. Many of the better picture writers have been drawn from the ranks of the concert musicians and from the serious men of the theatre.
