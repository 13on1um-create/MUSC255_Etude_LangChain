A TIMELY WARNING.

MISS ESTELLE RIVERA took lessons on the piano. Her father was a man of wealth, and though not all interested in music, he bought a grand piano and gave his daughter lessons, seemingly to demonstrate that really he did the same. Miss Estelle practiced in a diabolical manner and took two lessons a week, with furious interest and the look in putting up her hair as if she were wringing the pianos; she looked pale, took all necessities and she waged what she was done. MUSIC IN THE HOUSEHOLD.

By ARTHUR E. HOLT.

That music is no longer regarded as a mere social pastime, but is taking its just rank as the greatest normal educator, is no longer largely a matter of argument and discussion. The general community is now convinced that it is distinctly a factor in the cultivation of morals and religion, the inestimable value it has in the family circle should be highly appreciated. 

The difficulty of notation has, of course, been felt mainly by teachers; and this century has witnessed the proposal of hundreds of means of relief, thoroughly going and radical, all of which are not of a single origin. The tonic sol-fa has had and still retains a large following of complete and partial advocates among the most thoroughly enlightened teachers of the day.

As the widely used instruments have mostly been based upon the old systems of notation, inevitably some of the older devices of the notation have arisen to great difficulty, in which experience has demonstrated both in the teaching of the language and the correct ideas of the harmonics and the former expressiveness of music.

As for the composers, aside from the paltry attempt to present any new working out of their ideas, there are those who indicate or, at least, inspire what attorney in selfishness could commend the merit of individual works to any expression.

VOCALIZING AND SINGING. 

BY E. KIRCHNER.

What I propose in this response to Mr. Peasants' request to say something about singing shall turn out to be a beneficial reminder to teachers of singing, perhaps it will reconcile them to me in this manner, I confess at the outset that I have always had more or less prejudice against the vocal instructor. I say vocal instructor advisedly! I must mean your kind teacher. This prejudice for a while kept me in growth within my love for singing. I can remember the time when I did not like the educational context of poetry in my vocal phrases and often did certain vocal exercises more with a sense of frustration than enthusiasm.

Being accustomed to the classical music of my environment, I was jolted by teachers who had their own narrow viewpoint, straying away from the standard and combining it, in my mind, with a mere show of scientific knowledge. 

I noticed that students, led actively, had difficulties and often failed to lift their heads with back connecting voice; they encumbered themselves with technical exercises that made them more mechanical.

This was illustrated within the realm of vocalization, where I began to understand why one person struggled to feel the source of their own sound. I began thinking how important these processes are in the potential improvement of teaching.

Many experienced singers do, in fact, know precisely how to move forward in the discipline, and though their rosy expectations are not always met, they provide crucial insights into effectively expressing the source of one's own sound and voice. 

This means that education becomes an expansive journey for the self. Yet, within the constraints often imposed by traditionalists, the genuine nature of vocal expression can be lost.

I won't deny that I didn’t try to grasp the beauty of singing pure and simple. According to the statement of Domont, descriptive of the daily routine of those schools, the number of hours that are compared with those devoted to studying each of its rings was vast. This was true for those learning to hear the ringing of Bononcini’s record. He is speaking of the Peal singing school in Rome—the school in which Farinelli was educated. 

The pupils of those schools were obliged to practice one hour daily in difficult instructional scenarios to secure an easy execution; another hour was devoted to the precise melody with its range.

Now, observe that the after side of this picture is that another school was the contrary. 

It is here the development of simple singing has adequate importance, for without the nurturing of these methods, the risk of losing a listener’s attention is real.

The heart of the methods the instructions took must return, as the great standard used in pedagogy has voiced, to accommodate, thrive, and become the core around which everything must be nurtured.

Finally, I have often pondered about the manner in which vocal teachers respond to students. The thoughtfully crafted exercises often lead to rewards, but in some situations, one must surely appear to be more reactive than proactive to be fulfilling one’s purpose as a creator and educator. 

Many singing students indeed plod clumsily through their performances, easily disregarding the tactile responses - often misrepresenting how they experience voice.

In this landscape, it is crucial to maintain a balance between technical excellence and artistic expression. A conscious engagement with the subtleties of each voice leads toward learning as an exciting journey rather than a mere checklist to complete.

Humoreske

In order to carry out the composers intention, this piece requires a variety of touch and expression; besides artistic phrasing and careful use of the pedals. Grieg calls it a "Humoreske" and while it possesses all the essentials to a humorous piece as do other figures like Harlequin and Pantalon. Here we only see Columbine, dainty and pretty, attired in exquisite colors and radiant in beauty, vivacious and brilliant yet reserved and thoughtful. A vivid imagination could draw many pictures into this short tone poem, which as all of Grieg’s compositions has its "formal" shortcoming but never the less is full of character, aiming after an ideal.

The left hand must be played non legato in measures 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 27, 28, 29, etc. at 31, 32, as strongly accenting the first and third notes. This very accentuation produces an effective accompaniment to the melody of the right hand. Measures 5, 6, 13, 14 require a heavy legato touch, as if to imitate brass instruments of an orchestra. In measures 7, 8, 14–15, the half notes are well accentuated, both pedals are then held down, which changes the quality of tone and forms a charming contrast to the preceding forte. On piano at the beginning of the second part means that the keys should be played with the assistance of the arm, without however accelerating the passage. SLUMBER SONG

The Slumber Song of Heller does not offer so many peculiarities of execution as some of the pieces which have preceded. But it requires a full and song-like tone in the melody, and a very delicate performance of the left hand part, and a proper rise and fall of intensity in the piece as a whole. These necessities will probably occupy considerable of the student's time, and fully disabuse him of his first impression that the piece is too easy for its place.

Everything turns upon a simple and song-like delivery, which must come from the heart. The pedal has to be used almost constantly, but never while two different chords are played. Hence it is taken once or twice in every measure, and occasionally three times, but always just as the new chord begins, and never in such a way as to interfere with the tone-continuity between successive chords.

BY A. L. MANCHESTER.  
I WONDER if any of my readers have ever viewed their work (I refer to teachers of piano) particularly from the standpoint of a physician.  
There is, after all, a psychological study in teaching the piano, which is becoming quite pronounced.
