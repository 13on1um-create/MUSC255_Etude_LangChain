THE ETUDE.

MENDELSSOHN'S MUSIC FOR PIANISTS.

Some months ago, there appeared in The Etude an article signed "Old Maud," in which, besides challenging some of the opinions previously expressed by a number of our musical contemporaries, the writer made some pointed allegations in reference to the estimate placed upon Mendelssohn's piano-forte music by the pianists and thinkers of our day. She asked that a reply be made through the columns of The Etude, by some one of its editors. I was inclined to write for such an answer, I undertook it, in lieu of any other champion, and will cast down the gauntlet at once by saying that I agree absolutely and heartily with the favorable opinion of Mendelssohn's piano-forte music expressed by the writer in question. Since her reply to "Old Maud" I would say that the general opinion among leading musicians to-day, in reference to all Mendelssohn's works, seems to be unfriendly, or at least indifferent and patronizing.

In Continental or home circles, Mendelssohn's music is still held in great regard. Not so with our moderns. Mendelssohn's compositions are seldom heard here in concerts, either of artists or pupils. Miss Maud plays his concertos in G minor occasionally, and with that inimitable grace which characterizes her performances; but the daily use of his works is restricted to such schoolrooms as, in a manner, countenance domestic musical culture. There has been very little effort to bring his serious works before the American public, or to cultivate a taste for them.

After some interesting conversations with musicians of other countries, I became convinced of the general prevalence of this disposition here. To the modern musician, indeed, Mendelssohn's music seems to have become a matter of polite complacency rather than of living sympathy. It is criticized as graceful and refined, but as wanting in strength and passion. Young pianists, who make the study of Liszt, Chopin and Schumann their chief occupation, are content to regard Mendelssohn as a pleasant classic -- nothing more. They play his easier pieces, but avoid his greater works, as if they were of an order requiring critical cultivation which has ceased to be fashionable.

There is a marked distinction between the Mendelssohn of the salon and the Mendelssohn of the concert-room; and I am persuaded that a thorough revival of his pianistic works would be of great service to the cultivation of taste among our young players. His music is not superficial. It demands a lucid and delicate touch, combined with keen intellectual sympathy. Mendelssohn's style is primarily lyrical; his melodies are of beautiful line, and his harmonic treatment is pure and refined. To do him justice at the piano one must possess both technical resources and an artistic temperament capable of achieving the requisite quiet nobility.

The school that now prevails among us trains for power rather than for taste. It is quite possible to flare and flash upon the keyboard with little comprehension of the structural and poetic properties of the music. Mendelssohn does not reward such bravura. He requires restraint, culture, and a cultivated ear. To play him intelligently is to be free from all mannerisms and superfluous display; and in this respect I say most decidedly that Mendelssohn's piano-forte compositions are of the highest value for pupils who are to grow into cultivated pianists. His studies and his easier pieces exercise the same bright, crystalline qualities which are the foundation of all cultivated tone and touch.

Take his Songs Without Words, his Etudes, and his Preludes and Fugues, and you have works especially adapted to form taste. The study of Mendelssohn is, in fact, a discipline of mind as well as of technique. His phrasing, his rhetorical figures, his neatness of texture, are exactly the opposite of the turbid, smoky, or merely physical kind of display which so many aspire to produce. If we would reform our pianistic taste we must have more of Mendelssohn's lucidity and less of the theatrical noise. There is much more in him than what a cursory approach will discover, for his music is not primarily dramatic in the modern sense, but a steady and noble expression of musical thought.

The Etude needs to plead for more Mendelssohn in the homes and schools. By encouraging the study of his works we shall not only enrich the musical intelligence of pupils, but establish a purer standard of taste. It is time to reawaken an appreciation of his compositions, both the simpler pieces which are suitable for the younger pupil, and the larger works which demand discretion and culture.

THREE CLASSES OF PUPILS.

There are three classes of pupils everywhere, that differ in the teacher the graces are placed so very low.

CLASS I.
THE QUICK AND CAREFUL.

Who come in once a week, and in class promptly, well dressed, and with clearly-recognized ability to obey. One hour a week will hardly improve their respect for the forms of music. To those who are possessed of the faculty to obey there should be given the lessons for that week, and they should be expected to practice the corrections. A few of this kind will improve very far beyond their chance. They will be our best supporters in any society which desires to encourage music.

CLASS III.
THE ROADWAY-MUCHOREL?
Who has just graduated and returned home to make her debut in society. In other words, to play her part in the opening scene of the street department where is required the usual "Dover" of experience. The hand is filled with work laid out by the teacher, but the student requires constant and delicate supervision of details. They are mostly the girls who take to the stage and make their impression by manner rather than by music. They are a most wearisome set when one has a number of these pupils.

LESSONS IN MUSICAL HISTORY.

III. J. C. FILLMORE.

The epoch of the Netherlands, about 1490 to 1600.

With the beginning of the fifteenth century there came a new and very important element in the history of music—the epoch of the development and elevation of the science and art of harmony. It is commonly called the era of polyphony. It became noticeable about the time of the Netherlanders, because Netherland composers took the most prominent part in the movement and were for some time unequaled in Europe in musical power. For more than a hundred and fifty years. After that time Italians and others, who had learned the art, shared their supremacy, and with the death of Orlando di Lasso, in 1594, they disappear from the pages of history.

As we have already said, the ground had been prepared for them by the intervention and gradual improvement of an adequate system of musical notation, and by numerous composers, who had tried their hand at "discords." Harmonies were treated as incidental, or as sufficient supports for melody, which was all the composer had thought of before. The development of the voices, the wants and the imagination of the art-masters led to new effects which made polyphony gradually a dominant force in the musical life of Europe from about 1400 up to the very last decay of the conservatism, but within whose bounds the art had become classical.

EARLY DUTCH, 1380-1430. (?)

The first distinguished Netherland composer was Guillaume Du Fay, a Belgian. His countrymen who were made one of the chief recorders in the archives of the chapel of the duke of Burgundy were the ancestors of Du Fay, but Du Fay was the first widely known singer. Both in the progression of his art and in the treatment of his harmonies he is said to have made marked advances on his predecessor, and paved the way for a sharper perception of what is natural and fitting, in those who were to come after him. In his generally credited view he was the founder of a new school which had in its earliest form but little to do with the church modes. It was from him that the Netherland school of polyphony took its most prominent cues.

ANTOINE BUSNOIS, 1430-1492 (?).

From a distinguished Netherland composer of the fifteenth century, Busnois, the modern world owes much in musical art, so also did his successors, who carried on and developed his teachings. The Burgundian period was in some measure due to the political importance of the Netherlands, and their commercial activity brought them into close relations with other countries and arts. So their music was increasingly diffused and appreciated, and the forms and styles developed at that time became models for subsequent centuries. Their music, originally simple, grew in complexity and grandeur.

OCKEGHEM, 1420-1497.

The name of Johannes Ockeghem stands as a representative of the composers of the later fifteenth century whose works were not only distinguished by their art, but by the dignity of their musical conception. Ockeghem's masses are notable for their learned counterpoint and novel treatment of voice-leading, and for the size and variety of their composition. He carried the art farther than his predecessors and constructed a school of composition whose influence lasted for many years.

JOSQUIN DES PREZ, 1450-1521.

Josquin des Prez stands at the head of the school of Flemish composers who dominated European music in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. His music combined expressive melody with a profound sense of form and workmanship. Josquin's work is characterized by clarity of expression, a masterly command of counterpoint, and an artistic balance between parts. European fame and influence were his; his music was studied and imitated by others for more than a century after his death.

OTHE REPRESENTATIVES.

The group of Netherland composers included many other distinguished names who carried on the polyphonic art: Isaac, Obrecht, Brumel, Willaert, Adrian Willaert, and others who preserved and extended the traditions Josquin had given them. These composers founded what is known as the Netherland school, whose influence reached Italy and other parts of Europe, and in whose methods the groundwork of the later Renaissance music was laid.

THE DECLINE.

With the time of Palestrina and Lasso the school of Netherland composers passed into new forms, and the supremacy of the Flemish masters was supplanted by Italian influence and the new ideas of the late Renaissance, though their technical achievements remained a foundation for the subsequent history of musical composition.

AMERICAN COLLEGE OF MUSICIANS.

(For the Etude.)

When the constitutional members of the A. C. M. were present at their first meeting they had allowed themselves to be influenced, no doubt, by the strong desire to place the means of technical and artistic advancement at the service of professional musicians. The object was to secure the best methods and the highest standards of musical instruction. THE PRACTICAL VALUE OF CERTAIN MODERN THEO-
RIES RESPECTING THE SCIENCE OF HARMONY.
BY J. C. FILLMORE.

The practical value, I emphasize the adjective, because we are here,
not specially as scientific investigators, but as practical teachers; and
scientific theories cannot be properly used as they condoed to make con-
clusions more than practical, if it can be shown that their use in the theory is
actually based on rational principles, then we shall find a real representa-
tive value to us. For only that teaching is really practical which acts
upon and applies principles and laws found in the nature of things;
this also is rational and permanent. The question for us is, therefore:
Have these and any real discoveries of natural principles been enunci-
ated generally accepted and applied? If so, what are they, and how can
we put them to real practical use, so as to simplify our teaching and make
it more rational and intelligible? To the best of my knowledge, the
answer is in the affirmative, so far as the subject of Harmony is con-
cerned. It is needless for me to speak of fundamental theories in the
abstract, and that they are of immense practical value. The princi ples
which I hope to make clear relate: (1) to the Major Chord and Minor
Scale and (2) to Tonality and Modulation.

I. The Major Chord and Minor Scale.

(a) Motive. Liaphanousness: Some thirty-three years ago, Dr.
Mozart Hauptmann, of Leipzig, published a work called "Die Natur
der Harmonie," and we shall only say a few of profound insight into
the true nature of harmonic relations, so vigorous in its philoso-
phical thinking and so suggestive, that in a word it may be called the
beginning of the modern science of harmony. One of the most notable
and suggestive portions of the work is that which treats of the minor chords,
and this is the point which mainly concerns us at this time. Haupt-
mann called attention to the fact that the interval of the minor
third, which is familial in its origin in speech and emotion, results
from a diminution of the major third in passage from the major to the
minor mode. The so-called natural chord, or chord of the first degree
of the minor scale, he argues, is formed by the addition of the minor
third instead of the major third. He shows that in the harmonic se-
ries the minor third is represented by the 6th and 7th partials, whereas
the major third is represented by the 5th and 6th, and that hence the
minor third is not an accidental or arbitrary interval as many seem to
imagine, but it is a necessary consequence of the organization of the
tonal series. Mr. H. Riemann and other modern theorists have built
upon this basis with great wealth of suggestive results.

We will not pause to discuss the many refinements and scholas-
tic disputes which have arisen regarding the exact origin of these
intervals, but will only note that on this foundation a clear and practical
conception of the relation between major and minor has been estab-
lished. The minor chord, as commonly used, differs from the major
chord by the presence of the minor third instead of the major third,
and this difference is precisely that which gives the minor its different
color and emotional quality. For practical purposes in elementary
instruction the student should be taught to perceive the minor chord
as the major chord with a flattened third, and likewise to understand
the minor scale in relation to the major scale by the same type of
diminution. Thus the natural minor may be thought of as the major
with the 3d degree lowered.

A careful analysis of the overtone series shows that the interval
we call the minor third corresponds nearly to the ratio 6:5, and the
major third 5:4, and the difference between these is slight but suffi-
cient to justify the distinguishing quality of the minor and major
modes. It ought to be noted that the ear is extremely sensitive to
this slight variation when trained; indeed the faculty of distinguishing
these two kinds of thirds is among the earliest refined discriminations
which good musical education secures. The practical result is, then,
that a rational system of ear-training may be founded upon the natural
relations of the harmonic series, and that the proper conception of
the minor chord as derived from diminution of the major third gives
a helpful and clear account of such matters.

(b) The Minor Scale; Its Emotional Value.

The minor scale, in its various modes—natural, harmonic and
melodic—has apodictic behavioral characteristics which have been
made the subject of much aesthetic speculation. The principal
difference between the natural minor and the major scales is the
existence of the flattened third; and in the harmonic minor the
augmented second between the 6th and raised 7th (i.e., the interval
between 6 and 7 where 7 is raised) is another significant feature.
In practice, the minor scale produces that feeling of sadness, pensiveness,
and longing which we call "minor quality." To teach this in a
practical way to beginners, I have found that the best method is to
compare corresponding degrees of the two scales, and to make the
student sing and play these contrasts until the ear perceives them
as distinctly as the sight perceives color differences. The study of
arpeggios and chord progressions in both modes yields excellent re-
sults in cultivating the student's tonal feeling.

II. Tonality and Modulation.

(a) Tonality: The tone-scale. Some thirty-three years ago, Dr.
Mozart Hauptmann, of Leipzig, published a work called "Die Natur
der Harmonie," and we shall only say a few of profound insight into

The old idea of key was confined to the tones of the scales. The tonic
or keynote was held to be the central tone of the system; its position

THE PRACTICAL VALUE OF CERTAIN MODERN THEO-
RIES RESPECTING THE SCIENCE OF HARMONY.

By J. C. FILLMORE.

The practical value. I emphasize the adjective, because we are here,
not specially as scientific investigators, but as practical teachers; and
scientific theories concern in primary only as they conduce to make our
teaching more practical. If it can be shown that this or that theory is
actually based on rational principles, then we shall find it of real prac-
tical value to us. For only that tendency is really practical which ex-
poses and applies principles and laws found in the nature of things;
this alone is rational and permanent. The question for us is, therefore;
Have there been any real discoveries of natural principles and laws not
yet generally accepted and applied? If so, what are they, and how can
we put them to practical use so as to simplify our teaching and make
it more rational and intelligible? The object of this paper is to suggest
an answer to these questions, so far as the subject of Harmony is con-
cerned. I am decidedly of the opinion that such discoveries have been
made, and that they are of immense practical value. The principles
which I hope to make clear relate: (1) to the Minor Chord and Minor
Scale; and (2) to Tonality and Modulation.

I. The Minor Chord and Minor Scale.

(A) Moritz Hauptmann's Theories. Some thirty-three years ago, Dr.
Moritz Hauptmann, of Leipzig, published a work called "Die Natur
der Harmonik und der Metrik," in which he called for profound insight
into the true nature of harmonic relations as exhaustively as the phi-
losophic thinker, and as suggestive, then, he was regarded as not in the
development of the theory of music. One of the most notable and
suggestive portions of the work is that which treats of the minor chords,
and of the relation of the minor to the major scales. Hauptmann
began aimed attention to the fact that the interval of the minor
third or which is only a relative consonance is not the first, as in the major
chord, but the second. In the major chord the second—that is, the minor
third, as in C major chord, rests the harmonic tone, as Hauptmann
first, for lower tone, is the formative one, on which the unity of
the chord is conditioned. It was in a third, and a fifth, dependent on it,
growing out of it. In the minor chords the conditions are reversed.
The third and fifth do not grow out of the first (or lower) tone, but are
first on it. To this, in short the organ of the half notes is fifth of the
lower tone and third of the third one. In Hauptmann's view, the third
(the minor third), and the major third, etc., are only intervals that
could be considered directly intelligible. All other intervals be derived
from these. The relation of a major third he finds between the third and
fifth of the minor chord. He is anxious to avoid, he concludes which
determine the unity are nervous—the root has a third and a fifth. In the
minor chord, the conditions which determine the unity are preserved, the
root note is third and a fifth. The two chords are, therefore, recipro-
cals, counterparts, polar opposites. He points out, also, that this
reciprocal relation is not only valid as regards their repetitive points of
unity, but also as regards their numerical proportions and their character-
istic emotional expression. The numerical proportions he lays down for
the intervals of the major chord are: 4: 5: 6; those of the minor chord are
: 1: : 2: 3.

2:1:1? The number rule, he thinks, striven upward; the minor drops
downward. He compares the later to a sweeping willow; the former to
a tree whose branches point upward. The characteristic expression of
the major chord is, in its boldness, upward striving, aspiration,
forceful activity. That of the minor chord is rather heavy-heartedness,
depression, melancholy, passive submission and endurance.
It seems never to have been conceded to Hauptmann that the real point of
unity of the minor chord might also be its real generation. He saw no
reason, apparently, for thinking the chord downward instead of upward.
He does, indeed, insist that the tone C is not only the point of unity of

its points out their reciprocal relations. But it seems not to have
occurred to him that the minor chord as well as the major might be named
from the tone on which it has its count in condition; that intervals can be
thought downward as well as upward; that it is really just as truly a major

a fifth from C down to F as from F up to C. But this was perhaps, more
than was to have been expected. All honor to Hauptmann for the force,
suggestive, vigorous thinking which made further advances possible. He
opened by way and pointed out the direction in which others were to go
farther than he did himself. We owe an inviolable consequence of his
discoveries about the minor chord that they should lead to others.
Thinkers were far too much who would accept this view as true to be as they
were, and who would not be able to stop with his conclusions. Once
admit that the unity of the minor chord is determined by its upper tone

The Techniphone is an instrument with a piano-like keyboard and gentle piano touch, designed to render effective aid to pupils in learning the mechanical part of the technique of piano-playing.

It is the result of long experience as a practical teacher, and embodies the perfect adaptation of mechanical means to the ends of technical instruction. It enables the pupil to obtain an even and uniform legato and a precision of action which can not be acquired in so short a time upon the piano without fatigue, and which, when once acquired upon the Techniphone, will be retained upon the piano with great facility.

The instrument is especially valuable to teachers of the higher and middle grades, because it relieves the pupil of the embarrassment caused by the tone of the piano, and enables the teacher to separate the technical from the musical difficulties.

Three months of faithful work on the Techniphone will give a better foundation in all the elements of touch and finger independence than is ordinarily secured by the same practice on a piano.

The tone of the Techniphone is clear, sweet and sympathetic, and while differing from the piano in quality, is best adapted to the purposes for which the instrument is intended.

The Techniphone is auxiliary to the piano and not a substitute for it. It is designed to prepare the pupil for the piano, and to abbreviate the time required for acquiring the mechanical parts of technique.
