Youth and Music

American Folk Songs

By

Blanche Lemmon

AMERICAN YOUTH ARE PRIVILEGED this
year to be able to enjoy the treasurers which
we do not have. In England and across the Atlantic
there is a lively folk-song movement which
is devoted really to revivification as we understand
it. In England traditional songs are being collected
in a way similar to the way we collect art treasures.
It is only fair, however, to say that the Americans
have not been careless in this matter. There has
been from the beginning that studying has been
carried on in many circles. The work of local
groups and of institutions in collecting and preserving
the folk-music of our country has meant much
in the history of our musical development. It is
from this basis that the recent revival has grown.

There are two chief sources of the American
folk-song. One is the folksong of the white
stock, the songs of farmers, of seamen, of pioneers,
and of the people who have lived in small communities
in America. The other source is in the songs
of the Negro. Both sources have produced
important material.

It is necessary to distinguish carefully between
the task of preserving the songs and the task of
using them in the modern musical life. Too often
they are used merely for curiosities. We must
learn to understand and to appreciate them so
that they may be used as living sources of music.
To this purpose many educators and music
lovers have turned their efforts. Folk-song collect-
ing projects are being undertaken in many
parts of the country and the results are revealing
a treasure of simple tunes and old words.

In addition to Mr Lomax's work in the important
work mentioned above, there are a number
of other field workers who have gone out among
the people and learned the songs with the same
enthusiasm. The collections of these songs have
been published in many forms and are now the
basis of much of the renewed interest in American
music. The Library of Congress has been notably
active and many of the folk-songs now being
sung in schools and colleges were first brought
to light by the courage of these pioneers of
music research.

The interest of the present generation is not
only in the preservation of the songs but in the
use which may be made of them in schools,
in concert halls and in the home. The tendency
is to adapt them to the modern sense and to
teach them in a way that will make them as
natural as the songs of any country. In the
process of adaptation there is danger, of course,
of losing the simplicity and the flavor of the
original. But if the songs are kept within reasonable
limits and with respect for their sources,
they may easily become a part of our living
musical tradition.

It is inevitable that the study of the folk-song
should lead to a reexamination of our musical
ideals. The songs give us a direct line to the
emotions and experiences of our people. They
are the music of our daily life, sung at work,
at play, and in the simpler celebrations. The
songs teach us about the life and the character
of the people who created them.

The student of folk-song must not be content
with the mere collecting of tunes. The true
worker must study the words, the history, and
the social setting of the song. He must be
willing to enter sympathetically into the life
from which the song came, and must give
attention to the variations in text and melody
which occur in different regions.

This does not mean that the songs should
be treated as museum pieces. In many instances
they are being used with respect and understanding
as living materials from which may be drawn
much of the real music of the future.

In schools especially the use of folk-songs is
proving of incalculable value. The child who
learns the simple songs of his native land gets
an immediate sense of musical values which
cannot be gained by the mere technical study
of music. Songs teach rhythm, melody, phrase
structure and the basic instincts of musical form.
In many cases the folk-song forms the basis
for the musical life of whole communities.

To meet the needs of the schools many new
arrangements have been made which preserve
the simplicity and the essential qualities of the
original. The best of these arrangements are
those which make the songs singable by the
young voices without distorting them.

It is fortunate that so many people are now
interested in the collection and preservation
of our folk music. The work is being carried
on by individuals, by state agencies, and by
national organizations. The Library of Congress,
the American Folklore Society, and the
Juvenile Music Clubs have all contributed to
this work. The combined efforts of these
groups are producing a body of material which
should form the basis of a genuinely American
musical literature.

Another important phase of the present work
is the training of those who will teach the songs.
A knowledge not only of the melodies and
words but of the social and historical background
is necessary. The teachers must be made
familiar with the resources available and with
the methods of presenting the songs in a way
that will be intelligible and natural to the
pupils.

Perhaps the best service which can be
rendered at the present time is to increase the
interest in the songs and to provide good
sources of material for teachers and students.
This demands patience and a steady, careful
study of what is practical and what is authentic.
With such guidance the American folk-song
may become truly a part of our everyday musical
life.

Just What Really Is
Swing Music?
By Ethan A. Snow

SWING MUSIC is a highly rhythmic presentation of melody with accompaniment in bass, but it is far more character and spirit than the following principles.

The accompaniment may be in any one of three ways (using the piano as illustration):

(a) A simultaneous bass note and middle chord on each beat of the measure.
(b) Fourths and fifths in the middle chord on the second and fourth beats of the measure.
(c) Two bass notes, on the first and third beats (shown in two octaves) and the middle chord on the second and fourth beats of the measure.

All of these will be played with a deserved accent on the first beat.

The melody notes (excluding grace notes etc.) may be accented on any beat either on the beat or off the beat (beat or on) in 4/4 time; eighth notes are usually played as a two-note syncopated system with eighths swung as a pair (the first of the pair slightly more pronounced) or the accented notes may be legato and the unaccented short. The swing or “shuffle” has a tendency to approach syncopation. Some tempos which sound machine-like (precisely even) in tempo are usually made stylish by the addition of a slight rubato. A slower tempo will usually have the swing pronounced and a faster tempo somewhat closer to straight rhythm. In jazz bands the repeated melodic pattern (riff) plays a very important part; it is usually short and repeated many times. The soloist improvises over the repeated background.

There are two practical methods for teaching the swing. One method is to teach a regular pattern to the left hand (or the accompanist) and have the melody play against it. The other is to have the accompaniment play the straight rhythmic pattern and the melody player (or soloist) learn to swing by accenting and delaying notes in accordance with the feel.

When Ear Ignores Eye

It would be interesting to know just how many contemporary composers and arrangers upon whom we rely for contemporary music are actually aware of the important fact connected with the art of the violin. In teaching or arranging for the violin one must be aware of the natural tendencies of the ear as it receives melody, harmony and rhythm. The average listener has certain expectations and when these are violated the music loses its appeal.

The ear sometimes does not accept things that are seen when the sight is different from the sound, and reason always tells us that the ear should govern.

Means of Correct Bowing
By
George H. Kurtz

One of the most important elements in the art of violin playing is correct bow manipulation. The musician when one is competent to become proficient in the production of tone upon almost indefinable parts is the mechanical adjustment of the bow. A teacher who has the proper idea of tone production will naturally insist upon correct bowing from the beginning, for the sound is easily spoiled otherwise. The bow should be moved across the strings so that however it is grasped, the hand remains free and the arm relaxed.

The bow makes true tone because the hair of the bow is maintained in contact with the string, producing friction and allowing the string to vibrate. The student must learn to control the bow so as to make the sound uniform, without forced accents except where required by the phrasing.

[Caption under photograph]
Correct position of bow on the strings

A few hints from the lower order of first principles. In the lower part of the bow the hand must be held so that the first finger points slightly inward so that the tip of the first finger and the thumb are nearly in a straight line with the string. The thumb must be slightly bent. The hand should be flexible at the wrist and the finger joints should be free. The arm must be relaxed and the upper arm should not be stiff.

The distribution of the weight of the arm and hand upon the bow is more easily adjusted when the bow is held with the second and third fingers larger and the thumb in its natural position. The right hand should hold the bow lightly enough to permit the fingers to act as springs in order to adjust the angle of the bow so the hair will remain in flat contact with the string.

Control From the Wrist

We come now to an important feature of bowing — the wrist. The wrist alone moves for most purposes while the forearm remains relatively stationary. There should be a slight inward motion of the wrist in the down-bow and a slight outward motion in the up-bow. By this means an even and steady tone can be produced. The wrist should not be too rigid, nor should it be too weak. A slight rising of the hand for the down-bow will give a warmer tone as the bow approaches the bridge while a slightly flatter hand will give a lighter tone nearer the fingerboard.

Bridge and the Arm

The bow must be drawn across the string at the proper distance from the bridge. The nearer to the bridge the more incisive and brilliant the tone; nearer the fingerboard the tone is softer and sweeter. The student should be taught to vary the point of contact so as to obtain the desired color.

The use of the upper arm and shoulder is reserved for loud, strong passages and for long, sustained notes. The elbow should move slightly and the forearm follow, but the motion must be governed by the wrist and fingers so as to produce a fine control over the tone.

The Right Hand Guideline

This right hand guideline will enable the player to control the bow and to obtain a wide variety of tones. Keep the hand relaxed, use a flexible wrist, maintain the bow hair in contact with the string, and vary the point of contact for tonal color. Study the balance of the bow so that the stroke will be even from frog to point.

The arm, shoulder and chest must be relaxed. Any stiffness will betray itself in the tone and in the control of dynamics. The student should practice long bows, playing slowly and listening attentively to the uniformity of tone. The bow should be straight in its track, neither angled nor shifting sidewise.

Careful attention should also be given to hold the bow in such a manner that the thumb does not press unduly against it. The little finger should touch lightly and be used as a balancing finger. The use of the index finger is important for pressure in forte and for incisive accents.

Before a pupil attempts any difficult bowing, the teacher should give control exercises for the wrist and hand which will develop the necessary suppleness and agility. These exercises help to free the arm and to enable the player to use the bow with authority and beauty.

Q. I am at present studying under two teachers and find that they do not agree about certain technical points. One says use the arm more; the other insists on the use of the wrist and fingers. What is your view?

A. There are many schools of thought on this matter. Broadly speaking the arm should furnish the power while the wrist and fingers provide the adjustment and finesse. The whole arm must be controlled and subordinate to the judgment of the player. [...]

THE PIANO ACCORDION

Organ Style of Playing Upon the Accordion

By Pietro Deiro
As told to Elmer Callens

The organ and the accordion have the same principles of construction in that the tone is produced by the same set of reeds. The difference is that the accordion is essentially a right hand instrument and the organ a two-hand instrument. The accordion is played on one side with the right hand and the left hand manages the bellows and the accompaniment buttons. The organist, on the other hand, controls two keyboards and pedals.

The principal difference in playing the two instruments lies in the use of the left hand in the accordion which must supply the bass and chord accompaniment while the right hand plays the melody and any accompanying figures. On the organ, the bass and harmony are more evenly distributed among hands and feet.

The main point of organ style on the accordion is to imitate the sustained tone and legato of the organ. For this reason much attention must be paid to the bellows control. The bellows should be moved smoothly so as to maintain a constant air pressure and thus an even tone. Abrupt starts and stops of the bellows should be avoided.

It was taken from one of my instruction books called "The Melody and Chord Method" and it shows how to get the full effect of legato. The player keeps an awareness of the phrase and aims to make each note sing as if it were sustained on the organ by pedal or swell box.
