The writer's experience leads him to believe that fully one-half of the time spent in masterly preparation at the piano is absolutely wasted; the results accountable are so meager in proportion to the expenditure of labor, that it is not infrequently happens that the pupil who does not understand the secret of successful practice, becomes discouraged and gives up his study in despair.

What is the cause of this state of affairs, and how can we better them? Perhaps these questions can best be answered by stating, concisely as possible, the conditions of correct practice, from which the reader can draw his own conclusions.

The ultimate object of all practice is the establishing of correct and physiological terms "muscle memory." Along each of the subjects that is made a study of, in its different and only the connections of them, any series of careful repetitions becomes eventually automatic, and gives rise to the neglect of conscious action of the will. Walking does not require any conscious thought; memory helps in this, for it is instinctive and necessarily that we rarely think of why we walk. This same is true with extreme efforts of concentrated symphony playing; the instant obedience to the obtained control of the will is known.

It is in this continual struggle before the student to practice in a manner that shall develop independence to direct his own actions, and therefore the mastery of simple or complex actions.

From the beginning, the pupil should form the habits of listening to his own playing; a fine artist should observe more realistic and mental control of the music; the actual results of the Brotherhood Techniques will be followed by the surprising result; by the aid of notes attached to the music it should be with such rapidity that maintaining it should be as indispensable as the practice has no play whatsoever.

PATENT JANKO KEYBOARD

This new keyboard consists of six rows of keys, placed one above the other. The most essential peculiarity of this arrangement is that each key has its own individual place regardless of whether it is struck with the thumb, forefinger, middle finger, or any other finger. The keys are arranged in a single plane or wood as shown in the figure.

The Janko keyboard is a mixture of several systems. Its keys have a finger spread and may be operated by the same hand including the black keys. When starting from the flat, there is an excellent alternative to the extension. This keyboard also enables easy transitions and fingering.

Fig. 1 shows the chromatic scale represented in two octaves. Every note of the matrix maintains a more natural performance for all instruments. Each position can be adjusted to a standard striking which is most natural.

Fig. 2 represents the natural position of the hand and wrist in striking the chord. C, G, E, and the thumb is noted further down. Those who have studied this chord system will remember how it defines each passageway. While critical studies assist in the proper extension of fingers, switching from intervals and eliminating conditions or over-usage of finger positions can provide ample rewards.

The ease of fingering is essential and directed differently than the conventional piano structure. Each key follows a distinct pattern leading to one’s music flow. Every bit of operation regardless of amplitude and motion can seem bizarre; however, they simplify the piece significantly.

In Fig. 3, the proper position for thumb use is indicated, enhancing the look and feel of navigation through keys seamlessly.
