The Importance of Musical Craftsmanship

In every art craftsmanship is often the determining factor between failure and success. But craftsmanship, as we see it, is the Siamese twin of inspiration in the formula of personal advancement. The two are inseparable; without one the other expires. All this seems so obvious to us that we cannot comprehend how anyone with wide musical experience can hold a contrary opinion.

Craftsmanship in musical creation does not come down from the skies like manna. It is usually the result of long and hard study. There is no question, however, that through some humanly inscrutable process some people are gifted with far more perspicacity than others. That is, they are more quick-witted, more comprehensive, more understanding, more sharp-eyed, more sharp-eared, more acute in every way. They are born that way and that is all there is to it. Scientists, anthropologists, biologists, geneticists, historians, and theologians have spent lifetimes trying to tell us why, with about as much effect as trying to tell why a rose is beautiful. The fact is that we all have different fields of vision. Schopenhauer used to say, "Every man takes his own field of vision for the limits of the world." We all have a tendency to bend our logic to fit our personalities and desires. This often leads to misunderstanding and heartless misjudgment of our fellows, all seeking for truth.

Many are born with an inexplicably sharp musical-art judgment. This is often so enigmatic that it is not surprising that it is looked upon as a miracle of God. Mozart was certainly such a case, as has been the whole army of "wonder children" who have amazed the world. How small and those astonishing little ones who have acquired in their later years what adults have labored in vain for is to secure? They certainly seem blessed with a kind of intuitive insight endowed by a divine power—the power which mankind for centuries has recognized as God.

Many require long study under several masters to acquire craftsmanship—the art of holding their ideas and the most efficient method. Some achieve craftsmanship in remarkably short periods of study under masters whose names are not historically prominent. There is, of course, no rule here. Years of patient training will often produce an artist of great stature; sometimes genius flares rapidly. It is folly to attempt to measure any of these events by ordinary standards of time and technique.

No one, however, can get very far in music without craftsmanship, technique, the "know-how" of the trade. Many with great talents have failed through the absence of technique. There are many examples in the history of music. Lack of technique is a real handicap and must be remedied if the native endowments are to be given full play.

It is well to remember, however, that craftsmanship is not the only essential. There is always required something more than mere technical proficiency. Craftsmanship must be welded to imagination and to art itself. The maker of a fine instrument can produce reliable tools for others; the artist must do more. Persons who measure everything by their own narrow canons are always handicapped. They confuse mechanical skill with artistic worth. The true test of art is the effect it produces upon others. That test is neither mechanical nor solely subjective; it is human and social. The consummate artist rises above the rules and becomes a law unto himself; that law, however, must be intelligible and convincing to all who feel the same artistic sense.

There have been in recent years many heated disputes between those who insist that art is purely subjective and those who claim that definite standards must be maintained. Some of these controversies have been witty and stimulating, others irritating and barren. We are often told that the music of bygone days was narrow and philistine while modern composers are the only true progressives. Some of the modern masters have done brilliant and constructive work. Others have been largely destructive.

Only a short time ago we heard an English writer say that the music of the eighteenth century was merely a prelude to the real creative outpourings of the twentieth century. Such statements are usually the product of prejudice and of a desire to shock rather than to enlighten. The fact remains that good craftsmanship in earlier times produced masterpieces which even modernity must respect.

We must point out, also, that the whole question of "progress" in music is a complex one. Not all change is progress; many changes are merely fashions, and fashions come and go. The idea that everything new is necessarily better than everything old is a dangerous dogma, especially in art. The essence of craftsmanship is that it maintains continuity with the best of the past while permitting new interpretations and genuine growth.

There are, of course, many modern musicians whose technical feats are staggering. Some of them impress one most by their virtuosity. But virtuosity, after all, is not the same as artistry. A brilliant technician may still be a shallow artist. The secret lies in the relation between craftsmanship and expressive purpose. When technique becomes a servant of expressive content the miracle of art occurs. When it is merely an end in itself, the music suffers.

It is well, therefore, to cultivate in our schools and conservatories a genuine sense of musical craft. Young people must be taught not merely to perform but to understand the structural values of the works they interpret. They should be trained in the delicate balance between fidelity to the score and the living response of the interpreter. The greatest teachers have always insisted upon unity of purpose in all these respects.

Many of our conservatory methods are valuable and should be retained. Yet there is always opportunity for improvement. We ought to emphasize creative thought, listening, and the capacity to form a personal judgment. These qualities are as essential as cleanliness of technique and accuracy in performance. The cultivation of taste is the noblest task of the teacher. It cannot be accomplished by formula. It is, in large measure, a question of example, of the teacher's own character and genius for imparting love of the art.

We have sometimes been impressed with the fanaticism displayed by some critics and educators who would measure everything by their peculiar standard. Nothing is more destructive of young talent than narrow and officious criticism. The great teacher must be at once exacting and broad-minded. It is his duty to correct and stimulate, not to crush.

What we urge here is deliberate and catholic training, one that is both firm and generous. The art of music calls for craftsmanship of a high order; it calls as well for imagination and feeling. The two together constitute the soul of art. Let the conservatories and the teachers be judged by their success in producing men and women who can combine both.

ETUDE is no arena for polemical discussions, religious or otherwise, and we do not propose to start one now. We note the rise of a powerful wave of spirituality in the world when Martin Luther spoke of music as "next to religion the only art that can calm the agitation of the soul." He was quite in line with the most recent philosophy of natural theology.

The recognition of a divine power from which all evidence of existence rises is so widespread that many of the most violent agonists of the past have come to admit the existence of the soul and the spirit even as the final word in philosophy. It is hardly necessary here to rehearse the arguments for the existence of God. They are well known and are constantly restated by the great thinkers. Only a few would now deny the reality of the spiritual order.

Many of the foremost musicians of our time have given to the world an experience of the divine. They have opened up for us, as it were, the doors of a spiritual sanctuary in which the soul may dwell and be strengthened. The music of Schubert, who sometimes wrote four songs in a day, and later on was unable to recognize them as his own? Surely they were not ground out as soulless human compositions! As we peruse the great music of the nineteenth century we are deeply impressed by the sense of devotion and heartfelt sincerity that pervades so much of it. The works pay tribute to their Maker, as, did Johann Sebastian Bach with such phrases as "To God Alone Be Glory," and "In the Name of Jesus." Even those who lived worldly lives often opened to pay tribute to a divine source.

Music students in the great music schools of the world have acquired amazing craftsmanship. There have been hundreds of pianists who have, we are convinced, a real artistic attainments and a sense for the almost unconscious perfection of the creative process. Not until science is able to create a violet, a rose, or an orchid in a test tube can we, in this age of mechanism, claim with the smug materialists who contend that musical creation is merely the end result of an academic production line.

Look and Listen
I play two pianos with a friend of mine, but we are not satisfied about certain aspects of our performance. We find difficulty in falling together on the first beats, especially after a rest, or a cadenza. It gives our playing a character of insecurity and carelessness. What would you suggest as a remedy? — H. S., Michigan

Two-piano performance requires long study and considerable rehearsing if the partners are going to have that "feel" which makes each one aware of what the other one is going to do. After long years of public playing some teams develop it to such an extent that they sound like one single instrument. At times their identical rendering of shadings, phrasing, accents, and even the expression, is nothing short of amazing. They are musical twins who cannot be distinguished from each other.

However, you may be sure that for a long time they had some way of using signals, and perhaps they still do so. This can be done so discreetly as to remain unnoticed. A very slight nodding of the head may be enough, or a quick look, all of which does not go beyond the footlights. It is there, nevertheless, and it works.

With patience and perseverance, you and your partner will likely evolve a signal system of your own which will work out to your satisfaction. Then later on you may be able to discard it and enjoy that feeling of security which will be the best reward for your efforts.

More Stumbling (subhead partly legible)
In my study of the piano I have a difficulty about which I would like to request your advice. I believe it has come to do with nervousness, and with the fingers. When I have learned a certain passage quite well I often stumble when starting it. After that I go through the piece quite comfortably. I need only twenty or thirty hours of practice and you'll get your suggestion and would be glad for any corrective help. — (Mrs.) F. V. L., Maine.

[Answer text partly legible:] ... I could be sure that you start stumbling has nothing to do with either nervousness or the fingers. Rather, I would ascribe it to a condition which I have observed many times and for which I give the following advice: Please take time to get started—comfortably and conveniently. If the score is too low, ask for a cushion, or some books; if it is too high, don't hesitate to ask for a lower one. Place your feet on the pedals, ready for action. Then concentrate your thoughts on the tempo of the piece, so you can start at the proper speed instead of using the first measure to get adjusted. Better still; hum inwardly the first measure, and count the beats mentally. With such preparation you ought to "pitch in" right. Too many people start before they are ready. Here, once again, be wise: "Take time to take time." — (Mrs.) B. M. W., Georgia.

Concerning the Concertmaster
A Conference with
Harry Zarief
Concertmaster, CBS Symphony Orchestra
by Gunnar Asklund

[Portrait photograph — caption:]
HARRY ZARIEF

BEING a concertmaster is a field in itself — different both from solo work and from playing in the orchestral ranks. May I say at the start that neither the concertmaster nor the orchestral player is a frustrated virtuoso? He is, rather, a musician with a set of native abilities which fit him for ensemble work and, in the case of the concertmaster, for ensemble leadership. He is the orchestra because he wants to be the leader, and he develops the abilities born in him, he scores an important place in music and secures to himself an interesting, satisfying life.

The concertmaster plays with the most, occupying the first desk of the first violin section. Necessarily, he must be a man of wide orchestral experience. He must also be qualified to impart the conductor's interpretative wishes to his own section and to the orchestra as a whole. He must further be able to take over the conductor's duties, if necessary. This over-all picture varies somewhat according to whether he plays in an independent symphony or in a radio orchestra. The radio orchestras prepare their programs in a minimum of rehearsal time, and the programs vary in the range of every possible musical style, from symphonies to bits of background music. (A radio symphony orchestra, as a unit, does not play popular or dance music; however, many of the individual men take over work in these forms. Thus the concertmaster is required to be able to play in more than one sphere). The special radio requirements make it necessary for the radio musician to be even better prepared in forms and styles, even more alert to interpretative nuance, and even more fluent at sight-reading than the straight symphonic player. This is especially true

of the concertmaster engaged in radio work.

When I began my work as radio concertmaster, a colleague told me that the most important part of my job would be — knowing when not to play! That sounded odd; didn't the indicated rests take care of the silence? I soon learned better. Emergencies can arise. It is not impossible that, after the brief rehearsal time, and under the strain of a big broadcast, the conductor may raise his hand for an entrance a shade of a minute too soon. Then the concertmaster is able to help him by holding up his bow for the split second of time it takes for the conductor to catch himself. A good concertmaster must be alert and ready for things like that — indeed, for anything!

The concertmaster's post calls for extra musicianship, esprit, alertness, an extra sense of deep responsibility, plus those qualities of leadership which will enable him to transmit the conductor's wishes to the men, with harmony on all sides. Besides leading the first violins, his duties include:

1. Responsibility for the intonation of the entire orchestra. Before each rehearsal and each performance, the concertmaster takes the oboe (the official pitch giver) to sound "A." Then the orchestra tunes up, preparing itself as an instrument to be played upon by the conductor. The concertmaster must make sure of exact pitch.

2. A measure of responsibility for the discipline of the orchestra. When the conductor wishes quiet to begin rehearsal, he tells the concertmaster, who then gets the men into order.

3. The possible need for conducting rehearsals if the conductor leaves the podium.

4. Responsibility for understanding the conductor's interpretations and for transmitting them to the men. Since the concertmaster has no contact with the conductor, he must be more than usually alert during rehearsals.

5. The ability to perform solo passages in all styles of work.

"The concertmaster does not rehearse the men without the conductor (except for the brief moments indicated), and he does not engage the players (although, in symphony orchestras, he is usually one of the auditioning body)."

How does the concertmaster come to his post? Naturally, there are three ways. Occasionally, a soloist of fine reputation is asked to take the position. If I am not mistaken, this was the case with both Mischa Elman and Nathan Milstein. The second way is for a thorough and persevering young musician to enter his section as a player and, on his record and on a call to the conservatories, to win his credit, in the manner of a man to attain the concertmastership of a small orchestra (student orchestras are especially useful as training), and to give a sufficiently good account of himself to be ranked as concertmaster material, from the start. In such cases, a solid basis of musicianship and experience lies behind the call.

Special Requirements

In radio, the requirements of the concertmaster are more stringent, if in a sense different, than in the symphonic field, for the reason that the radio orchestra must often play many kinds of music and change quickly from style to style. Because of the shorter rehearsal time and the pressure of broadcasting schedules, the radio man must have quick perceptions and must be able to accomplish rapid ensemble work. He must also be an excellent sight-reader and an alert leader. He must know how to make his first violins play together with a proper tone quality, and how to secure balance with the other sections. In addition to these musical requirements, the radio concertmaster must be of fine temperament, capable of maintaining good relations with both conductor and players under trying circumstances.

If the concertmaster is to be sound in his art he must be a musician who has the qualities and understanding necessary to meet varying conductors and their varying ideas. A novice who is too rigid will not fit well into the radio field where flexibility is required. A concertmaster must be able to blend, to shape the line, to phrase, and to make technical adjustments quickly.

The concertmaster has important duties in connection with principal bowings, phrasing and in preparing excerpts and parts for extra rehearsals. At the very least, he must see that his own desk is in order and must often be called upon to take precedence in decision and to stand between the conductor and his section as an intermediary. In the professional world, this duty assumes an importance which calls for experience, judgment and tact.

[Small section continued:] ... of the concertmaster's many duties, the following list contains the chief items required of an orchestral leader: exact intonation, knowledge of bowing and stringing, little time to instruct and demonstrate, discretion, the ability to control tempo and dynamics, and the capacity to be a convincing leader. The concertmaster must always be ready to put his abilities at the service of the conductor and of the orchestra.

The Finger Stroke in Piano Playing
by Henry Levine
Well-Known Pianist, Teacher, and Editor

In Collaboration With Annabel Comfort

WE can shape our fingers in several ways. For example, we can stretch them straight out as we line with the back of the hand. From this position, we can let the fingers tip in slightly, whereby we play on the soft part and, just behind the tips. This is the extended finger position. By curving the tips still farther in, we get the conventional rounded finger shape with tips pointing straight down. By pulling the fingers in a little more, the tips would point in so that we would play on our fingernails. If we continue the inward motion of our finger tips we end with the clenched fist.

It is interesting to note the changes in the hand position as the fingers change shape. When the arm is placed in playing position over the keys, another way, and the one usually suggested, is to hold a round object like a ball or apple. This should set the finger shape. Still another way to develop the finger shaping sense is to extend the fingers all the way out. Then bring them all the way in, in one movement, and then, without looking, have the fingers open up to the normal rounded shape. After a few trials and checkups, the fingers should find the proper shape.

Of the several shapes which fingers can assume, two are chiefly used in current piano playing; the arched or rounded position and the flat, extended position. The rounded finger with its soft rounded form and the rounded joint, and with fingers used in the conventional rounded form, is used for legato playing and for phrasing purposes. The flat or extended finger is of value for staccato, for fast repeated notes, for certain kinds of accent and for some unusual effects. Which style to use at any time is dependent upon the musical requirement and the personal formation of the hand.

There are still other devices for loosening the fingers; but these mentioned should suffice for general purposes. Another method for the rounded finger is the habit of placing the thumb in a characteristic position of the hand. Change the thumb position quickly and you will notice that his fingers form a natural arch. This natural curvature of the fingers should be kept when the arm is placed in playing position over the keys. After a few trials and checkups, the fingers should find the proper shape.

If the correct shape should be held without stiffness, the fingers will have constant nervous firmness in the hand. When the arm and wrist are relaxed there is less tension in the fingers, and in consequence less tendency to strain in playing. If, on the other hand, the fingers are carried too high the finger joints have a tendency to tire. The arm should be free and the wrist should be free from tension so that the motion of the finger tips can be used in a natural way and produce the proper tone.

There are still other devices for loosening the fingers: but those mentioned should suffice for general purposes. The arm must be well adjusted so that the fingers will operate freely. The rounded finger, in most cases, should be used for tone production while the flattened or extended finger may be used for certain effects. Use both types according to the demands of the music.

To see that the arm is not resting too heavily on the key bottom, move the wrist up and down a few times. Bruno Walter's Momentous Beethoven-Cycle

by Alfred Lindsay Morgan

ON FEBRUARY 27, Bruno Walter began a six weeks' series of Beethoven concerts, including performances of all nine symphonies and concerted works. This series, rightfully states its sponsor—the Columbia Broadcasting System—"serves as a fitting climax to Bruno Walter's two-year tenure as Musical Adviser to the Philharmonic-Symphony Orchestra of New York." Records may recall that last year the distinguished conductor celebrated the twenty-fifth anniversary of his first appearance with the New York Symphony, predecessor of the combined Philharmonic-Symphony. There is no question that during these years Walter's sensitive and dependable musicianship has greatly enriched the musical life in this country. From about 1934 to 1939, Walter was mainly in Europe, where he was associated chiefly with the Vienna State Opera and the Vienna Philharmonic Orchestra. He also conducted at Salzburg in 1938 and 1939. With the Nazi domination of the continent, Walter, like so many others, refused to remain, and America was fortunate in having him return to us permanently. The veteran conductor, now in his seventieth year, has long been a favorite with American audiences and with American record buyers.

Walter, born Bruno W. Schlesinger, began his career early, after study at the Stern Conservatory. By his twenty-fifth year he was an established Kapellmeister, having successfully served terms at Cologne, Hamburg, Breslau, Pressburg, Riga, and Berlin. In 1902, he was appointed conductor at the Vienna Hofoper. This was the real beginning of his long and brilliant career. In 1914 he succeeded Mottl as Hofkapellmeister and Generalmusikdirektor in Munich, remaining there until 1922. The character name his first visits to America, an appointment as conductor at the Staatskapelle Opera in Berlin (Charlottenburg), and in 1929 his succession to Furtwängler as conductor of the Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra. His guest performances in England, this country, and elsewhere were too numerous to state. After the First World War, Walter led the first season of German opera at Covent Garden, London. In England he has been much admired, and in France his popularity was equally great, both in concert and opera. (He became a French citizen in 1939.)

Walter is especially admired in his interpretations of the German romantics. The late composer of the school—Mahler—was a favorite of his, and, as much as anyone living, has done a great deal in keeping their music alive and before the public. Walter was a close friend of Gustav Mahler, and has written a book on the composer. As an interpreter of Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, and Schumann, Bruno Walter has enjoyed considerable prestige. His sensitive nature has endeared him to audiences and critics of the highest caliber. He is quite evident that the often wears his heart upon his sleeve and that his art is at once intimate and emotional. These qualities in turn have endeared him to the hearts of so many people.

Walter, at seventy-two, still retains his emotional power. He is unusually a rich musician and, as one leading critic (Virgil Thompson) has said, and his work truly "has breadth and depth and a certain grand sincerity." Walter belongs to another era, an era that had a deep appreciation for the romantics, and his interpretations of these works are rich in that tradition. He is one of the great living interpreters of German music.

The Beethoven cycle has shown his perceptibility and depth of feeling, and those of us who follow radio performances cannot help being grateful that the Philharmonic-Symphony Board urged him to arrange these concerts, terminating as they did on Easter Sunday with the heartfelt performance of the great Ninth Symphony. In presenting the Viennese-born violinist, Erica Morini, in a performance of the Beethoven Violin Concerto, and the English-born pianist Clifford Curzon, in a performance of the Emperor Concerto, Walter provided radio audiences with rich editions of both works that will long remain in memory. This opera revival, with Fritz Reiner conducting, was one of the big successes of the Metropolitan 1948-49 season. Welitsch and Reiner were hailed by critics as an unrivaled team. Preceding "Salome" came Puccini's delightful "Gianni Schicchi," with Italo Tajo in the leading role. Though Strauss's opera, with its orchestral brilliance and dramatic fervor, overpowered Puccini's lyric music, it cannot be denied that the latter was an enjoyable occasion with such talented singers as Tajo, Licia Albanese, and Di Stefano. NEW MINE OF FOLK MUSIC AND LORE

In 1848 the first saw mill was built on the Suquan River in Michigan by a fur trader named Williams. Lumber was in the heart of one of the greatest primeval forests in the new world. From that date on, for over fifty years, lumbering and the State of Michigan were synonymous. Billions of feet of lumber, both pine and hardwood, poured out from its famous territory to help build the rapidly expanding industries and homes of the new country. In one year 1,917,225,000 feet were cut. Thousands of lumbermen engaged in this tremendous work. It was natural that those men, isolated from their families, should develop a literature of verses, and the new volume collected by Earl Clifton Beck immediately becomes a most valuable book of reference to one of the most entertaining examples of the music of the forest and humor. The dialect songs are especially fine in one instance there is a good example that Dr. William H. Drummond took from "The Wreck of the Julie Plante" from a French-Canadian dialect song.

C. P. E. Bach's Essay

"ESSAY ON THE TRUE ART OF PLAYING KEYBOARD INSTRUMENTS." By Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach. Translated and Edited by William J. Mitchell Pages, 149. Price, $6.00. Publisher, W. W. Norton & Company.

William J. Mitchell, who is an Associate Professor of Music at Columbia University, tells us in his preface that this the first complete English translation of one of the most notable of musical books— C. P. E. Bach's "Versuch über die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen." The original edition of Part One was in 1753.

The first impression upon the part of the reader will be to stand in amazement at the immense amount of labor Bach's third son, by his first wife, C. P. E. Bach (1714-1788), spent so upon this, one of the first great pedagogical works upon keyboard instruments. The work is divided into two parts and seven chapters. The first part has to do with fingering, embellishment, and performance and it embodies modern and early service usages. Your Summer Teaching Term

NOW is the time to make plans for your own summer teaching term. Why not offer a special six weeks' course from (say) June 20 to July 30, at the beginning of summer vacation as well as prior to pre-summer? Print on mimeograph a sheet setting your plan and fees, and send it out to your mailing list. Many teachers have found such short courses relieving and very profitable, and parents are only too happy to have their children continue practicing and "taking" during the summer school break.

The notion that young people should discontinue piano study during summer is completely erroneous, for that is just the time when, freed from school duties and activities, they are able to take more lessons, work more concentratedly, practice longer, and really enjoy music.

Include some alluring features that you cannot put in the winter schedule, such as one group a week in early evenings reading and playing four students two pianos. A class in simple improvisation for young children (this will be fine for you, too!), or special group work in sight reading. Mrs. Esther Foster of McAlester, Oklahoma, offers all grades a more informal studio piano party: she gets personal lessons and two miscellaneous class lessons a week, or eighteen lessons in six weeks, paid for on that basis, with no missed lesson made up.

Her first class lesson each week is a small group of four to six students (all classes, too, are forty-five minutes); the second on Saturday mornings brings the entire group together. All beginners are in one group; the others are placed in three loosely graded groups. Mrs. Foster plans each class lesson very carefully in advance, not only for the separate classes, but for the various abilities within the groups. On large white-cardboards are written the students' names of each group, with stars for the week's work. One class begins, with a brief history of a composer's life (or an event), then pieces played from memory (gold star); or with some "mysterious paper", which tells no more than the title of a piece until it has been played; next a group of six pupils are "play pieces" and the performance is rated according to that standard, and so forth. Then, as a special treat or "lucky" reward, pupils are awarded small tokens.

Mrs. Foster also gives small recitals at the end of the term with parents present and serves tea. The pupils are grouped by ability and the teacher plans carefully so that the work of each week is balanced and interesting. Teachers who plan such summer work often find the extra effort pays off handsomely.

One of the upper group's projects requires each pupil to bring to a general lesson a colored card and to make a rhythmical pattern for it in class; this plan is a splendid way of developing imagination in the young students. The children make up short ensemble numbers and the cards are used for rhythm displays.

Many teachers prefer simply to retain a reduced private lesson schedule in the summer and for good reasons. Sometimes I think it is better to keep pupils at home on the small daily lessons, which have proved very effective with younger children. Nevertheless, I feel that every teacher should consider some sort of summer program for the older pupils and the beginning group. It not only helps to keep the pupils practicing, but it also brings new students into the studio and gives the teacher a welcome change of pace.

The Singer and Stage Fright

by Silvia R. Bagley
Associate Professor of Music
Wesley College, University of North Dakota

Caruso said, "My high notes come only when I have le trac"

SILVIA R. BAGLEY

Silvia R. Bagley received her M.Mus. degree at the University of Colorado. She was graduated in voice from The Institute of Musical Art and then studied under two fellowships at the Juilliard Graduate School. Her teachers have included Mme. Marcella Sembrich, Ella Peder? and Estelle Liebling. Miss Bagley has given many recitals and has appeared on radio networks. She has made a special study of stage fright, and gives in this article much practical advice for overcoming the bogey. — EDITOR'S NOTE.

IF STAGE fright is part of your fate, don't try to avoid it. Welcome it, and instead of letting it paralyze you, use its power to help you give a better performance! I do not mean impossible? It isn't that one can always rid oneself completely of nervousness, for even an artist who seems to be entirely free from it probably has a little of it left; but one can learn to control and use it. The singer who allows himself to be mastered by stage fright loses control of his varying emotions and the result is not only a poor performance but, sometimes, injury to the voice. The nervous singer may find that the voice is strangled, or that certain weak spots show up and grow worse.

I wish that those who write about the stage fright of the famous would remember to stress one important point; namely, that the average solo artist of today who has stage fright has had stage fright — not because of any lack of talent — but because he or she is the sort of person who gives of himself, who takes risks, who cares. The so-called great singer who is never afraid is as rare as the man who is never sick. To the extent that the singer feels and cares, he will be subject to apprehensions, but these may be turned to account; they may be converted into a finer, more spontaneous feeling in the performance.

Stage Fright Defined

But first, what is stage fright? It is simply quickened heart action due to anxiety. In fact, doctors classify it as one of the "nervous" neuroses. "Why," you ask, "should I be anxious about something? I have practiced and can do well? Isn't the song I rehearse at home the same song I sing at the concert?" It may seem absurd to say, but it is the same song. The song thrilled in your studio is in an enjoyable exercise; the same song before an audience becomes a potential source of humiliation, therefore suffering, for you. Your subconscious mind is keenly aware of the difference, however much you try to forget it.

So, you have stage fright, and certain well-meaning friends may advise you to "control yourself," to "try not to get nervous," to "forget the audience and pretend you're singing at home." Such advice not only is useless as a truly frightened person; it is also downright harmful. It aims to suppress something which is no more suppressible than the heart-beats; and when suppression becomes the only means of attempting to control, the nerve cells are held desperately in check; and the effect in a concert may be outright disaster. Remember — there are plenty of people without anxiety!

Our College Voice Department has long followed a definite, but not too obvious, program to develop an easy freedom in those who sing or who teach. We have ways for curing the badly frightened, to enable the timid singer to become a healthy, spontaneous, person who will enjoy the emotional excitement which is the artist's friend.

In our belief that students make advances musically who are well trained in their art but who are frightened as singers their skill is only half developed. One psychological point here is the effect upon the nervous system, which if exhausted, will bring on failure after failure until confidence goes. In the teaching of the past much has been based upon the method of making the pupil practise alone, and when the performance time arrived suddenly the singer found out for himself what the reaction would be. The knowledge of the past does much to build up the confidence of the singer and to give him an idea of what to expect.

The Difficult Pupil

Sometimes — and this touches on Point Two — even good teachers cannot succeed with certain pupils. The trouble is not in the pupil but sometimes lies away in early training. Certain types of pupil are certain to develop a tendency toward stage fright — they are the "high-strung" nervous ones. When such a pupil reaches adolescence the trouble usually becomes pronounced. In such cases the teacher must be able to handle the pupil tactfully and gradually to strengthen the will-power and the self-confidence by patient, sustained training. Above all, do not use harsh criticism or disparaging remarks; these will only add fuel to the nervous fire.

A Severe Treatment

Of course, every studio has some who cannot be helped in the long run; emotionally "sick" singers are not easily made over. There are occasionally pupils who may be classed as "nerve-cases" and while they may be given certain exercises and a course of careful training they will never become the sort of performer one hopes for. In the case of those who can be helped, however, a few modest rules, properly carried out, will work wonders. The teacher must avoid the use of scare tactics which only increase the pupil's sense of insecurity. He must help the pupil to meet the situation by degrees, gradually enlarging the field of action until confidence grows.

Summer Organ Study
by Alexander McCurdy, Mus. Doc.

In our department of the May 1947 ETUDE, we had an article on "Summer Courses for Organists," and from the weight of the mail bag it seemed that there was considerable interest on the part of our readers in Summer Schools. This year, from all reports, there will be even more opportunities for summer study. This is a healthy sign for I hear too much time is wasted during the summer holidays, if a brush-up on choir work and vocal technique is desired, there are short courses in New York, Princeton, [San?] or Westminster Seminary; and in March, the Berkshire Center and Redlands offer short courses. There are other schools and courses which many are not familiar with, for instance: the Union Theological Seminary, the Peabody Conservatory; Tufts College, and many others. Actually, about the only place we have found was the organ department of the Curtis Institute, which for many years has given a series of summer organ courses, organists from all over America have been familiar with it. I am sure, as gratification that the children of such a reputation as the Curtis Institute will be familiar with it. The staff of the Curtis School is a worthy group of teachers including Harold Friedell, Dr. [Samuel?] Prowse, and many others.

I shall, in general, try to make some suggestions which may be helpful to those who expect to take a summer organ course. If a prospective student can spend from two to six weeks in a summer course, he can accomplish definite and valuable musical improvement. The one thing to decide is what you want to do. If you want to catch up with the theory and organ literature you may take courses in organ literature and organ methods. If you want to concentrate on technique and registration, you may take private lessons in technique and organ registration. If you want to work on choral training and accompanying, you may do that. In some cases the organist should also work on his piano playing. A summer course is an excellent opportunity for the developing organist to enlarge his repertoire of literature and improve his overall musicianship.

The Methods recommended are as follows:
Bach—Volume containing Preludes and Fugues, Chorale Preludes, and eleven booklets.
Buxtehude—Organ works.
Bach—The Great Eight or other collections.
Bach—Trio Sonatas I, II, V; The Schubler Chorales; The Great Eight; Toccatas and Fugues in C, F, D minor & Dorian; Preludes and Fugues in minor & major.
Franck—Grande Piece Symphonique, Prélude, Fugue & Variation; Pièce héroïque.
Mozart—Sonatas for Organ and Strings.
Handel—Concerto for Organ & Strings.
Brahms—Chorale Preludes.
Brahms—Chorale Preludes.
Dupre—Three Preludes and Fugues.
Tournemire—Suites No. II and No. III, Nos. 28.
Messiaen—La Nativité du Seigneur; Le Banquet Céleste; Apparition de L'Eglise éternelle; The Ascension Suite.
Karg-Elert—Sonata.
Milhaud—Nine Preludes.
Elgar—Prelude and Fugue.

One can see at a glance that this is a repertoire to lead all organists. I am sure that a student brought along for "organ-habilitation," a Hindemith Sonata, or the Sowerby Symphony, the members of the faculty will welcome him with open arms. It seems to me that in the average organist's list of works in this repertoire there is such a place that he could plug in many well-known organ works. The student should keep in mind the great advantages of organ summer schools. The concentrated work with a competent teacher can lay the technical and musical foundation of real progress. Many good opportunities are available for those who want to study in the summer and get a real "tune-up" in organ technique.

Strive to decide what is most important to you. If you need to build technique, choose teachers who emphasize the technical aspect of organ playing. If you need to build repertoire, choose teachers who can advise on good program material. If you need more performance experience, sign up for recitals and master classes. The student should also take advantage of the various choral and ensemble offerings available in many of the schools. The experience of accompanying, conducting small ensembles, and playing for choral rehearsals will be invaluable.

Study is always more effective if planned. For Summers are short and the time must be made to count. Plan your course to include daily practice, private lessons, theory classes, and opportunities for public performance and accompanying. Remember that the organ is essentially a practical instrument. The student's greatest asset is the amount of healthful playing which will keep the muscles and the mind quick. Do not waste the summer; a little planned effort can achieve lasting benefits.

Think, if you will, how important it is for him to have his technique on a high level. Practicing daily for four or five hours is desirable, but for most students this is impossible. If you can practice two or three hours a day you will increase very much your playing ability. Build a program for the summer that will give you steady, sound progress.

Listening to Learn
It has been my experience that if a student can hear something played a few times, he becomes much more receptive to the idea. Students who come to me for summer study often seem to have an appetite for some given piece; he may become much more quickly. Very often, too, a teacher who plays a piece once, can point out certain important details and can correct many mistakes.

We are always looking for better methods to teach organ. If a student is exposed to it as he is in the school, he surely will have all the material he can ever need.

When one thinks of the ideas that must be floating around Methodists in July and August concerning the organ, one thinks of the teachers, and what is the most important thing that will give the student the largest repertoire. A good guideline is: listen, practice, and perform. It is the best way to achieve lasting results.

While a great many schools do have proper facilities conducive to study, they do not have a sufficient number of organs for practice, nor the required number of organ teachers. The organist in a private family home, or perhaps three is not a variety that gives full opportunity to explore the many aspects of the organ. However, there are summer schools which have many wonderful instruments for the student to play. Another advantage of the summer schools is the number of students who are thrown together from different parts of the country. One of the great pleasures of summer study is the making of new friends. For the student, the social and musical life is very important.

Cherish Student Friendships
The best part of the love of friendship one makes in summer study is sometimes the most lasting. The fellowship with others who share the same serious interest often yields lasting benefits. It is well worth the time and effort.

While we should encourage ourselves to spend a few weeks at a summer organ school, the main thing is to study with a purpose. Plan your time carefully, and try to get the maximum benefit from a short stay away from duties of home and work. The student who plans his summer weeks in advance and works steadily will have the best returns.

The School Orchestra

An Approach to General Music Education
by William E. Knuth, Mus. Doc.

Chairman, Division of Creative Arts
San Francisco State College

IN planning a functional place for the school orchestra in the general education program of the average school, one of the most important things is to get a clear picture of the goal. Two pitfalls immediately appear which must be avoided. On the one hand, we must not be so optimistic as to paint castles in the air which only the gifted few could ever realize, and leave the great mass of average students destined to discover the cold reality of a fool's paradise. On the other hand, we must not follow a course which would result in a few scattered knowledge, some very limited appreciations, and superficial techniques developed with all the flashy superficial features of the march-and-show type of band and orchestra.

It is expedient that we define the term "average school" and its program. What might be a daily occurrence in one school might not happen at all in another school. Many variations of schedule, instrument selection, student background, and administrative organization will exist among schools from different communities. The "average" school is a section of statistics. For our purpose, we will assume the average program of music education as a kind of normal standard; one of which will be surpassed in many schools by more highly selected talent, greater financial support, and more favorable status of music in the family life of the time academic setting. However, in many other schools this average program will not be reached—and for a wide variety of reasons.

The term "general music education" should be considered. General music education encompasses a breadth of basic music experience in listening, creating, and performing with appreciable development of perception. Its basic activities are attracted to the life well being of all students, but which do not necessarily prepare them for any kind of vocational training. The significance of general music education resides in its emphasis on experiences that promote insight into music, create the desire to think of things to do with music, and actually enrich fulfillment in doing them with a growing sensitivity to musical values. While general music education might be placed in a position opposite the specialist, the music training in its various vocational aspects, it should not ignore the implementation of such specialized training; nor should this specialized concentration on some aspect of music be carried on without reference to the place of general music education. There is a need for balance and integration between the specialization and general music emphasis.

A Basic Pattern

The trend towards a greater emphasis on general education in the overall curriculum of our schools implies a basic pattern of broad educational experiences, each developed in relation to the other and administered with flexibility to provide for individual differences of boys and girls. They should be given a more active and responsible role in co-operative planning and an opportunity to carry out this program for their own growth and development in the understanding, appreciation, and competence.

It is immediately apparent that the average school situation with its program is a dangerous one in its unique in terms of its community, its homes, its children, and its teachers. The development of any successful program requires the meeting of minds and spirits in an cooperative endeavor constantly blended with good-will and mutual respect. Most certainly, the team work of a faculty is the prerequisite to the unfolding growth of such an educational program. Just as the point of view of general education is applied to the traditional school subjects, so also is it applied to the teaching of music. Too many teachers forget that music is simply a necessary aspect of living, here and now, and is not actually a practice room ritual of the life of tomorrow as an intense devotion to the technique of sound and an exacting discipline in the techniques of music.

Thus, the school orchestra seeks to find its place with the other specialized music activities in the general education program of the school and in the general music program of the school department. The wise and forward looking instructor of school orchestra will study his responsibilities and interpret his contribution to the total school program in terms of broad cultural studies and activities. One certain implication in current educational thinking is that all specializations will become the subject of inquiry and justification. General educators and administrators who in the past have been cold on the practicality value and magic of specialized activities such as orchestra and choir, are beginning to ask how much time, money, and credit recognition should be given to these activities. After all, these specialized activities have a direct bearing on the individual needs of a relative small percentage of the total student body.

Growing out of this participation in a specialized activity are questions of even greater importance. How much of a student's time can be justified in specialized orchestra training in terms of his personal needs and the vocational opportunities a justification of this emphasis, and should he make such a choice under these circumstances? If not, can the specialization be justified in terms of the broad cultural development of the student? How often do we request a student's consent in orchestra class year or his school life based on orchestra needs rather than student needs?

Philosophy of Music Education

It is axiomatic that what one teaches (content) and how one teaches (method) depend upon one's philosophy (aim). A very strong case can be made out for the claim that the most important thing about one's educator is rather the philosophy of the music educator. Just as the most important thing about any person is his philosophy of life. Let us see an illustration in another professional field. If the medical faculty thinks that the most important aim of a medical school is to teach doctors how to save as much money as possible in so short a time as possible, then the content of the medical school course will depend and the methods of teaching would take one direction. If, on the other hand, the medical faculty believes that their most important job is to teach young doctors to go out and serve humanity, regardless of monetary reward, the content and methods of the medical school will take a somewhat different direction.

Likewise, it is true that the development of a school orchestra program is determined by the philosophy of its conductor-teacher. Unfortunately, while every conductor-teacher has a philosophy of music education, a sense of values, and a set of ideas most of them would sit in helpless dismay if they were given ten minutes to write these down on a sheet of paper. Too few of us have ever thought out our conducting and teaching philosophy, or organized it into clear simple terms. Because of this vagueness, we are apt to say we have no philosophy. This is, of course, far-fetched, for the music teacher without an educational philosophy could never decide what to teach or how to teach. Any conductor who desires to have his orchestra play this instead of that, and to use this method instead of that one, has a philosophy of music education.

It is likewise unfortunate that if every school orchestra conductor were to write up on a full account of his philosophy, about ninety-nine out of a hundred would not be amazed at all the contradictions and astounding at the indefensible theories they have been following. It is suggested that the reader pause here and resolve to take time in the immediate future to write out his own personal philosophy of the school orchestra program. Just what are its aims?

A Process of Development

Every teacher, whether a college professor, nursery school teacher, ex-perf, or other calling his son in the rural, a fraternity brother coaching an initiate in the ritual, must make up his mind whether he is teaching human beings or whether he is teaching skills or knowledge. And so it is with the conductor-scholar of the school orchestra; he must determine whether his teaching pupils is of a higher level than the music lessons he must give. Teaching which helps the pupil to be inspired to want to play, to think musically, and to have the capacity to listen and to interpret music is far more important than the development of mere mechanical habits. The teacher who ignores this is guilty of a crime against the youth of his community.

BEFORE proceeding to the main theme of this article I would like to present a few remarks regarding the care of bassoon reeds. Long life of reeds is important for other than financial reasons. Changing reeds on a bassoon corresponds to changing mouthpieces on a clarinet. Each reed having its own peculiarities necessitates major or minor embouchure adjustments on the part of the performer, in order that he may produce satisfactory results. The ideal situation would be that whereby the student would be able to use one excellent reed throughout his playing career. This is impossible because of the expense, the nature of the reed; however, reed life may be greatly increased by the application of a few simple practices.

The first step in reed care is to acquire a good substantial reed box or case, which should be standard equipment with every school bassoon. Second, impress upon the student how fragile the reeds actually are; also how expensive they are to replace. Third, always remove the reed from the "stock" when not playing. Do not permit the student to walk around with the reed on the "bocal" — it is an open invitation to a reed spitting accident. Fourth, keep the reed in a reed case of quality and keep it in a moistened corner of a clean handkerchief; the inside should be tucked out under part running water. If there is a heavy deposit inside of the reed, it may be necessary to carefully run a small feather or pipe cleaner through the reed from the butt toward the tip. This last process should not be done with any other than necessary. Needless to say, food, cans, nuts, and lipstick do not contribute to long reed life. Last but not least, remember that a "free reed" represents a far greater monetary investment than its individual cost. Recalling the five or six unusable cents that must be discarded before you find a good reed, the total cost runs into money with a capital M.

the same throughout the entire register of the bassoon.
It has all the warmth and vibrancy of a singing voice,
plus the added color of the double reed. This is what
you, as a listener, hear—this is the listener's con-
ception.

To the fine performers, however, there is an en-
tirely different conception. They have learned that the
important thing is not how they sound to themselves,
but rather, how they sound to others. With this in
mind they "play to please you." To do this they must
allow a "ready" edge to be present in their playing,
which is both felt and heard by the performers at all
times. It is this edge which adds life and carrying
power to what would otherwise be a dead, uninter-
esting sound. There must be, in addition to this edge,
a core of resonance which expands and fills, leaves
the instrument to envelop the "edge" and travel along
it much in the same manner as a radio broadcasts
travels along a carrier wave. (See Illustration No. 4).

A good bassoon tone is one which has the proper
proportions of both "edge" and "resonance," to satisfy
the tonal conception of both performer and listener.

The student, hearing a fine bassoonist over the radio,
or in a concert hall, says, "Gee, I wish I sounded like
that," then he rushes home and tries to do so. He works
with the reed and embouchure until all vestiges of
"edge" disappear and the tone becomes "big, round,
and full." If he could stand off ten or fifteen feet and
listen to himself, he would be sadly disillusioned, for
he would sound no more like his correct listener's
conception than he did before his efforts; perhaps
even less. We, as teachers, must be his listener's and
help him to originate his performer's conception.

The method I use for this orientation is to have
him produce a marked reedy tone at first, then work
back from that by cushioning the reed with the lower
lip. The point is to use him is where the edge is no
longer evident for the listener when standing in the
audience, and for the player who has grown to like
some of these sounds from his standpoint cannot hear
the difference. The method will reveal, rather a little
too much "edge" in a new student's tone. It is well,
therefore, that the teacher examine the student's tone
and find the best way to find the proper balance be-
tween "edge" and "resonance" or "body."

To develop this conception, work mainly between
Low G and Open F. This range is the heart and soul
of the bassoon. Until the student can produce a true
bassoon tone and accurate intonation in this range,
it is useless to proceed to the higher registers, as all
tonal defects we have formed on the bassoon in early
practice stages will be carried on into the adjacent
register, and then to the high notes. Usually Low C is a
good resonant note from which to start; work with it
until some control of these fundamentals is achieved.
When satisfied with Low G, play it again and slur into the adjacent A, trying to

match the two; repeat this until the minor embou-
chure and breath adjustments needed are found and
mastered. Move up the basic register by seconds each
time returning to Low G and passing through the
intermediate notes until the whole basic register has
been mastered as to equal resonance, quality, and
relative pitch. (See Illustration No. 5) Do not be per-
turbed if at first the student plays on the flat side.
He will learn to compensate for the greater relaxa-
tion of embouchure by an increase in breath intensity.
Balancing this basic register is a little more difficult
than it sounds, because of the inherent differences
in tonal characteristics, even between adjacent notes.
It is up to the performer to overcome the deficiency
of his instrument and produce an even basic register.
Only after this has been accomplished should the
student be allowed to go "over the break."

Going "over the break" means to use breath and to
utilize the first series of overtone produced by the
basic register. The easiest approach is to return again
to Low G, playing with the greatest possible reso-
nance, and then by "half-holding" with the first finger
with the left hand, a slur to the octave will be pro-
duced with a minimum of lip effort. Repeat that until
the Octave G has the same basic quality and reso-

used on the upper, because the fundamental reed

The Essentials of Teaching

by Harold Berkley

WHATEVER his subject, it is well for the
teacher periodically to take stock of himself
and his approach to his work, to examine
and analyze anew the functions and the responsibil-
ities of a teacher, and to ponder what is implied by
the verb "To Teach."

Such searching are especially important for the
teacher of music, for he has to deal with a subject
most closely associated with the very nature and
development of his pupils, a subject which, if prop-
erly taught, can have a profoundly beneficial influ-
ence on the character and spiritual development of
those who study it. In order that this influence may
be effective, the music teacher should possess much
understanding of cultural expression in fields but
incidentally connected with musical literature.
Painting, History, to name a few—and he should be
able to coordinate these subjects with the teaching of
music. Above all, it is necessary for him to remem-
ber always that teaching is education, and that the
word "education" comes ultimately from a Latin
word meaning "to lead or draw out."

The teacher may well be considered the most in-
dispensable member of the music profession. The
Genius, the great Artist, is the inspirer, but it is the
Teacher, more than any other, who is responsible for
the continuity of the great line in his art.

If he is to fulfill the responsibilities that are laid
upon him, the Teacher cannot look upon his profes-
sion merely as a means of existence; he should regard
it rather as an honor for, as a creator of the fine arts,
and should think of himself as a creator. For he has
in his hands the power of forming taste and enrich-
ing the character of those whom he instructs; he must
not think of teaching as merely the imparting of
technical information, but as the cultivation of the
whole personality.

A Great Privilege

To create a love for music in eager and receptive
young minds—and, through music, a keener and more
sympathetic appreciation of all beauty, even of those
subtle beauties which surround us at all times and
which many people pass by unobserved—this is diffi-
cult to imagine any activity that would better justify
one's existence on earth. The Greeks knew the value
of beauty in daily life, but that awareness has been
largely lost in the more materialistic ages which
followed. It is the privilege of the Teacher, by his
actions and influence, to bring this awareness back
into the lives of his pupils.

That this influence may be potent, he must be a
leader, more than a mere instructor to his pupils; he
must be a friend to them, an older friend, with whom
they will consult concerning personal and other trou-
bles. To teach is to guide. The teacher must always
remember that the aim of his task is to lead his pupils
toward more complete and satisfying musical achieve-
ments.

A great teacher will not condemn the pupil merely
because some conception of interpretation which the
pupil adopts does not tally with his own, is no reason
for condemning it. If it is not foreign to the spirit and
style of the music, he should encourage the student
with words of praise. If, however, immature judgment
has resulted in poor taste, the teacher should endeav-
or to point out how this fault may be remedied with-
out sacrificing the over-all individuality of the inter-
pretation.

The First Essential

Where violin students are concerned, it goes with-
out saying that the first essential is good intonation.
But the teacher must be content merely to say,
"That F-sharp must be higher," or "That B-flat is
not low enough." He will explain to the pupil why,
in the key of G minor, for example—the F-sharp
must be high and the B-flat low. In other words, a
technical mistake should rarely be treated purely as
such; its bearing on the ultimate musical result should
always be kept clear in minds. This approach will
not hold, of course, in those cases where technical
errors are many, and the hopes of pleasing musical
results are dim. In such cases, the desire for techni-
cal accuracy must be insisted upon from the pupil's
mind as the one and only road to success.

The long-range aim of teaching should be to train
students that in the course of time they may become
their own teachers. For too many talented students
are completely lost when they leave formal study and
must depend on themselves. The task for the teacher
is the student; in nine cases out of ten the teacher is
to blame—he has not taught the student to think for
himself. A pupil must be made to obey orders, told
to do this or that "because it is right", without hav-
ing it explained to him at length or having it done
for him. The pupil must be taught to think, to test,
and to appraise what he is doing; it is generally that
from the earliest stages he be given the habit of see-
ing clearly every point carefully made clear to him,
both in its musical and in its technical aspects.

There are many pupils who conscientiously think
they must be passive and receptive, and like funnels,
they swallow without question all that is presented to
them. Such pupils are very easy to teach, but nothing
really good can come from them because of lack of
initiative and original thinking. The teacher should,
as far as possible, train pupils to think for themselves,
not merely to obey. This does not mean lack of disci-
pline; quite the reverse. It is the teacher's function to
help the pupil think out his own problems and to use
his judgment.

The Student Personality

To train each pupil to use his own brain—that is
perhaps the chief secret of good teaching. It is the
synthesis of the "giving orders" method and the
"explanatory" method. The ideal teacher combines
the two, giving definite directions when necessary and
at the same time encouraging the pupil to reason out
the artistic and technical problems for himself. Indi-
viduality—the pupil who is to be taught to reason
for himself—should be cherished and developed by
every teacher. Too much mechanical teaching kills in-
dependence and initiative.

Young students instinctively know what good prac-
tice really is, and when they do good work, it gen-
erally follows that they have had a good teacher,
one who has taught them to think and to apply
themselves. It is an essential duty of the teacher to
provide his pupils with the necessary mental tools to
practice and to think for themselves. The pupil who is
deprived of this training will be a mere imitator when
he leaves his teacher—an imitation which will not
stand the test of time.

Preparing for Opera

A Conference with

Polyna Stoska

Distinguished American Soprano
A Leading Artist, Metropolitan Opera Association

by Stephen West

Born in Worcester, Massachusetts, of Lithuanian background, Polyna Stoska has inherited marked artistic abilities from both sides of her family. Far back, her father's people were musicians. Her mother is a gifted designer of clothes. Untrained and self-taught for many years, Mrs. Stoska created and made all her daughter's concert gowns. Most people will remember this Miss Stoska's stage career as a "soubrette." One of the most beautiful and glamorous figures on the concert stage, Polyna Stoska is exceedingly musical, she began vocal study at the age of seven; but as long as she can remember she has sung, humming tunes around the house and following the records of famous artists. She played in her high school orchestra and then joined the glee club, and when she was sixteen, her voice attained its full strength. Later, she went to Boston and studied at the New England Conservatory, and after school she went abroad to further her study with several noted teachers. She studied in Berlin and in Vienna and it was while in Austria that she made a decided step forward in her dramatic interpretations. Her first public appearance in New York was as a recitalist and soon afterwards she made her debut with an opera company. Her first assignment with the Metropolitan was a small role in a new production, and she sang many small parts until, by constant work and application, she became an important and leading member of the company. Miss Stoska's voice is of unusual quality, well produced, and capable of great expression.

Every young singer who is interested in opera dreams of one day entering the Metropolitan. Rankling as the foremost operatic organization in the world today, the "Met" is the inspiration and the dream of every young artist. The first steps in the direction of the Met are the same as those for any professional career. One must have talent, yes, but talent without training and continued work is only raw material. The young singer must rounded the training of both voice and mind. One must study languages, diction, dramatic expression and the art of concert and oratorio singing. Success depends not only on a fine voice but on a cultivated taste and an intelligent mind. It is no longer enough to have a good voice and a pretty face. The Metropolitan is not content merely to engage charming personalities; the company requires artists who are musicians, actors and truly cultivated performers.

Many Different Skills

The beginner should realize that vocal training, important as it is, is far from the only requirement of stage success. Languages, stage deportment, dramatic instinct, style of singing and — most important — experience in public performance. The rubbing off of those corners is not the result of one appearance! Actually, one needs practice in performance just as one needs to practice an instrument. We all know the feeling of taking up a new work and trying to look fresh at it instantly, trying simply to get the notes right. Only after a number of study and practice do those notes begin to merge into a unified pattern of phrasing, of meaning—and then the work seems entirely different from what it did at the start. Exactly the same is true of a role in performance. No matter how well you have mastered it under your teacher, that role is nothing but isolated notes and gestures until you have clarified its pattern in many performances. That, precisely, is why the experienced prima donna brings so much more out of a role than the most studious newcomer can hope to do. Hence, the best preparation for opera is the acceptance of the small and constant working position in an opera company. Fortunately, we have American companies today!

Opportunity Knocks

And yet, the best plans sometimes go astray—sometimes with happy results! The young singer whose experience was limited to concert work failed to make an impression in some of the small companies in which she auditioned; but she was so well cast in one particular role that, after a few performances, she suddenly found herself in demand by the big houses. Such things happen in the most unexpected ways. The important point is to be ready—always—when opportunity appears. I remember one young singer who had little stage experience, but who had studied long and hard, who finally won a place in a small opera company and who, by sheer determination, was able to force recognition. She studied, she rehearsed, she filled in, and before many seasons had passed she was singing leading roles. Such careers are not built overnight; they are the outcome of years of preparation and patient work.

To be able to turn out a convincing performance night after night, to be ready to step into a larger part at a moment's notice, to know exactly how to cope with the demands of the rehearsal — these are the qualities that managers value most. The artist who lacks such qualities may have a beautiful voice, but will find it hard to maintain a steady career.

The High Cost of Living
Even dear old Bach was just as plagued by it as you and I! Here follows an excerpt from one of the few surviving letters written in his own hand. In 1730 Bach found that his position as Cantor of St. Thomas' Church in Leipzig was not a bed of roses. He could no longer tolerate the unpleasantries and bickering of the powers that ruled the church. He decided to look for a job elsewhere, and wrote his friend Erdmann, who lived in Danzig as Russian agent. (The original letter is in the Russian archives in Moscow.)

"I have discovered that this situation is not as good as it was represented to me, that living is expensive, and that my masters here are strange folk who care very little for music. I am subjected to constant annoyance, jealousy, and persecution. It is therefore in my mind, with God's assistance, to seek my future elsewhere. If you know of any good position in your city I beg you to give me your valuable recommendation. I promise on my part to give satisfaction, show diligence, and justify your esteemed support.

"My present post is worth about 700 crowns a year, my income being derived mainly from extra service, such as fees, weddings, and funerals. If the death-rate is higher than usual, my revenue increases in proportion; but Leipzig is a healthy place and for the past year I have received about 100 crowns less than usual for funerals. The cost of living, too, is so excessive that I was better off in Thuringia on 400 crowns a year.
"May I add that I can arrange to give vocal or instrumental concerts solely for the members of my own family. All my children are born musicians; my wife has a very clear soprano, and my eldest daughter can give a good account of herself too."

Poor Johann Sebastian! Despite his unparalleled qualifications, he couldn't find another job.
Next year, 1950, will find artists, orchestras, choruses, teachers everywhere observing the two hundredth anniversary of Bach's death. Make your own plans for it now.

Preparation for Opera
(Continued from Page 300)

other is never to be jealous!
The young singer needs to realize the immense importance of studying a part dramatically as well as vocally. Many cannot be too much stressed. Many young singers come to me who seem to sing as if it were a matter of vocal mechanism and then, afterwards, to add a few gestures and motions. Nothing could be more dangerous! Actual vocal and dramatic study should begin early and progress together—neither comes first and neither is more important. Indeed, if there were a shade of greater importance, I think the dramatic side would be first. I say this because I finally believe that of the two the musical part should be worked out from character. The person you have to portray should be clearly fixed in your mind before you attempt to create her, either through voice or gesture. In Berlin we spent much time comparing characters according to their basic types. Elisabeth in Tannhäuser, for instance, is very different in person from Sieglinde. Sieglinde is the amazonnian figure, of large than - mortal thoughts and habits. Elisabeth is the medieval gentlewoman, restrained, controlled, Gothic in her gestures. Both parts have to be sung, and both stem from the creative power of the same composer—but how different the personae! This must be made clear by their every least action—the way they hold their heads, the way they move their shoulders, their hands and feet, the way they glide about the stage. Being able to explain the difference in each character is as much a part of operatic preparation as being able to sing the aria!
As to actual singing, the young singer should learn to do too much warming up on the day of a performance—any performance. The professional soon learns to use (and the art life) the voice as little as possible on a singing day. My habit is to get my voice warmed and then to go to bed and rest mind, body, and voice. Then, at the opera house, an hour before I go on, I generally spend no more than ten minutes on scales and sustained notes. That's all! The voice after those ten minutes is to get the value of warm, and of exercise the muscles of the singing apparatus—I call it getting the diaphragm active. That is all one needs. If the voice is correctly used in the first place, ten minutes of warming up should be enough to assure a smooth performance.
Vocal needs and problems are too individual to attempt to sum up loosely and discuss methods or exercises. I may say that for myself, I have the greatest faith in scales, studies and more scales. But what I wish to emphasize here is that the best vocal work, by itself, will never be an operatic career. That requires additional skills (calculated not merely to please the ear (with vocal tone) but to transport the whole being of the spectator. Thus, the best singer, the young singer who intends to be one of the future stars, must learn how to act and not merely to sing. On the stage there must be a genuine and completely intelligent preparation for opera.

The Singer and Stage Fright
(Continued from Page 291)

No secure can last forever; given time, it finally wears off, leaving its victim exhausted and free for a long period.

There are several ways to evoke premature stage fright; one of the best is to sit down, quietly, sometime before the concert and call to mind all the reasons why one might be afraid. Perhaps some critical or valued person is coming. There is one great cause for an over-wish to do well; this is resulting nervousness. Perhaps the singer fears “breakdowns,” with their aftermath of humiliation. To a young artist, the very look of a well-filled, brightly-lit auditorium means that so much will be expected that they cannot possibly measure up. There are innumerable devices, half-plausible, or even acknowledged reasons feeding the fever of fright. The best thing to do is to recall them as searchingly and completely as possible; hold them up, full-view, before the trembling self and let these thoughts make their full round for as long as they will. If, when all is said and done, the “real” mind and body will go on, then overcome fear, with the whole brain. The former will be the first to feel “whatever happens tonight is bound to happen and I cannot worry about it or stop it. I have made as careful preparation as I know how; the rest isn’t up to me but to fate.” (Luck, or the thinker is pious.) In the hands of God.

Not everyone can evoke premature stage fright at first try; for some it takes the repeated effort. Others never do it with that end in view. A teacher or cooperative friend: “But, however you accomplish it, the first emotional catharsis” if you are troubled with severe, disabling fright. It is the hardest, but it never fails to work. One of its best features is the fact that you will never need to undergo it more than once or twice. Fear of fear is a stage fright after all. Once you know of a sure way of relief, you are cured, from that moment. Often one hears young singers say, after a performance, “How I wish I could go back in there and do it over again!” I’m not a bit scared now. Now I could really show them what I can do.” What a pity to feel this way, after the proper time for “showing them” has passed! In our experiments with “sensational catharsis” we found that those who underwent it never felt in a mood for “showing” an audience before the show! How thrilling it becomes when we can approach the business of public singing, not with the thought, “There is an ordeal. Will I survive it?” but with the joyous anticipation, “There is an opportunity for me to create something beautiful.”

Tried and Tested Procedures

A number of interesting “highlights” are contributed by other teachers, singers, and workers who found ways to defeat stage fright. A tenor once said, “I have this stage fright business almost like a physical thing. It used to make me have enough breath, and I surely enjoy singing for people, but I still cannot keep my mind from getting dry. That’s the only thing I fear.” For his cure he was told to buy a baby-size rubber ear-syringe, fill it with cold water, and carry it in his handkerchief whenever he sang in public. When an audience saw him presumably wiping his face, our tenor was actually getting a drink by slyly pressing the bulb. Of course, there are other ways to fight those dry throat and failure of voice, but the method with the syringe is the handkerchief and the knowledge that the hand mustn’t be seen caused the voice to find its freedom. One teacher I know feels that the most distressing symptom of stage fright is the stiffening of the face. “When the ‘masque’ grows rigid, says she, the voice becomes pinched and colorless, I cannot move my features; it grows over more sacred.” So, to combat this, she tells her pupils to undergo a short orgy of wrinkling their noses, gargling back their lips, and generally manipulating their faces, just before stepping out to face the audience. It does help! The young folks, less publicly concentrating on keeping the “loose masque” partially forget their fears, and soon, hearing their own voices come out as colorfully as usual, they feel encouraged and really start to sing. There are others.

Still another teacher advises that it is helpful to imitate outwardly the poise and “graces” of the experienced, while achieving such poise oneself. She exhorts her pupils to watch (as well as listen to) the great singers of our times. Then they are to go home and practice making a “grand entrance” and “a regal exit”; to hold the grand manner on an imaginary stage, which anyone can pretend to have in her own bedroom. She also recommends that pupils practice their set of words (not sung, but softly spoken) in a mirror, while making the face expressive and each word, somewhat as a movie actress would do in a closeup.

An old friend, who had been a fine concert singer, told how she had finally found freedom from this stage fright through four religious faiths. “It was a scared to death,” she says, “until the day I realized that it is not I who will finally decide how great a success I am to be in this world. I am only in charge of the preparation, which I do as completely as I can. But once I set foot on a stage, I let ‘go’ completely, knowing that no matter what I do, I am in the Hands of a greater Stage Director.” This artist had the beginnings of a concept which all over-zealous people should consider.

In conclusion: If you have stage fright, you owe yourself the experience of overcoming it. To conquer the emotion will give you a feeling of accomplishment, self-discipline and power, that no lesser victory could bestow. Perhaps you only want to sing solos in a country church, or take part in the high school operetta. Whatever you do, cultivate your talents to their fullest, but add to them the skill and exhilaration of enjoying your performance and of knowing how to “hold” an audience in the palm of your hand. In all the world there is no other feeling like it.

VOICE QUESTIONS
Answered by DR. NICHOLAS DOUTY

Once More the Young Contralto
Q. I am fifteen and have two and one-half years of age, and will be a Junior in high school next term. I am studying singing and my teacher says that I have an unusually mature voice for my age. However, I have heard that no singer should start lesson before seventeen. Is this true? I sing better before and after, but the rest is unusual. I shall probably become piano, do you think I should continue to college. Do you think I should continue singing music or pursue some career more suitable to a person of my circumstances? My range is from B-flat below Middle C to High C. Is this a nice range for a person of my age? I also study piano. Do you think I should discontinue either piano or voice? Do you think both are too much? Which do I start to sing between? I should like, if possible, to have more training in voice. I should make singing my main aim. I am under the impression you would like to answer.

—L.D.

A. 1. If you are physically and mentally mature for your age, you should not start your singing lessons immediately.

2. You are not too young without nervousness before an audition is a distinct advantage. Cultivate it.

3. This is a question that should be answered by your parents, your friends, and your teacher. You know your possibilities the best.

4. Your range is good, provided your lows are good musical quality and well under control.

5. If you are strong enough to take piano and singing lessons and still keep up your school schedule, well and good. Do not attempt too much that your health will be impaired.

6. Without hearing you play and sing we could not summon up the courage to answer this question.

7. Have a thorough examination of your vocal cords, lungs, etc. as a check on anything that is a severe handicap to the singer’s career.

A French-Canadian Baritone. Shall He Study the Usual Repertoire?
Q. Last summer I had an interview with a talent scout. He said that if I had the goods, and that I would be accepted, but I was unable to say in New York and the matter dropped. I found a teacher in Canada, a very well known man, who insists that I should become a Wagnerian baritone, that I am not of the lyric type, however, that I am more of the Tibbett type, especially since I read the article of Mr. Friedrich Schorr in a magazine recently. Only the artist who understands the background of his own art should sing Wagner’s pathetic role and dramatic interpretation of his characters, will be able to interpret the Wagner roles. Mr. Schorr ... What can a person do who is confronted with this conflict? Is the character of baritone the man or is he a baritone? Is there a way to study certain roles? Or is it a case in which the baritone voice should be studied and developed? It is not necessary to decide at once, the matter should be studied carefully by a qualified teacher. Also, remember that your voice will change with time, and what is correct at twenty-seven, will sober the condition. Perhaps your teacher knows with whom to consult, and please remember that next year you will be twenty-eight, and the proper time to decide is later. Study the right repertoire for your voice and do not try to be a singer alone. My ranges is about two octaves, and I want to be a confident singer if I could like to. — E. T.

A. There is, and there always will be room enough on the concert stage and in the opera for a singer with a voice like the Wagner, Tibbett, Thorner, and similar types. Many singers have chosen the middle, or lighter works. Now your type will be best served by careful training. Your voice is not small in size. The secret of the much-discussed Mat-

been more misunderstood—is simply to
keep the playing more free from
tensions. Matthay always said his was no

Matthay also used this splendid concep-

much forte playing runs the risk of
sounding percussive and harsh is that
the playing arm tenses itself to bring
force upon the keys and then remains
tense. Matthay taught me never to allow
the slightest tension to creep into my
playing. Where forte and even fortis-

get my arms, wrists, and hands down to
free, and then release my fingers in a
weight straight from the shoulder-section,
very quickly. Loudness of tone is gen-
erated by the moisture of weight rather
than speed, and that is essential, and the
speed that is released. Then, after the note has been struck,

the playing members must immediately
be relaxed (or "freed) of the weight
which went into the tone. In this way,
tension passes and can be played with-
out the least stiffness or tension to the
player, and without the least harshness
or erratic release.

A distinction should be clearly understood between Method, in the sense in which the word has just been used, and "teaching methods", which many teachers, as a whole to assist in a rapid system. The Finger Stroke In Piano Playing

(Continued from Page 285)

so hard to undo, are avoided. Having learned to start a key, the next thing to do is to get more tone, by swinging the finger and the key gently and freely from the top of the key to the bottom. If there is no hesitation in the down swing, you will get a soft tone. If there is hesitation, there will be tone because the hammer has not received enough impetus to reach the string. To let the key rise, simply relax the finger. If the key will lift the finger to the key surface. Gentle down and up finger swings should be practiced a few times slowly and softly, until the feel of a smooth swing is established. Avoid hurried and aimless repetitions.

If you want more tone, simply swing the finger and the key a little faster, but still smoothly and unhurried. When you reach key bottom, do not dig in. You will not get any more tone by doing this. Just use quick, light touch to hold the key down. It is surprising how little effort is necessary to attain increasing amounts of tone, when you apply the effort judiciously. Less than two ounces of force will succeed to start a key. With practice you will get the key into position very easily. You can feel the light touch between finger and key, so that the key seems to take on a personality of its own. If you think of two hard and heavy keys it fights back, and the tone is sharp. The key should always be coaxed into motion. The resulting tone will then be beautiful. For orderly procedure in working the fingers we may use the following chart:

1-2, 1-3, 1-4, 1-5
2-1, 2-3, 2-4, 2-5
3-1, 3-2, 3-4, 3-5
4-1, 4-2, 4-3, 4-5
5-1, 5-2, 5-3, 5-4

The first of the two numbers in any combination represents the finger on which you rest; the second number is the finger number which plays. For example, in the combination 2-4, you rest on the second finger, and play the fourth finger a number of times as described above. The combinations may be practiced slowly with other keys.

After we have the "feel" of these close finger strokes, we may try the higher strokes. At first, raise the playing finger about a half inch above the key surface. From this slightly elevated position, let the finger swing down gently, just to start the key; but not yet trying for tone. Practice a few key starts until the finger settles into an easy swing. Do not jab at the key. Check up on the other fingers so that they remain quiet and curved. Now try for a soft tone, by swinging easily through the air, and onto the key in one smooth motion to the key bottom. For more tone, increase the speed of the swing smoothly. If the finger stroke is easy and smooth, the key will not wiggle out or in.

Now you may gradually raise the fingers to a higher position over the key, but always let the fingers fall easily through the air and dock. Do not jab at the key or strike the key surface even if you want a strong tone. Approach the key comfortably and increase the speed of your finger swing as you go through the key, stopping only when you reach the key bed.

Much controversy has raged over the height of the finger stroke. The inquisitive student has been bewildered by the claims of the rival theories. He has seen great pianists play with highly raised fingers and he has read articles in which they strongly proclaim the virtues of their method. He has also seen other great pianists play with close fingers, and has read their equally strong defenses of their method; or he may have been stalwart with a teacher who believes only in a high stroke. This then are those who teach a high stroke for slow practice, and a low stroke for fast playing. These different types of stroke have their good and bad points. Let us examine them. The structure of the fingers should be considered first. Players with short, stubby, fleshy fingers, and with little freedom of motion in the joints simply cannot play with high fingers without straining their muscles. Such players should and do play with close fingers. You have seen them scamper over the keys with free finger action, though the range of the stroke is low. Players with normal fingers and free joints may use the higher stroke. Each player should find the finger height most comfortable for him. There is no danger in raising the fingers, so long as there is no strain in the fingers, hands, and arm. Some players do not feel that they can have control of finger articulation unless there is an appreciable range to the finger swing.

Those who favor close finger action claim there is no lost motion in moving the key, and that there is greater accuracy in finding the keys, and a surer command over tone control. They criticize the high finger stroke because it may cause strain in the flexor muscles. Furthermore, in the hand that uses the close stroke the higher tone and there is tendency to draw in that high stroke are inclined to draw the finger too hard towards and through the key, thereby causing further strain to the muscles, and resulting in a harsh tone. The hard-driven finger is also accompanied by a sudden lift of the peculiar wrist, giving us that jaunty and jerky finger action which looks bad, and sounds bad.

Those who favor the high finger action claim that the finger can be held up comfortably, that it can swing through the key, and be lifted smoothly, and without strain. They claim that high finger action develops the playing and lifting muscles, and thus insures greater clarity and positiveness in finger action. To such players the low finger action seems confusing and conducive to muddled playing.

Let us not be dogmatic about these matters. We can take the good points and discard the bad in both types of stroke. In my own teaching experience I have found that best results are obtained by starting with a close stroke, and then gradually raising the stroke, watching for freedom, smoothness and no strain of the technique. In all types of finger strokes we must keep three things in mind, (1) The finger should be held up easily, no matter at what level. (2) The finger should swing freely through the key, no matter what amount of tone is desired. (3) The stroke should stop as soon as the tone is heard.

For Mother's Day
by Plowden Kerman

When Mother sings, her hands flash rings;
She weaves a song, so gay, so long,
We love her better than before,
And cry, "Oh, mother! Sing some more."

And then we hear her voice ring clear;
And as she plays, she smiles, she sways,
An Angel in soft, bird-like wings
Is Mother, when she sits and sings.

Style Show
Piano Recitalogue
by Leonora Sill Ashton

Scene: Interior with piano.
Piano Pupils: Arthur, Ethel, Jean, Ralph, Jack, Meg, Laura, who play solos or duets. (Other pupils may be added.) Hugh, master of ceremonies.

Hugh (steps to front of stage): When we speak of a figure or a sonata, everyone who has studied music knows from their names in what form these compositions were written. But there are other forms of composition which receive their titles from the style in which they are written. Today, we are going to have "a musical style show," so that you may hear some of those other types of compositions. These types are going to play for you today, and to various dance forms. They will play first, and tell you about some dances.

First: Some of the oldest styles of music are heard in dances. Among the earliest of these is the Allemande, a cheerful dance which comes from Germany. Another is the Sarabande, a slow and stately dance from Spain; the Gigue, a lively dance of uncertain origin; the Gavotte and the Bourrée, probably of French origin, are similar in character, the Gavotte beginning on the third beat in four-four time, while the Bourrée usually begins on the first beat. To show you the style of the Gavotte I will play one. (seats herself at piano and plays.)

Arthur: How will you tell us about an Italian dance?

Hugh: The Italian composers created many beautiful musical styles, both in instrumental music and in song. One of the Italian dance styles is called the Tarantella, a lively, fast dance in six-eight time. The old legend about it is that its rapid performance will cure people who were bitten by the tarantula, a very large spider. I will play a Tarantella by (name cut off).

Jean: (rises) Now some of the dance styles of other countries we will hear. Among the Spanish dances are the Seguidilla (se-gee-dee-ya) and the Bolero. The Polonaise is a Polish dance, stately in character, and known as a processional dance. (Continued on next page)

Symphony of the Pines
(Prize Winner in Class B. Special Poetry Contest.)

Three Pines, with melancholy sing
Your wings in minor key;
Your voice is in a restless wind
Which sighs through needles drear,
And lifts your music to the sky
With leaves that rustle in the year.

The Instruction Book Beethoven Never Wrote

A few days before Beethoven's death the whole of his life- long music interests were in his mind. He had composed so much and had many schemes for future work which he was never able to write. Once during his life he said to a friend, "There are many ways of teaching composition." He also said that he would like to write an instruction book that would tell the secrets of musical form and make the art clearer to the beginner. Beethoven's instruction book would have been helpful to many piano students who have enjoyed it.
